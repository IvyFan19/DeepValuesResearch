# **Latest Quarter Earnings (Q4 FY2025)**

Super Micro Computer (SMCI) reported **fourth quarter FY2025** (quarter ended June 30, 2025) results that showed strong year-over-year revenue growth but pressured margins. **Net sales** were $5.8 billion, up 7.5% from $5.4 billion in Q4 FY2024. This growth was fueled by continued demand for SMCI's AI-focused server solutions, as revenue for full-year FY2025 reached $22.0 billion (47% annual growth). However, **gross margin** in Q4 was **9.5%**, slightly below the 10.2% a year prior. Higher component and tariff costs weighed on profitability, and **operating expenses** surged (\~22.6% YoY) due to increased headcount and salaries. Consequently, **net income** was $195 million with GAAP **EPS** of $0.31 (down from $0.46 EPS in Q4 FY2024). On a non-GAAP basis, EPS was $0.41, also lower year-over-year. Management attributed the profit decline to **tariff costs and a major customer's last-minute specification changes**, which delayed some revenue recognition and raised costs. They noted that some large customers delayed orders in the quarter due to economic uncertainty, but those deals are expected to close in coming quarters.

Despite the margin pressures, SMCI generated **operating cash flow** of $864 million in Q4 (helped by working capital timing) and ended FY2025 with a strong **cash balance** of $5.2 billion, providing liquidity to fund growth. **Total debt** was about $4.8 billion, including convertible notes. For **FY2025**, SMCI achieved record GAAP earnings of $1.0 billion (EPS $1.68), though this was slightly below FY2024's $1.2 billion profit due to margin compression.

**Outlook:** The company issued **weaker-than-expected guidance** for the next quarter (Q1 FY2026), projecting $6.0-$7.0 billion revenue and GAAP EPS $0.30-$0.42. This EPS outlook was below analyst consensus (\~$0.60) and reflects continued cost headwinds. The subdued guidance and margin concerns led to a sharp drop in SMCI's stock (it fell \~20% after earnings). CEO Charles Liang emphasized that some short-term impacts (tariffs, customer delays) hurt results but **remains confident** in long-term growth, citing strong demand for AI server solutions. Indeed, SMCI raised its **FY2026 revenue target** to at least $33 billion, implying \~50% growth, as delayed projects are expected to materialize. Overall, the latest quarter showed robust top-line growth in the AI server market but also highlighted temporary margin and execution challenges that management is working to address.

# **Background Research**

## **1. Business Model and Revenue Streams**

**Company Overview:** Super Micro Computer, Inc. (Supermicro or SMCI) is a leading provider of **server and storage solutions** optimized for enterprise, cloud, and AI applications. The company's business model centers on designing and manufacturing high-performance, energy-efficient **server systems**, as well as related components, using a modular "Building Block" architecture. Supermicro sells these hardware solutions primarily to data centers, cloud service providers, enterprises, and resellers/integrators. Its product portfolio ranges from complete server and storage systems (rackmount servers, blade systems, multi-node systems, GPU servers, etc.) to subsystems (motherboards, chassis, power supplies) and accessories. Supermicro also provides **software management tools and support services**, although hardware sales make up the bulk of revenue.

**Main Sources of Income:** The **major revenue segments** are: (1) **Server and Storage Systems** - complete server solutions which include assembled systems plus any associated software/services, and (2) **Subsystems and Accessories** - individual components and peripheral products. Over the past decade, Supermicro has increasingly focused on selling full systems rather than just parts. By FY2023, approximately **92%** of net sales came from complete server and storage system solutions, while about **8%** came from subsystems and accessories . This revenue mix has shifted toward systems - for comparison, in FY2020 systems were \~78.5% of sales. Selling full solutions (which bundle components with higher value-add assembly, software, and support) has driven growth and higher absolute gross profit, whereas standalone component sales have become a smaller portion of the business. Geographically, SMCI sells worldwide (100+ countries), but revenue is not highly segmented by region in reporting beyond noting large markets like the U.S., Asia, and Europe.

**Customer Base and Units Sold:** Supermicro has a broad and diversified customer base. In FY2022, it sold to **over 1,000 direct customers in 100+ countries**, spanning enterprise IT, cloud and telecom providers, OEMs, and others. No single customer accounts for more than 10% of sales, indicating revenue is not overly concentrated. Beyond direct sales, Supermicro also sells indirectly through distributors and value-added resellers, reaching many end-users globally. The company does not disclose the exact **number of servers or units** sold annually, but it measures volume in terms of "compute nodes" shipped (each node is a server unit). This volume has been growing significantly alongside the AI and cloud boom - for example, in FY2022, the number of compute nodes sold rose \~23% year-on-year. As of 2025, Supermicro's manufacturing capacity is substantial - the company can produce about **5,000 complete server racks per month**, including over 2,000 liquid-cooled racks, to fulfill large-scale orders . This implies tens of thousands of server units (nodes) are delivered each month, since each rack contains multiple server nodes. The emphasis on complete rack-scale solutions (branded "Datacenter Building Block Solutions") for AI clusters has led to higher average deal sizes. In summary, SMCI makes money primarily by selling integrated server systems at scale to a wide range of IT infrastructure buyers, with the breadth of its product line allowing it to serve many use cases (cloud, AI, edge, storage, etc.).

## **2. Short-term and Long-term Headwinds & Risks**

**Short-term / Temporary Headwinds:** In the near term, Supermicro faces several economic and industry factors that could **temporarily impact** its business performance:

-   **Trade Tariffs and Supply Chain Costs:** Ongoing tariffs (particularly U.S.-China tariffs) on imported components have increased SMCI's cost of goods, pressuring margins in the short run. In the latest quarter, management explicitly cited higher tariff-related costs as a reason for earnings misses. These tariffs, along with pandemic-era supply chain disruptions, have raised component prices and logistics costs. Although SMCI has mitigated some impact by expanding manufacturing outside China (e.g. in Taiwan and the U.S.), tariffs remain a headwind until trade policies stabilize.

-   **Customer Order Timing and Macroeconomic Uncertainty:** Supermicro's sales can be **lumpy**, especially as it wins larger customers. In Q3-Q4 FY2025, some big buyers delayed orders due to economic uncertainty and evolving tech needs. Management noted that a major customer required last-minute design changes, delaying revenue recognition to future quarters. Moreover, in a cautious economy with tight IT budgets, certain enterprise projects may be postponed in the short run. These timing issues are temporary - indeed, SMCI expects many deferred orders to be booked in coming quarters - but they can cause quarter-to-quarter volatility.

-   **Ramping Production Constraints:** The explosive demand for AI servers in 2023 outpaced SMCI's ability to **scale production immediately**. The company acknowledged "capital constraints" limited how fast it could ramp up manufacturing in the June 2025 quarter. Essentially, orders came so fast that short-term capacity (and working capital to procure parts) was strained. Supermicro is investing to increase output (even building a new factory in 2025), so this should ease in a few quarters. In the interim, however, inability to fulfill all demand instantly is a transient headwind (a "high-class problem," but a challenge nonetheless).

-   **Regulatory and Legal Overhang:** In August 2024, a short-seller's allegations led to regulatory scrutiny - SMCI received DOJ and SEC subpoenas and became the target of securities litigation related to its financial reporting delays. While management strongly denies wrongdoing and has since caught up on filings (with no restatements), the investigations create some short-term uncertainty and legal expense. These sorts of issues can distract management and weigh on stock sentiment temporarily, even if ultimately resolved.

-   **Global Economic Conditions:** Broader macro factors like high interest rates or currency fluctuations can soften IT spending in the short run. If global growth slows or if a strong USD reduces overseas revenue when reported in dollars, SMCI could see a temporary dip in growth. For instance, economic slowdowns in key markets (US, Europe, China) might delay data center expansions in the near term.

**Long-term / Structural Risks:** There are also fundamental factors that could **permanently impact** SMCI's business or industry position:

-   **Intensifying Competition and Margin Pressure:** The server market is competitive, with major OEMs (Dell, HPE, Lenovo) and original design manufacturers (ODMs) all vying for cloud and enterprise deals. As SMCI targets *larger customers and opportunities, its customer base may become more concentrated and its margins lower*, since big cloud clients demand volume discounts. Competition could lead to pricing pressure that permanently narrows SMCI's gross margins. Already, average selling prices may decline if customers don't need the "latest generation" product or if rivals offer lower-cost alternatives. The risk is that servers become more commoditized over time, eroding SMCI's differentiation on cost/performance and forcing it into price wars.

-   **Technology Transitions and Innovation Pace:** The IT industry evolves rapidly. A permanent risk is failing to keep up with major technology shifts. For example, if *hyperscale cloud providers decide to design more of their own servers in-house* (as Google, Amazon have done to some extent), it could cut into SMCI's addressable market. Similarly, new computing paradigms (like cloud hyperconvergence, specialized AI chips, or even quantum computing in the far future) could reduce demand for traditional server architectures. SMCI must continually invest in R&D to support new CPUs, GPUs (NVIDIA, etc.), and cooling technologies. If it falls behind - e.g., missing a generation of chips or not supporting a new standard - customers might shift to competitors. Thus, long-term success hinges on *continuous innovation*, and this is a risk if innovation stalls.

-   **Geopolitical and Supply Chain Risks:** Geopolitics pose a structural risk given SMCI's global footprint. The company relies on manufacturing in the U.S., Taiwan, and to some extent China. **Tensions involving Taiwan or broader U.S.-China relations** could permanently disrupt its supply chain or raise costs. For instance, an escalation in trade wars, sanctions on critical components, or restrictions on Chinese customers could materially impact SMCI . The company noted that increasing tariffs or export controls on high-end chips (like recent U.S. curbs on exporting certain GPUs to China) could affect demand and component availability. While SMCI is mitigating by diversifying production (opening a new campus in Malaysia was mentioned by analysts) and sourcing, geopolitical instability remains a long-run risk to operations.

-   **Reliance on Key Suppliers and Components:** Supermicro's servers incorporate CPUs (Intel, AMD), GPUs (NVIDIA), memory, etc. A permanent industry factor is that these component markets have *cycles and consolidation*. If key suppliers face shortages (like the semiconductor shortages in 2021-2022) or significantly increase prices, SMCI's sales and margins could suffer. The company has noted that shortages of any *key component* (CPUs, memory, drives, etc.) can delay its product deliveries. Additionally, as technology progresses, if a component supplier like NVIDIA gains pricing power (due to AI chip dominance), SMCI's cost of goods could structurally rise, squeezing profit unless passed to customers. In short, component supply and pricing are long-run variables largely outside SMCI's control.

-   **Corporate Governance and Financial Controls:** A risk highlighted by past events is internal controls and governance. In 2018, SMCI had a major accounting issue (related to revenue recognition) that caused a prolonged financial restatement and even a temporary stock delisting, eventually resulting in a $17.5 million SEC fine in 2020. Again in 2024, filing delays raised questions (though no misstatements were found). If such issues recur, they could permanently damage investor trust and increase the cost of capital. Management has taken steps - hiring a new CFO in 2021 and adding independent directors in 2024 - to strengthen oversight. Nonetheless, any future lapse in financial reporting or governance could have a lasting impact on the company's valuation and credibility.

In summary, Supermicro's long-run growth opportunities (AI, edge computing, etc.) are significant, but so are the structural risks. It must navigate a **dynamic competitive landscape** and geopolitical environment while executing perfectly on technology adoption. Many of these factors - intense competition, tech shifts, trade tensions - could *permanently alter the industry's profit pool*. SMCI's ability to adapt will determine whether these risks are mitigated or realized over the long term.

## **3. Cost Structure: Fixed vs Variable Costs**

Supermicro's cost structure includes a large proportion of variable costs tied to manufacturing its hardware, as well as relatively smaller fixed costs for R&D, sales, and administration. Understanding these costs provides insight into the company's operating leverage and how costs behave in different conditions:

-   **Cost of Goods Sold (Variable Costs):** The **largest cost category** for SMCI is its cost of sales, which consists primarily of the **components and materials** used in building servers (CPUs, GPUs, memory, motherboards, etc.), along with manufacturing labor, contract assembly costs, and freight/logistics. These costs are largely **variable** with production volume. When SMCI sells more servers, it must buy more chips and parts - so COGS rises proportionally. Historically, SMCI's **gross margin** has been in the mid-teens as a percentage of revenue, meaning cost of sales is \~85% or more of revenue. For instance, in FY2022 gross margin was 15.4%, so COGS was 84.6% of revenue. In recent quarters gross margins have dipped below 10%, implying COGS near 90%. Thus, the majority of each sales dollar goes to pay for the hardware components. Notably, component cost swings (due to supply shortages or tariffs) directly impact profitability - e.g., in FY2021, SMCI's gross margin fell because component costs rose faster than selling prices . The company has indicated these higher costs (memory, GPUs, tariffs, etc.) can persist during industry upcycles, compressing margins . Variable costs also include assembly labor and warranty expenses. Overall, *hardware component procurement is Supermicro's most significant cost*, and it scales with revenue.

-   **Operating Expenses (Fixed/Semi-fixed Costs):** SMCI's **operating expenses** are much smaller as a percentage of revenue (single digits) and include **R&D, Sales & Marketing, and G&A (general & administrative)** costs. These are relatively fixed (or at least do not vary directly with each unit sold, especially in the short term). In FY2023, total operating expenses were \~$521.9 million, which was only about **7.3% of revenue** - down from 8.9% in 2022 and 11.5% in 2021. This decline in percentage reflects *operating leverage*: as revenue grew, opex grew more slowly, improving efficiency. Breaking down opex:

    -   **Research & Development:** R&D was **4-6% of sales** in recent years (5.2% in 2022; 4.3% in 2023). These expenses (engineers' salaries, prototype costs, etc.) are relatively fixed - SMCI invests steadily in designing new server boards, systems, and cooling technology. R&D grew in absolute terms but has been diluted by rapid revenue growth, indicating good scale.

    -   **Sales & Marketing:** S&M has historically been **1-2% of revenue** (1.7% in 2022; 1.6% in 2023). SMCI does minimal mass-market advertising; marketing costs include sales staff, partner commissions, trade shows, etc. The low ratio shows an efficient go-to-market model (leveraging distributors and a focused sales team). These costs are semi-fixed - they might rise when entering new markets or launching campaigns, but not with each unit sold.

    -   **General & Administrative:** G&A (overheads like management, finance, facilities) was **\~2-4%** historically (2.0% in 2022; down to 1.4% in 2023). This has also scaled well, with G&A growing only modestly even as the business more than doubled, implying productivity improvements. G&A is largely fixed in the short term (you need a certain corporate infrastructure regardless of quarterly sales fluctuations).

-   In sum, SMCI's operating cost base is relatively fixed, and most opex line items have *declined as a % of revenue*during growth spurts. For example, from FY2021 to FY2023, revenue grew \~2×, but R&D and SG&A grew much slower, causing opex% to drop from 13.2% to 7.3%. This operating leverage boosts profitability in high-growth periods.

-   **Labor Expenses:** Labor costs are embedded across both COGS and opex. Manufacturing labor (in assembly facilities) is part of COGS, while engineering and corporate staff are in opex. SMCI has over **4,000 employees** (as of mid-2020s) primarily in Silicon Valley (San Jose HQ) and engineering centers overseas. In the short term, labor costs act like fixed costs - e.g., SMCI did significant hiring in 2022-2023 to support growth, which raised the expense base. The CFO noted a **jump in compensation and headcount costs (22.6% YoY)** in the latest quarter, which weighed on earnings. However, as revenue grows, the existing workforce can handle more volume, making it semi-fixed until a new hiring phase is needed. If business were to slow, labor is relatively inflexible in the immediate term (you can't easily trim R&D teams without hurting future products), so high labor costs could temporarily hurt margins - but SMCI's track record shows they keep labor lean relative to sales.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** As noted, marketing spend is minimal (\~1-2% of revenue). Supermicro's primary marketing is targeted: technical webinars, industry events, and joint promotion with channel partners. It's not a consumer-facing company, so it doesn't incur large advertising campaigns. Thus, cuts or increases here have only a small effect on the total cost structure. For example, even if marketing spend increased by 50%, that might raise opex by only 0.5% of revenue - not very material. The low marketing spend also indicates SMCI relies on product performance and partner networks to drive sales rather than heavy advertising.

**Significance and Trends of Costs:** The **most significant costs by far are the variable component costs** (COGS). A fluctuation in component prices or tariffs can have a larger dollar impact than, say, a 10% change in R&D budget. For instance, during 2021-2022, global supply shortages drove up materials costs, which **compressed gross margins** - SMCI's gross margin dropped to \~15.0% in FY2021 and 15.4% in FY2022, whereas it had been \~15.8% in FY2020. The company noted that logistic cost increases and component price inflation were offsetting its sales price increases. Meanwhile, operating costs were fairly stable; the company could not cut them much in the short term to offset gross margin pressure, illustrating how fixed costs can hurt profits when revenue surprises on the downside. In a downturn, SMCI's fixed costs (factories, R&D teams) remain, so **operating margins would shrink** if revenue falls unexpectedly (the company has to carry those fixed expenses despite lower sales) .

Conversely, in strong growth periods (like 2022-2023), Supermicro's largely fixed opex becomes a smaller percentage of sales, significantly boosting operating margins. In FY2023, operating income margin jumped to \~10.5% (from \~6.5% in FY2022) as opex efficiency improved. This demonstrates high operating leverage: once the heavy variable COGS is covered, additional sales revenue flows through at a high incremental margin due to low marginal opex.

**Cost Behavior in Different Economic Conditions:** In **economic expansions**, SMCI benefits from scale - revenues grow, component costs per unit may even fall (volume discounts), and fixed costs are spread over more units, yielding higher margins. For example, during the recent AI-driven boom, SMCI's revenue more than doubled from 2022 to 2024, and although gross margin % dipped (due to supply cost issues), the sheer revenue volume made absolute gross profit soar, and opex% dropped, leading to much higher net income. In **economic downturns or industry slowdowns**, however, SMCI's cost structure can strain profitability: revenue may stagnate or drop (as in 2019-2020 when cloud spending paused), and while component costs might also ease, the company could be left with excess inventory or factory under-utilization. Fixed costs like R&D and facilities won't drop commensurately, so margins tighten. For instance, in FY2017-2018, Supermicro's sales and gross profit were flat-to-down, but it still had to maintain R&D spending; combined with an accounting-related pause in shipments in 2018, this led to lower net income and even a quarterly net loss. The **key point** is that SMCI's high variable COGS keeps gross margins low, and its fixed opex, while well-controlled, means it relies on volume growth to drive profits. The cost structure thus amplifies the impact of revenue swings: good times see strong profit expansion, whereas lean times can quickly erode profitability if the company can't adjust costs fast enough .

In summary, Supermicro's cost base is dominated by variable hardware costs (tied to unit volumes and component prices), with fixed costs (especially R&D) providing operating leverage. The company's profitability is most sensitive to managing component costs (through supply chain efficiencies and pricing strategy) and keeping fixed costs in check relative to growth. Historically, SMCI has demonstrated discipline in opex and benefitted from economies of scale, but external factors like tariffs and supply swings have at times caused temporary cost headwinds that compress margins.

## **4. Management Team and Governance**

**Executive Team:** Supermicro is led by its **founder and long-time CEO, Charles Liang**, who also serves as President and Chairman of the Board. Liang founded the company in 1993 and has been the chief architect of its growth for over three decades. He has a hands-on engineering background (holder of multiple server technology patents) and is deeply involved in product direction. Under his leadership, SMCI grew from a small motherboard maker to a multi-billion-dollar server solutions provider. Liang is supported by **David Weigand, Chief Financial Officer (CFO)**, who joined in 2018 as Chief Compliance Officer and became CFO in Feb 2021. Weigand brought experience from Hewlett Packard Enterprise and has been instrumental in improving the company's financial reporting and internal controls after past accounting issues. Another key figure is **Yih-Shyan "Wally" Liaw, co-founder and Senior VP of Business Development**. Liaw co-founded SMCI with Liang in 1993 and initially led worldwide sales. He took a hiatus in 2018 but returned to the company in 2021-2022 to focus on strategic partnerships. Liaw's deep knowledge of the company and industry relationships is a valuable asset on the exec team.

Other notable executives include **Don Clegg (SVP of Worldwide Sales)**, who has been with SMCI since 2006 and has 35+ years of industry experience , and **George Kao (SVP of Operations)**, who oversees global manufacturing and joined in 2016 with decades of semiconductor operations experience. Overall, the management team blends company veterans (Liang, Liaw, Clegg) with some newer hires (Weigand) to bolster specific areas. Importantly, *Charles Liang remains the driving force* - as founder-CEO he sets the vision (for example, aggressively pursuing AI server leadership) and is deeply identified with the company's culture and strategy.

Now, with Weigand and new legal counsel (they hired a Chief Compliance Officer/General Counsel in 2025), the management team appears more robust.

One key aspect is **founder influence**: Charles Liang (and family) are significant shareholders (more on this in Value Alignment below), so there is a strong founder presence in both management and governance. There is no outside "professional CEO" - Liang's vision and long-term commitment anchor the company's strategy, which can be a positive (stability and deep industry knowledge) but also raises succession questions for the long term.

### **4.2 Management Track Record and Integrity**

**Strategy & Execution:** Under the current management, Supermicro has generally delivered on its growth goals, albeit with some hiccups. Charles Liang is known for setting ambitious targets - for example, a few years ago he stated a goal to reach $10 billion in revenue "in the near future", which skeptics doubted, but the company then proceeded to far exceed that (hitting $14.99 billion in FY2024 and $22 billion in FY2025). This demonstrates that management *follows through on growth promises*, capitalizing on industry opportunities (like AI/5G). Liang's strategic pivot to total solutions (full racks, liquid cooling) and rapid product launches (often being first to support new Intel/AMD chips) has kept Supermicro at the cutting edge. The management team also successfully navigated the challenging pandemic supply environment in 2020-2021, ultimately turning supply constraints into a huge revenue surge by being ready to fulfill pent-up demand.

However, there have been areas of concern in management's past performance regarding transparency and controls. **Financial Reporting Issues:** In 2015-2017, SMCI had repeated delays in filing financials, culminating in an accounting investigation (related to recognition of sales transactions at quarter-end). Management at the time assured investors it was minor, but it dragged on over a year, causing the stock to be temporarily delisted from NASDAQ. Eventually, in Aug 2020 the SEC fined Supermicro $17.5 million for prior period accounting violations. This indicated that *management did overlook or fail to disclose problems properly* in that period. The company brought in new finance leadership and by 2021 had remediated those issues. Fast forward to 2024, and again SMCI fell behind on filings (the FY2024 10-K was delayed, as were Q1 and Q2 FY2025 10-Qs). This raised investor anxiety - especially since a short-seller (Spruce Point) alleged aggressive revenue recognition and other governance red flags in Aug 2024. The board formed a special committee and did an internal review with outside counsel (overseen by new director Susie Giordano). By Feb 2025, *management completed all delayed filings and stated that no restatements were needed*, essentially vindicating their accounting (the delays were apparently due to workload and ensuring accuracy). They regained NASDAQ compliance. While it's good that no fraud was found, the fact that filings were late at all two times in a decade reflects on management's operational controls. It suggests that while visionary on technology, the team historically under-invested in accounting infrastructure - something they are actively fixing now (hiring compliance officers, expanding the finance team).

**Communication and Transparency:** On investor calls, CEO Liang tends to focus on technology and opportunities, sometimes to the point of being overly optimistic or opaque about challenges. For instance, in late 2024, management initially maintained very high forward guidance ($26-$30 billion FY2025 revenue outlook) despite internal delays - only to cut that forecast to $23.5-$25 billion a few months later when preliminary Q2 FY2025 results came in. This may indicate an overenthusiasm or reluctance to issue cautious guidance. Analysts have occasionally pressed management on gross margin pressures and got somewhat **rosy assurances rather than concrete answers**, leading to volatility when results don't match the upbeat tone. That said, Liang and Weigand do answer questions on earnings calls thoroughly regarding operations. There's no evidence they "hide" operational problems - for example, they openly discussed the impact of **customer delays and tariff costs** during the Q4 FY2025 call, taking accountability for the earnings miss. They also disclosed the DOJ/SEC subpoenas promptly in 2024. So, while execution issues occurred, current management appears committed to transparency in remediation.

**Management Integrity and Investor Trust:** The repeated financial reporting issues have caused some to question management integrity in the past. Notably, Spruce Point's short report in 2024 accused management of aggressive accounting and raised doubts about inventory valuations and customer financing. The company strongly refuted these claims, and so far nothing material has been proven - but the fact that federal subpoenas were issued shows the allegations were taken seriously. Management's stance is that these complaints are "without merit", and importantly, the independent review did not find fraud. The presence of investigations means some cloud of uncertainty lingered, but as of early 2025 SMCI has largely put it behind by complying with regulators. The board turnover (adding an audit expert, etc.) also helps restore trust.

**Tenure and Stability:** Many of SMCI's leaders have very long tenures - Liang (32 years), Liaw (with a brief break), Clegg (\~17 years). This stability can be positive (experienced team with deep company knowledge). It can also mean a strong founder-centric culture that might resist outside influence. That said, the board bringing in new directors and Weigand as CFO shows they are willing to augment skills when needed. The audit committee is now led by Robert Blair (appointed 2022), indicating increased independent oversight.

In terms of **shareholder relations**, aside from the accounting episodes, management has been friendly to shareholders through value creation (no dividend, but they did authorize share buybacks when they felt the stock was undervalued - see Section 6). Charles Liang often emphasizes *long-term targets and vision* in communications, which aligns with long-term shareholders' interests (for example, talking about setting "a strong foundation for $40 billion revenue in FY2026"). Some investors appreciate this visionary outlook; others worry it can be promotional if not backed by execution. So far, Liang has delivered remarkable growth, which builds credibility.

**Analyst Interactions:** On earnings calls, analysts occasionally probe on margins or guidance. Liang's responses can be somewhat evasive or focus on positives. For instance, when asked about gross margin decline, he'll pivot to talking about high customer demand and new products rather than directly conceding competitive pricing pressure. CFO Weigand is more straightforward on numbers. Overall, the tone is optimistic, and while they don't disclose every detail (like exact large customer names or order pipeline, often citing NDA reasons), nothing suggests deliberate misinformation. It's more a matter of emphasis - management highlights opportunities more than risks in public forums, which is common.

**Integrity Track Record:** There have not been allegations of personal misconduct or egregious ethical lapses by key executives. The concerns have been around financial controls (which they're addressing) and full transparency. With the measures put in place (additional independent directors, a new Chief Compliance Officer appointed April 2025), the trajectory is toward improved governance. It's worth noting that no executives were charged with fraud in prior accounting issues - it was reported as control failures, not intentional manipulation. This somewhat mitigates integrity concerns; it points to oversight issues rather than malfeasance.

In summary, management has a **strong operational track record** in growing the business and generally meeting (or exceeding) growth goals, but they've faced **challenges in administrative execution** (financial reporting) which they appear to be correcting. The founder-led culture imparts a clear strategic vision and continuity, and recent steps suggest a maturation in governance. ## **6. Share Buyback Program (Current and Historical)**

Supermicro has historically *not been a big repurchaser of its stock*, as the company focused on reinvesting in growth. However, in recent years - especially 2022-2023 - SMCI initiated share buybacks for the first time, taking advantage of what management perceived as undervaluation. Let's outline the current status and the past decade:

**Current Share Buyback Plan:** As of mid-2025, **Supermicro does not have an active share repurchase program in effect.** The most recent program, authorized in August 2022, **expired on January 31, 2024** . Under that program, the board had approved up to $200 million for buybacks, but it had a set duration (Aug 2022-Jan 2024). Management chose *not* to renew or extend a new repurchase plan after it expired, likely because the stock price had risen dramatically by early 2024 (making buybacks less compelling) and because the company was raising capital to fund growth (they did an equity offering in Mar 2024). Thus, as of the latest filings, there is no ongoing authorization to repurchase shares - any future buybacks would require a new board approval. In the conference calls around Aug 2025, management did not announce any new buyback despite a recent stock drop; instead, they seem to be prioritizing cash for operations and potential debt reduction.

**Comparison to Past 10 Years:** Prior to 2022, Supermicro rarely repurchased stock. Through the 2010s, the company was in expansion mode and also dealing with the 2018 accounting restatement, so excess cash was either conserved or used for working capital (and no dividends were paid either). The **first significant buyback** occurred in **FY2019**: in that year (calendar 2018-2019), the board authorized a small program - the details are sparse in annual reports, but we know by mid-2019 they had \~$20 million in stock repurchases (evident from treasury stock changes). It wasn't major and coincided with the stock being low after the accounting issue. This program likely expired in 2020. Indeed, the 10-K mentions a prior repurchase plan expired on July 31, 2022, implying one was in place before Aug 2022.

The **major buybacks** happened under the August 3, 2022 authorization:

-   It allowed repurchasing up to $200 million of common stock on the open market.

-   Management moved quickly: in FY2023 (specifically the quarter ending June 30, 2023), SMCI bought back **$150.0 million** worth of shares . This amounted to **15,533,500 shares** retired (post-split), which was roughly 2.9% of outstanding shares at the time. They paid an average price of about $9.65 (which corresponds to \~$96.5 pre-split). This was when the stock was relatively cheap - in late 2022, SMCI's split-adjusted price was under $10.

-   These repurchases were highlighted by management as a sign of confidence and a way to return value. The company stated it reflected their *"commitment to return value to our shareholders"*. Indeed, the timing was excellent - shortly after, SMCI's stock skyrocketed with the AI boom, so those repurchased shares saved shareholders from dilution and the company "bought low."

-   No shares were repurchased in the latter half of 2023 or the first half of 2024 under that program (they used $150M of $200M) . They allowed the remaining \~$50M authorization to expire unused on Jan 31, 2024 . Likely rationale: by early 2024 the stock was above $30 (split-adjusted), a \~3x from where they bought, and the company's focus shifted to raising capital for growth (they did an equity issuance in Mar 2024 rather than buying stock).

-   Therefore, **from 2015-2021, SMCI had negligible or no buybacks**; **in 2022-2023 it repurchased $150M worth**; and **in 2024-2025 it has repurchased $0** (instead issuing shares, net).

If we compare this to the past decade, the recent buyback is **the largest in SMCI's history**. Cumulatively over 10 years, SMCI maybe repurchased around $170-$180 million (including a small amount in 2019 and the big $150M in 2023). That's relatively modest (less than 2% of current market cap). For most of the decade, *share count actually rose* slowly due to stock-based compensation and one or two equity offerings. For instance, shares outstanding (split-adjusted) were \~470M in 2015 and about \~588M in 2024 - an increase largely from the 2024 share offering and some employee awards, offset partially by the 2023 buyback. So, the share count only dropped meaningfully in 2023 thanks to the buyback, and then increased in 2024 due to the new issuance.

**Capital Return Philosophy:** Management's capital allocation has shifted as the company matured. Historically, no dividends have been paid (and management states none are planned in the foreseeable future). This is typical for a growth company. Instead, they prioritize reinvestment, but they demonstrated in 2022 that if cash flow is strong and the stock looks undervalued, they are willing to do buybacks. Notably, right after the big buyback, they needed cash to support growth (inventory for huge AI orders), and they *paused buybacks and even raised equity*. This shows management is flexible and opportunistic: they will return cash when the company has excess and the stock is cheap, but they won't force buybacks at the expense of growth needs. Shareholders generally approve of this approach - the buyback was well-timed and the later share issuance was done at a much higher price, minimizing dilution.

In the **former 10 years**, had SMCI done more steady buybacks, the share count might be lower, but management likely felt the ROI on reinvestment was higher. Indeed, given the explosive growth opportunities they seized, that was probably true. As of 2025, with so much cash on hand ($5.2B), one might wonder if they'll restart buybacks. But management has guided that they foresee enormous demand and might use cash for strategic needs (inventory, maybe M&A for technology, etc.). Also, they did issue convertible debt ($1.7B of 2029 notes and $0.7B of new 2028 notes) - possibly they could consider using cash to *retire some debt* rather than buy back stock, as interest rates rise. At current share prices ($55-$60), the stock isn't as obviously cheap as it was at $10, so management might be less inclined to buy back aggressively now, focusing on deleveraging or keeping a war chest.

**Summary of Comparison:** Over the last 10 years, share repurchases were minimal until FY2023's significant buyback. The **current buyback plan is effectively none** (the previous one ended with some capacity unused). In contrast to some tech companies that repurchase stock regularly, SMCI's use of buybacks has been sporadic and strategic. The 2022-2023 program stands out - it reduced the float by roughly 3% just before the stock price multiplied, thereby delivering a strong benefit to continuing shareholders. In prior years, the effect of any repurchases was negligible on the float.

From a shareholder perspective, management's execution of buybacks has been favorable - they bought when valuations were low (P/E \~7-8 in late 2022) and did not chase the stock at high valuations. They effectively "returned" $150M to shareholders by repurchasing shares at \~$10 that later traded above $50. Over a decade, SMCI's main return to shareholders has come through stock price appreciation rather than dividends or routine buybacks, but the tactical repurchase in 2023 and the possibility of future buybacks (if excess cash accumulates beyond growth needs) indicate the company is open to returning capital when appropriate.
