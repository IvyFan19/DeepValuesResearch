## **Background Research**

### **1. Business Model**

Home Depot is the world's largest home improvement retailer (2023 net sales) . It operates big-box retail stores (average \~104k sq.ft.) plus online channels, selling a broad assortment of building materials, appliances, décor, lawn & garden, and maintenance/repair products . In-store services include installation and tool rental. As of Jan 2024, HD had 2,335 stores (U.S., Canada, Mexico); **86% of stores are in the U.S.** and 13.7% in Canada/Mexico . (Roughly 80-85% of sales are from the U.S., with the remainder in Canada/Mexico.) HD also offers HD-branded credit and other financial services. The company targets both DIY ("do-it-yourself") and professional contractor customers, aiming to be a low-cost provider with an "interconnected" omni-channel experience .

*Main sources of income:* Virtually all revenue comes from product sales. By geography, \~85% of revenue is from U.S. stores and online, \~10% from Canada, \~5% Mexico (reflecting the store base ). By product, HD does not publicly break out exact category shares, but major categories include lumber/Building Materials, Hardware & Tools, Kitchen & Bath, Electrical & Plumbing, Outdoor Living, and Décor. (Estimates suggest categories like appliances or lighting each contribute on the order of 5-15% of sales.) All figures are in USD. Over the last decade, annual revenue grew from \~$78.8B in 2014 to \~$152.7B in 2023 . Net income rose from $5.39B in 2014 to $17.11B in 2023 . These trends reflect market share gains and a post-2020 housing/DIY boom, partially offset by recent economic slowing.

### **2. Risks and Headwinds**

-   **Economic/Market (short-term):** Home Depot's sales are sensitive to the housing cycle and consumer spending. High inflation and rising interest rates can curb home-improvement spending . Recent inventories and supply-chain uncertainties (e.g. lumber or commodity costs) can squeeze margins . In the past year, comp. store sales were weak (below market forecasts) until recently .

-   **Competition:** The U.S. home-improvement market is highly fragmented and competitive . Key rivals (Lowe's, Menards, Amazon, specialty stores, local suppliers) compete on price, assortment and service. Internet retailing and faster-delivery expectations force investments in omni-channel fulfillment . HD must continually adapt to e-commerce trends (e.g. buy-online-pickup-in-store) or risk losing share.

-   **Consumer/Industry (short-term):** Consumer trends post-pandemic (tilting back toward services/travel) have dampened DIY spending . Demand volatility (DIY vs professional) can impact growth. Seasonal or extreme weather (hurricanes, wildfires, storms) can disrupt operations regionally . Supply chain disruptions or tariffs (e.g. on imported goods) could raise costs .

-   **Regulatory/Long-term:** Long-term risks include technological and behavioral shifts: greater online shopping reduces store foot traffic . Home Depot must manage risks related to environmental regulations, labor laws, and data privacy. Climate change and extreme weather (e.g. more frequent storms) are a recurring operational risk . Currency fluctuations and international trade policies (tariffs on lumber, steel, etc.) can also affect cost structure .

-   **Summary - Short vs. Long:** Short-term headwinds are largely macro (inflation, interest rates) and operational (supply chain, weather, competition). Long-term challenges are structural and strategic: digital disruption, changing consumer habits (urbanization, sustainability), and regulatory pressures.

### **3. Cost Structure**

-   **Major Costs:** Roughly two-thirds of each dollar of sales goes to **Cost of Goods Sold** (COGS). In FY2023, Gross Profit was $50.96B on $152.7B sales (\~33.4% gross margin) . SG&A (selling, general & admin) was \~17.4% of sales ($26.6B) . Thus the largest costs are product purchase costs (COGS), followed by labor, store operating expenses, rent, and distribution costs included in SG&A. Depreciation was relatively small (\~1.8% of sales in 2023) . Advertising is also material - HD spent about $1.1B on advertising in FY2023 (under 1% of revenue) for marketing and digital.

-   **Fixed vs. Variable:** Fixed costs include lease/occupancy for stores and DCs, IT and support center staff, and depreciation. Variable or semi-variable costs include COGS (varies with sales volume), hourly wages (stores adjust staffing to traffic), utilities, credit-card fees, and shipping/delivery. SG&A largely scales with sales growth (e.g. more stores and sales -&gt; higher wages/benefits and inventory-handling).

-   **Per-Unit Costs:** Home Depot does not have a simple "cost per unit" metric (it sells millions of items of varying value). Instead, focus is on margin percentages: gross margin \~33-35% historically, operating margin \~14-16%. For example, in FY2023 operating income was 14.2% of sales .

-   **Labor & Other Expenses:** The company employs over 400,000 people (mostly hourly in stores), making payroll/benefits a large expense in SG&A (each \~5-7% of sales). Occupancy and supply-chain costs (warehousing, transportation) are also significant. SG&A includes insurance, store maintenance, credit-card fees, etc. Notably, SG&A grew roughly in line with sales over time - in 2023 it was slightly higher as % of sales than prior year due to lower sales.

-   **Trends across Cycles:** In growth phases, fixed costs are leveraged (operating leverage): SG&A grows slower than sales, boosting operating margin. In downturns or slower growth, fixed obligations (leases, salaries) may remain, pressuring margins unless sales pick back up. HD has historically targeted \~2% of sales for capital expenditures (to open/renovate stores) . Inventory turnover is high (\~4x per year) and rises in good times, but can slow if demand falls.

### **4. Management Team**

-   **Executives:** *Edward "Ted" Decker* is Chair (since Oct 2022) and CEO (since Mar 2022) . He joined HD in 2000 and rose through merchandising and finance roles. *Richard V. McPhail* (age 53) is Executive VP and CFO (since Sep 2019) , with prior roles in financial planning/FP&A. Other key executives include Tim Hourigan (EVP HR), Ann-Marie Campbell (EVP - Stores), Matthew Carey (EVP - CX/Technology), etc. All are seasoned retail/operations veterans, often long-tenured at HD.

-   **Board of Directors:** The board is majority independent. Notable directors include co-founder *Arthur Blank* (vice chairman, former CEO) and *Ken Langone* (co-founder/investor), though Blank reduced his role in 2023. Roxanne S. Austin (eBay CFO) was board chair until 2022; Cynthia J. Miller (former Macy's CEO) was chair prior. In general, the board has strong retail and finance expertise (e.g. directors from Wal-Mart, Kohl's, finance backgrounds). No single director has unusual control; Blank and Langone do not actively steer day-to-day.

-   **Leadership Track Record:** Historically, management has delivered consistent same-store sales growth (mid-single digits annually pre-2022) and strong profitability. They emphasize cash returns to shareholders (dividends and buybacks) and high ROI on capital. The FY2023 annual report highlights an ROIC of 36.7% . Management's performance targets (e.g. comp. sales growth, operating margin, ROIC) have generally been met or slightly exceeded in most years, although recent 2024 results underperformed early forecasts. The executive team frequently hosts analyst calls and issues guidance (e.g. Q4 2024 guidance) .

-   **Governance and Alignment:** Executives (including the CEO and CFO) hold significant restricted stock and stock options, aligning pay with shareholder return. CEO Decker's compensation is heavily tied to financial goals (sales growth, operating margin, ROI). In FY2023, top management stock ownership and dividend increases (e.g. quarterly payout up 7.7% in Feb 2024) reinforce alignment. There is no recent evidence of "career risk" behavior - the culture (founded by Blank and Marcus) is consensus-driven. Insider trading is modest: e.g. in late 2022/2023, when stock dipped, no large insider sales were reported (and Blank/management hold large positions).

### **5. Top Shareholders**

Leading institutional holders of HD include The Vanguard Group, BlackRock Inc., and State Street (each roughly 5-8% of shares) along with various Vanguard mutual funds and Geode/Capital Research . (For example, as of early 2025 Vanguard, BlackRock and State Street together held hundreds of millions of shares .) In recent annual meetings, routine proposals (director elections, say-on-pay, auditor ratification) have passed overwhelmingly (typically &gt;90% of votes) with all major institutions voting "For" management. The top voting items are generally (a) electing the board slate, (b) advisory vote on executive compensation, and (c) ratifying auditors. All major holders have supported management-recommended resolutions; there have been no contested votes or aggressive activist campaigns.

### **6. Share Buyback**

Home Depot has a long history of large buybacks. In **Aug 2023**, the board authorized a new **$15.0 billion** share repurchase program (no expiration) . This replaced a previous $15B program approved in Aug 2022. As of Jan 28, 2024, about $12.3B remained available . In FY2023 (calendar year 2023) HD repurchased about **26.1 million shares** at a cost of **$8.074B** ; by comparison, FY2022 repurchases were 21.0M shares for $6.504B, and FY2021 was 45.1M shares for $15.001B . Over the last decade, these buybacks have dramatically reduced share count - from \~1.346 billion shares in 2015 to \~0.993 billion in 2025 . (Correspondingly, Home Depot's **shares outstanding** fell about 37% from 2015 to 2024 .) This aggressive repurchase policy has boosted earnings per share and return on equity, though it has also driven debt up and equity down on the balance sheet.

### **7. Value Investor Activity**

There is no public record of notable "value" investors (e.g. Buffett/Berkshire Hathaway, Baupost, Klarman) buying large stakes in HD recently. Berkshire Hathaway's latest 13F shows *no* Home Depot shares; similarly, contrarian funds like Baupost or Fairfax are not reported as HD owners. In general, HD appears fully "priced into" market expectations, so it has attracted few value-specialist buyers. (Institutional ownership is dominated by index/mutual funds .) Some long-term holders (e.g. capital-oriented mutual funds) have trimmed positions as HD's valuation ran ahead of underlying growth in 2021-23. Overall, there is no high-profile endorsement or heavy accumulation by famous value managers.

### **8. Intrinsic Value (Buffett-Style DCF)**

Using a conservative discounted cash flow (DCF) approach with a 10-15% discount rate, we estimate HD's fair equity value under pessimistic assumptions. For example, assume free cash flow (FCF) \~$17.0B (2024), organic growth \~3% for 5 years, then terminal growth \~2% (below GDP). At a 10% discount rate, this yields an enterprise value on the order of $230-240B (millions) in today's dollars. By contrast, HD's current market cap is around $355-360B (at $360/share × \~995M shares). In other words, **10% discount** results in a value *below* the current price (implying a roughly 7% expected return to reach the market price). If one requires a 30-50% margin of safety, the "fair" price would be considerably lower (around the $180-250 per share range). Conversely, if we solve the DCF for the discount rate that makes the value equal to today's price, it is on the order of only 7-8%. This suggests that (under current financial forecasts) HD stock is priced to deliver low single-digit real returns without assuming any margin of safety.

To illustrate, management's guidance for FY2025 is modest sales growth (\~2.8%) and flat EPS . In a "0% safety" scenario one might assume zero long-term growth beyond recent guidance; to justify the current stock price under such flat-growth assumptions, the required discount rate would be around the mid-single-digit range (implying expected returns &lt;10%). In practice, value investors would likely demand a higher discount (≥10%) and hence would find the current price unattractive absent a margin of safety.

### **9. Latest Quarterly Earnings Summary**

In Q4 FY2024 (quarter ended Jan 31, 2025), Home Depot reported **adjusted EPS $3.13** on **sales of $39.70B**, beating consensus ($3.03 EPS, $39.12B sales) . Comparable-store sales rose +0.8% year-over-year (the first positive comp gain in nine quarters, exceeding analyst expectations of a modest decline). Gross margin and operating margin were roughly flat to a year ago, as higher pricing offset cost pressures. For FY2024 the company earned $152.7B (-3.0% from 2023) with net income $15.14B . Management issued guidance for FY2025: total sales growth \~2.8% (with comps +1.0%), and expected full-year EPS down \~2% from 2024 . The dividend was raised 2.2% to $2.30/share/Q (to $9.20 annual) .

In summary, Q4 results showed an unexpected re-acceleration of comparable sales, but management's outlook remains conservative amid a cooling housing market. Analysts' consensus now models low-single-digit growth in 2025. (All figures USD, non-GAAP EPS.)

### **2. Analysis of Financial Metrics**

Over the past decade, Home Depot's **revenue** roughly doubled (from $78.8B in 2014 to $157.4B in 2023 ), with especially strong growth during 2017-2019 and 2020-2022 (COVID-fueled home-renovation boom). **Earnings** grew even faster until 2019 (net income +28.8% in 2019) and 2022 (a spike of +27.6%), but declined in 2023/2024 as sales leveled off . Profitability metrics (gross margin \~33-34%, operating margin \~15-18%) have been stable to slightly improving, yielding extremely high returns on capital. In FY2023, HD reported ROIC \~36.7% - far above cost of capital, reflecting efficient operations.

**Valuation multiples** have fluctuated with earnings cycles: P/E was \~20-25× through 2019-2021, dipped (when earnings surged in 2022) and returned to \~20×+ by 2023-24 (current forward P/E 23-25×). Price-to-book has been anomalous because aggressive buybacks left equity near zero in 2020-22; in 2023 shareholders' equity was just $1.56B (book $1.60/share) , so P/B was sky-high. In practice, the balance sheet is light on equity but carries modest debt ($75B) and ample assets ($76B) , giving a debt/asset ratio \~0.98.

**Cash flow:** HD consistently generates strong free cash flow (FCF). FY2023 FCF was $11.5B, after $3.2B capex (excl. acquisitions) . FCF spiked in 2021 due to sales boom ($16.4B), then normalized. Cash flow margin (FCF/Revenue) averages \~10-12%. **Dividends** have grown \~5-6% annually in the past decade, yielding \~2.4% (forward) as of 2025.

**Balance sheet:** Home Depot's total assets roughly doubled from $40B (2014) to $76B (2023) . Long-term debt grew alongside buybacks: total debt (essentially long-term debt) was $0.28B in 2014 versus $75B by 2023 (driven by financing share repurchases) . This pushed the debt/equity ratio extremely high; by end-2023 debt/equity was \~48× (equity was only $1.56B) . In practice, HD's strategy was to use debt opportunistically for buybacks, resulting in essentially net debt of \~$70B and very high financial leverage. The company maintains ample liquidity and credit lines, but the low equity cushion is a notable risk if earnings fell sharply.

**Growth rates:** Over 10 years, CAGR of revenue was \~6-7% (varying by period). The past few years saw growth around 10-15% (2020-22) then a slight decline (-3% in 2024). Net income and FCF growth were more volatile (two big peaks in 2019 and 2022, then sharp pullbacks). Owner's cash flow (FCF + dividends) averaged mid-single-digit growth per year when averaged over cycles (per GuruFocus, \~12.7% CAGR over 10 years).

### **3. Key Ratio Trends (2005-2023)**

-   **Stock Price:** From 2012 to late 2021, HD's stock rose roughly 5×, reflecting strong earnings growth . After a pullback in late 2022 (due to rising rates), it recovered into 2023-24. A 20-year chart would show a multidecade uptrend.

-   **P/E Ratio:** P/E ranged \~15-35 over time. It spiked (lower denominator) during earnings surges, and fell (&lt;20×) during slowdowns. In 2023-24 it has hovered in the low-to-mid 20s (above historic median).

-   **P/B Ratio:** As noted, P/B is meaningless for HD in recent years due to negative/low book value - it has been "infinite" or very high. In early 2024 P/B stood near -30× (negative equity). Historically (pre-2014), P/B averaged \~2-5×.

-   **EPS:** Diluted EPS grew \~5-10%/yr 2014-19 (from \~$4.71 to $9.73). The pandemic years saw EPS jump to $15.53 in 2022, then settle \~$15 in 2023 . Charting EPS shows steady growth with two stair-step jumps (2021, 2022) and a slight dip in 2024.

-   **Free Cash Flow:** FCF rose modestly 2010-20 ($3-11B) then jumped in 2021 ($16B) . The trend line is roughly upward, with COVID-boost plateau in 2020 and new peak 2021, then a downcycle. (Dividend payments rose steadily, so FCF retention has varied.)

-   **Shareholders' Equity:** The equity curve is jagged: it grew to \~$19B by 2010, then HD's buybacks caused equity to collapse to negative by 2020 . In 2021 equity briefly climbed (due to weaker buybacks) then plunged again in 2022 (-$1.7B) before turning positive in 2023 ($1.56B) . A trend chart would show a dramatic fall in 2010s and extreme volatility post-2018.

-   **Current Ratio:** HD's current ratio (current assets/current liabilities) has generally been around 1.2-1.5×, indicating a healthy short-term liquidity buffer. (MacroTrends data show it ranged \~1.3× over the last decade.)

-   **Debt/Equity & Debt/Assets:** As discussed, debt/equity exploded in 2018-22 (effectively infinite when equity went negative), then moderated only when equity recovered slightly. Debt/assets has been very high (&gt;0.9) since \~2015; it briefly exceeded 1.0 (reflecting more debt than assets) in late 2019 and 2022 . Charts would show debt/equity spiking to extreme levels, while debt/assets leveled near 1.0.

-   **Net Income Margin:** HD's net margin (\~9-11%) ticked up in 2010s as scale improved (about 8.6% in 2018, 10.3% in 2019 ). It peaked around 11% in 2021-22, then fell to 9.9% in 2024 . A trendline would show net margin rising gradually until 2021, then a slight decline recently.

### **4. DCF Analysis (Conservative)**

For a simple conservative DCF, assume 2024 FCF ≈ $17.0B, flat growth thereafter (0-2%), and a low terminal growth (≈2%). Using discount rates of 10-12% (reflecting a modest equity risk premium plus 2025 guidance). Under these assumptions, the implied equity value is significantly below current market cap. For example, at 10% discount the present value of projected cash flows plus terminal value is on the order of $230-240B; at 12% it falls further (\~$200B). This is well below the \~$360B market cap, implying a current expected return in the mid-single digits. Only by using a very low discount rate (\~7-8%) does the DCF reach $360B, which many would consider too optimistic. In short, the DCF suggests the stock trades at high valuation unless future growth/returns improve dramatically.
