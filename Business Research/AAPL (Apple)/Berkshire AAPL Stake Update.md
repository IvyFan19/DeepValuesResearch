# Analysis of Berkshire Hathaway's Q1 2025 Activity Regarding Apple Inc. Holdings

## I. Executive Summary

Berkshire Hathaway Inc. (Berkshire) executed a notable adjustment to its significant investment in Apple Inc. (AAPL) during the first quarter of 2025. This report confirms that Berkshire reduced its holdings in the technology giant by approximately 13%, continuing a pattern of trimming observed in the preceding quarters of 2024.<sup>1</sup> As of March 31, 2025, Berkshire's remaining stake comprised approximately 790 million shares, valued at $135.4 billion.<sup>1</sup>

This reduction occurred within a specific financial context for Berkshire: operating earnings declined 14.1% year-over-year to $9.641 billion, influenced partly by insurance underwriting weakness.<sup>13</sup> Concurrently, Berkshire's consolidated cash and short-term U.S. Treasury holdings swelled to an unprecedented $347.7 billion.<sup>15</sup> Furthermore, the company abstained entirely from share repurchases during the quarter, extending a pause initiated in the latter half of 2024.<sup>21</sup>

Despite the significant sale, commentary from Chairman and CEO Warren Buffett during the May 3, 2025, annual shareholder meeting provided critical context. He reassured investors that, barring a dramatic shift in capital allocation strategy, Apple would very likely remain Berkshire's largest single equity investment.<sup>1</sup> This suggests the Q1 reduction was not driven by a fundamental souring on Apple's business prospects.

Instead, the move appears to stem from a combination of factors. Portfolio management considerations, particularly managing the concentration risk associated with such a large holding, likely played a role.<sup>6</sup> Valuation sensitivity in a market perceived as potentially expensive, ongoing tax management strategies, and the desire to further bolster the already record cash position are also plausible contributors.<sup>2</sup> The decision reflects a broader theme of caution and selectivity evident in Berkshire's overall Q1 activities, rather than a specific negative verdict on Apple itself.

## II. Berkshire Hathaway's Q1 2025 Financial Landscape: Setting the Stage

Understanding Berkshire Hathaway's decision-making regarding its Apple investment requires examining the company's broader financial performance and position during the first quarter of 2025.

Operating and Net Performance:

Berkshire reported operating earnings of $9.641 billion for Q1 2025. This marked a 14.1% decrease compared to the $11.222 billion earned in Q1 2024.13 A primary factor in this decline was weaker performance in the insurance underwriting segment 13, which was impacted by approximately $860 million in after-tax catastrophe losses stemming from Southern California wildfires affecting policies written previously.15 Some analyst expectations for operating earnings were reportedly missed.15 This dip in core operational profitability underscores the significance of income generated from Berkshire's vast investment portfolio and cash reserves.

Net earnings attributable to Berkshire shareholders, calculated under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), experienced a much steeper decline. Q1 2025 net earnings were $4.603 billion, down approximately 64% from $12.702 billion in the prior-year quarter.<sup>13</sup> This volatility was largely driven by accounting rules requiring the inclusion of unrealized gains or losses on equity security investments. In Q1 2025, Berkshire recorded investment losses (primarily unrealized) of $5.038 billion, compared to investment gains of $1.480 billion in Q1 2024.<sup>13</sup> This highlights Warren Buffett's long-standing caution that GAAP net earnings can be misleading due to these non-cash investment value fluctuations, and his recommendation that investors focus on operating earnings for a clearer picture of the underlying business performance.<sup>13</sup>

Segment Contributions:

A closer look at the operating segments reveals a mixed picture:

-   **Insurance - Underwriting:** Earnings fell to $1.336 billion from $2.598 billion year-over-year, reflecting the catastrophe losses mentioned earlier.<sup>13</sup>

-   **Insurance - Investment Income:** Earnings increased to $2.893 billion from $2.598 billion in Q1 2024.<sup>13</sup> This increase demonstrates the positive impact of higher prevailing interest rates on the income generated from Berkshire's substantial insurance float (approximately $173 billion as of March 31, 2025 <sup>13</sup>) and its large cash and short-term Treasury holdings.

-   **BNSF Railway:** Earnings saw a modest increase to $1.214 billion from $1.143 billion.<sup>13</sup>

-   **Berkshire Hathaway Energy (BHE):** This segment showed significant strength, with earnings rising substantially to $1.097 billion from $717 million year-over-year.<sup>13</sup> This robust performance points to BHE becoming an increasingly crucial component of Berkshire's overall operating results, potentially reflecting successful capital deployment within the unit or favorable market dynamics in the energy sector. Berkshire took full ownership of BHE in 2024, and the segment aligns with the expertise of Greg Abel, Buffett's designated successor, whose background is in energy.<sup>4</sup>

-   **Manufacturing, Service, and Retailing:** This diverse group saw earnings dip slightly to $3.060 billion from $3.088 billion.<sup>13</sup>

-   **Other:** This category reported earnings of just $41 million, a sharp drop from $1.078 billion in Q1 2024. The decline was significantly impacted by foreign currency exchange losses of approximately $713 million in Q1 2025, compared to gains of $597 million in the prior-year period. However, this category also includes rising interest and dividend income from U.S. Treasury Bills and other investments not directly held by insurance or specific operating companies, which amounted to $869 million in Q1 2025 versus $303 million in Q1 2024.<sup>13</sup>

The rising income from investments, particularly Treasuries held outside the insurance operations, provides a meaningful buffer against volatility in other segments. The combined increase in reported investment income across the insurance and "Other" categories (totaling approximately $861 million year-over-year) helped partially offset the $1.26 billion decline in insurance underwriting profits during the quarter.<sup>13</sup>

Record Cash and Investment Activity:

A defining feature of Berkshire's Q1 2025 was its cash position. Holdings of cash, cash equivalents, and short-term U.S. Treasury bills reached a new record of $347.7 billion as of March 31, 2025.15 This was an increase from the already substantial $334.2 billion held at the end of 2024.2 This immense and growing liquidity became a major focal point for shareholders and analysts, generating significant interest income but also fueling questions about deployment strategies.2

Consistent with the cash build-up, Berkshire remained a net seller of equities for the tenth consecutive quarter.<sup>14</sup> During Q1 2025, the company purchased $3.18 billion worth of stocks while selling $4.68 billion, resulting in net sales of $1.5 billion.<sup>14</sup> Realized after-tax gains from these investment sales amounted to $2.4 billion in the quarter.<sup>13</sup> This pattern of net selling reinforces the narrative of caution and perceived lack of value in the market during the period.

Share Repurchase Hiatus:

Notably, Berkshire Hathaway conducted zero share repurchases during Q1 2025.21 This decision extended a pause that began in the third quarter of 2024, marking the first quarters without buybacks since the company revised its repurchase policy in mid-2018 to allow for more flexibility.25 The previous policy had tied repurchases to a strict price-to-book value multiple (1.2x) which was rarely met.26 The current policy allows buybacks when both Buffett and his late partner Charlie Munger (and presumably now Buffett alone or in consultation with Abel) believe the shares are trading below intrinsic value, provided a minimum cash level ($30 billion) is maintained.26

The absence of buybacks in Q1 2025, despite the enormous cash reserves, strongly implies that Berkshire's leadership did not view the company's own stock as significantly undervalued during the period.<sup>9</sup> This perspective likely reflects the stock's strong performance leading into and during the quarter <sup>2</sup> and potentially elevated valuation metrics, such as a price-to-book ratio near a 10-year high.<sup>26</sup>

Collectively, the record cash accumulation, continued net selling of equities, and the complete halt of share repurchases paint a clear picture of a pronounced defensive and cautious posture adopted by Berkshire Hathaway during the first quarter of 2025. This stance suggests a perception of limited attractive deployment opportunities in the prevailing market environment.

Analysis of Potential Drivers:

Several factors likely contributed to Berkshire's decision to further reduce its Apple stake in Q1 2025:

1.  **Portfolio Concentration Management:** Even after prior sales, Apple constituted a very large portion of Berkshire's publicly traded stock portfolio.<sup>5</sup> Reducing this concentration can be viewed as prudent risk management, mitigating the impact of potential adverse movements in a single stock.<sup>6</sup> The sale aligns with bringing the position size down from potentially over 50% of the equity portfolio towards a still very large, but less dominant, percentage.<sup>5</sup>

2.  **Valuation Considerations:** While Warren Buffett has lauded Apple as an exceptional business <sup>3</sup>, its stock valuation had reached levels significantly higher than when Berkshire initially invested.<sup>5</sup> The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio was noted as being elevated, particularly relative to Apple's more moderate recent growth trajectory.<sup>5</sup> Although not explicitly stated as the reason for the Q1 sale during the annual meeting, it's plausible that the prevailing valuation, even after an 11% price drop in the quarter <sup>1</sup>, was not deemed sufficiently attractive to warrant holding the entirety of the prior position, let alone adding to it.

3.  **Tax Strategy:** Warren Buffett had previously linked sales of Apple stock in 2024 to potential future increases in U.S. capital gains tax rates, suggesting a strategy of realizing some gains at potentially lower current rates.<sup>2</sup> While tax laws did not change leading into Q1 2025, this forward-looking perspective on managing significant embedded gains could have remained a contributing factor to the continued, methodical reduction.

4.  **Cash Generation for Future Opportunities:** Selling shares of a highly liquid and appreciated asset like Apple is an efficient way to increase Berkshire's already substantial cash reserves.<sup>6</sup> This bolsters the company's "dry powder," positioning it to potentially capitalize on future market downturns or pursue large-scale acquisitions should attractive opportunities arise.<sup>6</sup> The $2.4 billion in realized after-tax gains from overall investment sales in Q1 contributed to this cash build-up.<sup>13</sup>

The continuation of selling Apple shares even as the stock price declined during Q1 <sup>1</sup> suggests the move was part of a deliberate, strategic plan rather than opportunistic profit-taking at peak prices. This aligns with the idea that factors like concentration risk, tax planning, or a broader assessment of market value relative to alternatives (like holding cash) were primary drivers. Furthermore, the fact that Berkshire was a net seller of equities overall in Q1 <sup>14</sup> indicates the Apple trim was part of a wider strategy of reducing market exposure and building liquidity during the period.

Interestingly, Berkshire's selling activity contrasted sharply with Apple's own capital allocation strategy revealed shortly before the Berkshire meeting. Apple announced a massive $110 billion share repurchase authorization, the largest in its history, signaling strong confidence from its management in the stock's value.<sup>1</sup> This divergence highlights how even closely aligned entities - Apple's CEO Tim Cook attended the Berkshire meeting <sup>1</sup> - can arrive at different conclusions regarding valuation and the optimal use of capital at a given point in time.

## IV. Insights from the 2025 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Shareholder Meeting (May 3, 2025)

The annual shareholder meeting, held shortly after the Q1 earnings release, provided crucial qualitative context directly from Berkshire's leadership, particularly regarding the Apple investment and the company's overall strategy. Thousands of shareholders attended the event in Omaha, often dubbed "Woodstock for Capitalists," with many eager to hear Buffett's perspective on the economy, tariffs, and the company's direction.<sup>2</sup> The Q&A session featuring Warren Buffett, Vice Chairman Greg Abel, and Vice Chairman Ajit Jain was broadcast live by CNBC.<sup>10</sup>

Commentary on Apple:

Addressing the significant reduction in the Apple stake during Q1, Warren Buffett offered reassurance. He stated it is "extremely likely" that Apple will remain Berkshire's largest investment, provided no dramatic events occur that fundamentally alter the company's capital allocation approach.1 This commentary strongly suggests that the Q1 sale was not predicated on a diminished view of Apple's business quality or future prospects. While Buffett had cited tax considerations for sales in 2024 2, the specific rationale provided during the meeting for the Q1 2025 reduction centered on overall capital allocation decisions within the context of market alternatives and Berkshire's cash position.1 The clear implication is that the decision was driven more by portfolio mechanics and market context than by a negative reassessment of Apple itself.

Views on Cash, Market, and Economy:

The record $347.7 billion cash and Treasury bill position was a prominent topic.15 Buffett expressed comfort with this level, indicating it could potentially grow further, possibly exceeding $200 billion by the end of June 2025.1 He attributed the accumulation primarily to a lack of compelling large-scale investment opportunities available at attractive prices in the current market.15 This reinforces the idea that Berkshire's leadership perceived generally elevated valuations across potential investment targets.

Buffett also touched upon the broader economic climate, acknowledging a "challenging environment" influenced by factors such as U.S. trade tariffs and geopolitical uncertainty.<sup>10</sup> Tariffs were a frequent subject of shareholder questions submitted in advance.<sup>17</sup> While avoiding specific predictions, Berkshire's official filing noted the difficulty in reliably predicting the potential impact of these factors on its diverse businesses.<sup>16</sup> Despite global uncertainties, Buffett reaffirmed Berkshire's commitment to the United States, stating that any truly large future investment would "extremely likely" be domestic.<sup>1</sup>

Buybacks and Succession:

The Q1 halt in share repurchases was consistent with the overall theme of valuation sensitivity. While not discussed at length in the available meeting summaries, the action aligns with Buffett's stated philosophy of only repurchasing shares when they trade meaningfully below intrinsic value.37 Given Berkshire's strong stock performance and potentially high price-to-book multiple, the conditions for buybacks were likely not met in Q1.26

The meeting also served to underscore the established succession plan. Greg Abel, currently Vice Chairman overseeing non-insurance operations, is the designated successor to Warren Buffett as CEO.<sup>2</sup> Critically for investors concerned about post-Buffett investment strategy, it was reiterated that Abel would assume responsibility for Berkshire's capital allocation decisions, including major investments.<sup>29</sup> This public affirmation aims to provide reassurance about strategic continuity and centralized investment authority under the future CEO, though it leaves open questions regarding the precise future roles of portfolio managers Todd Combs and Ted Weschler.<sup>9</sup>

## V. Berkshire's Investment Strategy and Outlook

The events of Q1 2025 and the commentary from the annual meeting provide valuable indicators of Berkshire Hathaway's current investment strategy and outlook.

The Cash Conundrum and Market Perspective:

The $347.7 billion cash and Treasury bill hoard is the most visible manifestation of Berkshire's current stance.15 This massive liquidity represents significant "dry powder" 31, offering immense flexibility and the capacity to act decisively should market conditions change or a large acquisition target emerge at an attractive price.6 However, it also represents a potential opportunity cost if suitable investments remain elusive. The accumulation reflects Buffett's hallmark discipline of refusing to overpay for assets, even when faced with pressure to deploy capital.26 In the current interest rate environment, this cash is not idle; it generates substantial interest income, contributing positively to operating earnings and providing a cushion against weakness elsewhere.13 The combination of net equity sales, the halt in buybacks, and the sheer size of the cash pile strongly signals a cautious view on prevailing market valuations and potentially the broader economic outlook, colored by concerns about tariffs and global instability.10

Emphasis on Operating Businesses:

As Berkshire trims its public equity holdings and cash grows, the relative contribution and importance of its wholly-owned operating businesses-spanning insurance (GEICO, Reinsurance, Primary Group), railroad (BNSF), energy (BHE), and a vast collection of manufacturing, service, and retail companies-are amplified.4 These businesses generated $47.4 billion in operating earnings in 2024 4 and their continued performance is increasingly central to Berkshire's overall value. The strong results from BHE in Q1 suggest that capital deployment within these controlled entities may currently be a more favored avenue for growth than large external investments.13 This could indicate a strategic preference for internal or bolt-on growth within existing segments unless market valuations for external opportunities become significantly more appealing.

## VI. Conclusion

Berkshire Hathaway's reduction of its Apple stake by approximately 13% in the first quarter of 2025 represents a significant portfolio adjustment but should be interpreted within a broader strategic context. While continuing a trend of trimming from 2024, Warren Buffett's commentary at the May 2025 annual meeting strongly reaffirmed Apple's status as a cornerstone investment, likely to remain the largest holding barring unforeseen circumstances.<sup>1</sup>

The decision appears to be multi-faceted, driven less by a negative reassessment of Apple's business fundamentals and more by a combination of tactical portfolio management (reducing concentration risk), potential long-term tax planning, and the strategic generation of cash. This occurred against a backdrop of Berkshire accumulating a record $347.7 billion in cash and short-term Treasuries, halting its share repurchase program due to valuation considerations, and continuing its multi-quarter trend of being a net seller of equities overall.<sup>1</sup>

Collectively, these actions paint a picture of Berkshire Hathaway adopting a cautious and highly selective stance in Q1 2025, perceiving limited value in the broader market and its own stock at prevailing prices. The company seems content to let its cash pile grow, benefiting from higher interest income while maintaining significant "dry powder" for potential future opportunities. The strong performance of operating segments like BHE highlights the increasing importance of Berkshire's controlled businesses to its overall value proposition.<sup>4</sup>

Looking ahead, while Apple is expected to remain a key holding, further adjustments cannot be ruled out and will likely depend on shifts in valuation, market conditions, tax policy, and the emergence of compelling alternative uses for Berkshire's vast capital resources under the eventual leadership of Greg Abel. The deployment, or continued accumulation, of Berkshire's record cash position remains a critical factor for investors monitoring the conglomerate's future trajectory.
