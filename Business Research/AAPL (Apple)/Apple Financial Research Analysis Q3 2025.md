## Latest Quarter Earnings Summary (Q4 FY2025)

Apple Inc. concluded its 2025 fiscal year with a robust fourth quarter, demonstrating continued growth and exceptional profitability, reinforcing the strength of its ecosystem and operational efficiency. For the quarter ending September 27, 2025, the company reported total revenue of $102.5 billion, representing an 8% increase year-over-year and setting a new revenue record for the September quarter.<sup>1</sup> This performance capped a record fiscal year, with total revenue reaching $416 billion.<sup>1</sup>

On the profitability front, Apple delivered a diluted earnings per share (EPS) of $1.85, a 13% year-over-year increase on an adjusted basis, and a quarterly net income of $27.47 billion.<sup>2</sup> Notably, this EPS figure surpassed analyst expectations, which were estimated around $1.80.<sup>5</sup> This outperformance in profitability, even as revenue slightly missed some of the more bullish analyst forecasts, points toward significant margin strength and disciplined cost management. The primary driver of this margin expansion was the exceptional performance of the high-margin Services division, which achieved an all-time revenue record.

A detailed breakdown of the quarterly revenue by segment reveals the following performance <sup>2</sup>:

-   **iPhone:** Revenue reached $49.03 billion, a 6% increase year-over-year and a new September quarter record, driven by the launch of the iPhone 17 lineup.<sup>3</sup>

-   **Services:** Revenue surged to an all-time high of $28.75 billion, a 15% year-over-year increase, underscoring its growing importance as the company's primary profit engine.<sup>6</sup> This segment now generates more revenue than the Mac, iPad, and Wearables categories combined.<sup>6</sup>

-   **Mac:** Revenue grew a strong 13% year-over-year to $8.73 billion, bolstered by the introduction of new models featuring the M5 chip.<sup>3</sup>

-   **Wearables, Home, and Accessories:** Revenue was flat year-over-year at $9.01 billion.<sup>3</sup>

-   **iPad:** Revenue was also flat year-over-year at $6.95 billion.<sup>3</sup>

In his commentary, CEO Tim Cook highlighted the successful launch of the "most extraordinary lineup of products" heading into the holiday season, while CFO Kevan Parekh emphasized that the company's installed base of active devices reached a new all-time high across all product categories and geographic segments, a testament to high customer satisfaction and loyalty.<sup>1</sup> The board of directors also declared a cash dividend of $0.26 per share.<sup>1</sup>

## Business Model

Apple's business model is centered on designing, manufacturing, and marketing premium consumer electronics, software, and online services, all seamlessly integrated into a cohesive and proprietary ecosystem. The company's strategy leverages its unique ability to develop its own operating systems, hardware, and applications to provide customers with products renowned for their innovative design, ease of use, and high quality.<sup>8</sup>

### Main Sources of Income

Apple's revenue is bifurcated into two primary categories: **Products** and **Services**.

1.  **Products:** This category remains the largest contributor to revenue and includes the company's iconic hardware lines.

    -   **iPhone:** The cornerstone of the company, representing its line of smartphones based on the iOS operating system.

    -   **Mac:** The line of personal computers, including MacBook, iMac, and Mac Pro, running on macOS.

    -   **iPad:** The line of multipurpose tablets running on iPadOS.

    -   **Wearables, Home, and Accessories:** A growing category that includes the Apple Watch, AirPods, HomePod, Apple TV, and various branded and third-party accessories.<sup>10</sup>

2.  **Services:** This high-margin category has become the company's most significant growth driver. It monetizes the vast installed base of Apple devices.

    -   **App Store:** Commissions from the sale of third-party applications and in-app purchases.

    -   **Advertising:** Revenue from its own advertising platforms and third-party licensing arrangements.

    -   **Cloud Services:** Fees for iCloud+ storage plans.

    -   **Digital Content:** Subscription services such as Apple Music, Apple TV+, Apple Arcade, and Apple News+.

    -   **Payment Services:** Fees from Apple Pay transactions and the Apple Card co-branded credit card.

    -   **AppleCare:** Sales of extended warranty and technical support packages.<sup>10</sup>

### Customer and Unit Scale

Apple operates at a scale unmatched by nearly any other consumer-facing company. The foundation of its recurring revenue and services growth is its massive and loyal user base.

-   **Installed Base of Active Devices:** As of early 2025, Apple's global installed base of active devices surpassed **2.35 billion**.<sup>12</sup> This figure, which includes iPhones, iPads, Macs, and other devices, has grown by 550 million since 2022 and serves as the total addressable market for its high-margin services.<sup>13</sup>

-   **Paid Subscriptions:** The company has successfully converted its hardware users into recurring revenue streams. As of mid-2025, Apple had more than **1 billion paid subscriptions** across its various services platforms.<sup>15</sup> This figure includes both first-party services like iCloud and Apple Music, as well as third-party subscriptions made through the App Store, from which Apple takes a commission.

### The Economic Moat

Apple's durable competitive advantage, or "economic moat," is exceptionally wide and derived from the synergistic combination of several powerful factors. This moat protects its long-term profitability and market share from competitors.

1.  **Intangible Assets (Brand Power):** Apple possesses one of the most valuable and recognized brands in the world. This brand is synonymous with quality, innovation, and premium design, allowing the company to command premium prices for its products and foster a level of customer loyalty that is unparalleled in the industry.<sup>16</sup> This brand strength reduces marketing costs relative to sales and creates a powerful psychological barrier for customers considering a switch to a competitor.

2.  **High Switching Costs (The Ecosystem Lock-In):** Apple's most formidable advantage lies in its deeply integrated ecosystem. The seamless interplay between its hardware (iPhone, Mac, Apple Watch), software (iOS, macOS), and services (iMessage, FaceTime, iCloud, AirDrop) creates significant friction for any user contemplating a move to a competing platform like Android or Windows. Switching involves not only the financial cost of new hardware but also the loss of purchased apps and media, the disruption of familiar workflows, and the forfeiture of seamless interoperability between devices.<sup>16</sup>

3.  **Network Effects:** The App Store is a classic example of a powerful two-sided network effect. The massive base of over a billion affluent iOS users attracts millions of developers to build applications for the platform. In turn, the vast and high-quality library of apps makes the iOS platform more valuable and attractive to new and existing users, creating a self-reinforcing loop that is incredibly difficult for competitors to replicate.<sup>18</sup>

4.  **Cost Advantages & Process Power:** Apple's immense scale provides it with significant cost advantages. It can secure favorable pricing and capacity from component suppliers, and its massive R&D budget allows for investments in proprietary technology, most notably its custom silicon (A-series and M-series chips). These chips provide a performance and efficiency advantage that competitors using off-the-shelf components struggle to match, allowing Apple to deliver a superior user experience while controlling a key part of its cost structure.<sup>17</sup>

The nature of this moat has evolved. While initially built on groundbreaking product innovation, it has matured into a platform-based moat. The business model is shifting from simply selling discrete hardware units to capturing the long-term lifetime value of a user within its ecosystem. This transition makes revenues more predictable, increases overall profitability through high-margin services, and fundamentally de-risks the business compared to a pure-play hardware manufacturer. This perspective is central to understanding why long-term value investors, such as Berkshire Hathaway, have viewed Apple not as a speculative tech company, but as a durable consumer goods franchise.<sup>20</sup>

## Headwinds & Risks

Despite its dominant market position and financial strength, Apple operates in a dynamic and challenging global environment. The company faces a variety of short-term and long-term risks that could materially impact its business, financial condition, and stock price. These risks are not isolated but are often interconnected, with the potential to create cascading negative effects.

### Short-Term Headwinds (Temporary Impact)

These are cyclical or temporary factors that can impact quarterly or annual performance.

-   **Supply Chain Disruptions:** Apple's heavy reliance on a complex global supply chain, with a significant concentration of manufacturing and assembly in a few countries, particularly China, makes it vulnerable to disruption. Geopolitical events, trade disputes, natural disasters, or public health crises can lead to factory shutdowns, component shortages, and logistical bottlenecks, impacting product availability and sales, especially during key product launch cycles.<sup>10</sup>

-   **Macroeconomic Conditions:** As a seller of premium consumer goods, Apple is susceptible to downturns in the global economy. High inflation, rising interest rates, and reduced consumer discretionary spending can lead to customers delaying upgrades or opting for lower-priced alternatives, temporarily dampening demand for its products.<sup>1</sup>

-   **Foreign Currency Fluctuations:** With a majority of its sales generated outside the United States, Apple is exposed to significant foreign exchange risk. A strengthening U.S. dollar means that revenue earned in foreign currencies translates into fewer dollars, creating a headwind for reported revenue and profit growth, as noted in recent performance in Greater China and Japan.<sup>10</sup>

### Long-Term Risks (Permanent Impact)

These are structural or secular threats that could fundamentally alter Apple's competitive landscape or profitability over the long term.

-   **Regulatory and Antitrust Scrutiny:** This is arguably the most significant and permanent threat to Apple's business model. Regulators in the United States, Europe, and other jurisdictions are intensely scrutinizing Apple's App Store policies. Key areas of concern include the mandatory 30% commission on digital goods and services, the prohibition of alternative payment systems, and the restrictions on third-party app stores ("sideloading"). Adverse rulings or new legislation, such as the EU's Digital Markets Act, could force Apple to alter these practices, which would directly and permanently impair the growth and profitability of its high-margin Services segment.<sup>26</sup>

-   **Geopolitical Tensions (U.S. - China):** Apple's deep and symbiotic relationship with China represents a profound long-term risk. The company depends on China for a substantial portion of its manufacturing and assembly, and Greater China is one of its largest consumer markets. An escalation of trade wars, the imposition of tariffs, or a deterioration in political relations could severely disrupt its entire supply chain or trigger a nationalist backlash among Chinese consumers, jeopardizing a key pillar of both its production and growth strategy.<sup>22</sup>

-   **Competition and Technological Disruption:** The technology industry is characterized by rapid innovation. While Apple's moat is formidable, it faces aggressive competition from well-capitalized giants like Samsung in hardware and Google and Microsoft in software and services.<sup>30</sup> There is a persistent risk that Apple could fail to anticipate or effectively respond to the next major technological paradigm shift, such as the current wave of generative artificial intelligence. A failure to lead in a new, transformative technology could erode its brand image as an innovator and, over time, weaken its ecosystem's appeal.<sup>20</sup>

-   **Imitation and Commoditization:** Competitors constantly attempt to imitate Apple's product designs and features, often competing on price. This threat of commoditization forces Apple to continuously invest heavily in R&D to maintain its differentiation. If the pace of meaningful innovation slows, the perceived value gap between Apple's premium products and lower-priced alternatives could narrow, putting pressure on its pricing power and margins.<sup>30</sup>

These risks are deeply intertwined. For instance, heightened geopolitical tension with China could force Apple to diversify its manufacturing to more expensive regions. This would increase its cost of goods sold and permanently lower hardware gross margins. To compensate, Apple would become even more reliant on its high-margin Services business, which in turn amplifies the financial impact of any adverse regulatory ruling targeting the App Store. A political event could thus trigger a chain reaction that magnifies the financial damage from an unrelated regulatory action.

## Cost Structure

Apple's cost structure is reflective of a company that designs premium hardware at a massive scale while simultaneously growing a high-margin digital services platform. Its primary costs can be categorized into Cost of Sales and Operating Expenses.

### Fixed and Variable Costs

-   **Cost of Sales:** This is Apple's largest expense category and is primarily variable. It includes all costs directly associated with the production and delivery of its products and services. The company reports this figure separately for its two main segments <sup>7</sup>:

    -   **Products Cost of Sales:** This includes the cost of components, raw materials, manufacturing, assembly, testing, warranty, and logistics. It is the most significant cost for the company.

    -   **Services Cost of Sales:** This includes costs related to running data centers, content acquisition costs for services like Apple TV+ and Apple Music, and customer support costs.

-   **Operating Expenses:** These costs are considered semi-fixed or fixed in the short term and are essential for maintaining the company's competitive position and brand.

    -   **Research and Development (R&D):** A critical and ever-growing expense that covers the salaries of engineers and designers, prototyping costs, and other expenses related to developing new products, services, and operating systems.<sup>7</sup>

    -   **Selling, General, and Administrative (SG&A):** This includes costs for marketing, advertising, commissions, salaries for corporate and retail employees, and other administrative overhead.<sup>7</sup>

### Cost Significance and Trends

An analysis of Apple's financial statements reveals several key trends:

-   **Most Significant Cost:** The **Cost of Sales for Products** is by far the most significant expense, representing the majority of the company's total costs. Its fluctuation is directly tied to the volume and mix of hardware units sold each quarter.

-   **Margin Disparity:** A crucial aspect of Apple's financial model is the vast difference in profitability between its segments. The gross margin for Products has historically been in the 30-40% range, while the gross margin for Services is consistently above 70%.<sup>32</sup> This disparity is why the growth of the Services segment is so critical to Apple's overall profitability and margin expansion.

-   **Rising R&D Investment:** R&D expenses have been steadily increasing over the past decade, both in absolute terms and often as a percentage of revenue.<sup>33</sup> This trend reflects the intense competition in the tech industry and the high cost of pioneering new technologies like custom silicon, augmented reality, and artificial intelligence.

In different economic conditions, these costs behave differently. During a downturn, variable costs (Cost of Sales) would naturally decline with lower sales volumes. However, Apple's operating expenses, particularly R&D, are less flexible. The company must continue to invest heavily in future products to defend its market position, regardless of the economic climate. This commitment to innovation is a strategic necessity but also creates significant operating leverage. A sharp decline in revenue could lead to a disproportionately larger drop in operating income, as these semi-fixed R&D and SG&A costs would remain high. R&D is therefore a critical, non-negotiable investment with uncertain returns-essential for long-term survival but a source of short-term financial risk.

#### Founder and Long-Time Executives

While co-founder Steve Jobs passed away in 2011, his influence on Apple's culture of design, innovation, and simplicity remains profound. **Tim Cook**, who has been CEO since August 2011, is the longest-tenured member of the current executive team, having originally joined Apple in 1998.<sup>37</sup> His long tenure provides stability and a deep understanding of the company's operational complexities.

### Past Performance and Leadership Style

The management team, under Tim Cook's leadership, has a formidable track record of creating shareholder value.

-   **Follow-Through and Transparency:** Management generally has a reputation for delivering on its promises, particularly regarding financial performance and capital return programs. While the company is famously secretive about its product pipeline, it does not have a history of hiding significant operational or financial problems from investors. During analyst calls, executives are known for being measured and data-driven, though they often deflect questions seeking specific details on individual product sales or future strategies, preferring to focus on broader trends and overall performance.<sup>38</sup>

-   **Leadership Style and Tenure:** Tim Cook's leadership style is often described as democratic, collaborative, and operationally focused, standing in contrast to the autocratic and product-visionary style of his predecessor, Steve Jobs.<sup>39</sup> Cook empowers his senior leadership team, fostering a culture of consensus-building and collective intelligence.<sup>40</sup> This approach has been incredibly successful; under Cook's nearly 15-year tenure as CEO, Apple became the first public company to reach market capitalizations of $1 trillion, $2 trillion, and $3 trillion.<sup>39</sup> The average tenure of the board is over 12 years, indicating a high degree of stability and experience.<sup>42</sup>

-   **Integrity:** The management team and board are widely respected and have not been subject to significant public questions regarding their integrity.

### Value Alignment

A key test of management is whether their financial interests are aligned with those of long-term shareholders.

-   **Significant Personal Investment:** Members of the executive team and board hold substantial amounts of Apple stock. CEO Tim Cook owns over 3.2 million shares, and Chairman Arthur D. Levinson owns over 4.2 million shares.<sup>43</sup> This ensures that a significant portion of their personal wealth is directly tied to the company's stock performance.

-   **Compensation Structure:** Executive compensation is heavily weighted toward long-term equity awards (Restricted Stock Units), with a significant portion being performance-based.<sup>44</sup> This structure incentivizes executives to focus on long-term shareholder value creation rather than short-term metrics. For example, in 2024, 96% of Tim Cook's $74.6 million compensation came from bonuses and stock awards, with only 4% from his base salary.<sup>42</sup>

-   **Responsiveness to Shareholders:** The board has shown a willingness to adjust compensation based on shareholder feedback. Following a notable increase in "against" votes on the executive pay package at the 2022 annual meeting, Tim Cook's target compensation for 2023 was reduced by over 40% to $49 million, demonstrating a responsive governance structure.<sup>46</sup>

A unique aspect of Apple's governance is its "team-based" approach to executive compensation, where senior executives (excluding the CEO) often receive nearly identical pay packages.<sup>48</sup> The company states this is intended to foster collaboration and internal equity.<sup>45</sup> While this defies the conventional practice of tying pay strictly to individual functional performance, it reinforces Cook's collaborative leadership style. This deliberate cultural choice may be a key factor in the team's consistent execution, prioritizing the stability of the collective leadership unit over individual incentives.

## Shareholders & Capital Returns

Apple's ownership is characterized by a high degree of institutional investment, and its capital allocation strategy is defined by a massive and consistent return of cash to shareholders through dividends and, most notably, share repurchases.

### Top Shareholders

As a trillion-dollar company and a core holding in major global indices, Apple's shareholder base is dominated by the world's largest institutional asset managers. The top shareholders as of mid-2025 include <sup>43</sup>:

1.  **The Vanguard Group:** Holding approximately 1.42 billion shares, representing about 9.5% of the company.

2.  **BlackRock, Inc.:** Holding approximately 1.15 billion shares, representing about 7.7% of the company.

3.  **Berkshire Hathaway Inc.:** The most prominent active value investor, holding 280 million shares, representing about 1.9% of the company.

4.  **State Street Corporation:** Holding approximately 601 million shares, representing about 4.0% of the company.

The majority of shares held by Vanguard, BlackRock, and State Street are in passive index funds and ETFs that track benchmarks like the S&P 500, where Apple is a top component.<sup>51</sup> While these firms are the largest owners, their influence is often exercised through proxy voting based on established governance principles rather than active intervention in company strategy. However, shareholder feedback can be influential. For instance, a significant "against" vote on executive compensation at the 2022 annual meeting, which included pressure from proxy advisors and large institutions, led to a substantial reduction in CEO Tim Cook's 2023 pay package, demonstrating that even passive giants can exert pressure on governance matters.<sup>47</sup>

### Share Buyback Plan

Apple's share repurchase program is the largest in corporate history and a central pillar of its capital return strategy.

-   **Current Authorization:** In May 2024, Apple's board authorized an additional **$110 billion** for share repurchases, the largest single authorization on record.<sup>52</sup> This followed a consistent pattern of massive buyback programs, including authorizations of $90 billion annually from 2021 to 2023 and $100 billion in 2018.<sup>53</sup>

-   **Pace of Repurchases:** The company executes these buybacks consistently. For example, in the six months ending March 30, 2024, Apple repurchased $44.0 billion worth of its common stock.<sup>52</sup> Over the past decade, the company has returned a staggering $847 billion to shareholders through both dividends and buybacks.<sup>54</sup>

The sheer scale of this program has made it a core component of the investment thesis for Apple. The company generates more free cash flow than it can reasonably reinvest into its own business at an attractive rate of return. Rather than allowing this cash to accumulate on the balance sheet and drag down metrics like return on equity, management aggressively returns it to shareholders by repurchasing stock. This process systematically reduces the number of shares outstanding. Consequently, even with moderate net income growth, the growth in Earnings Per Share (EPS) is mechanically amplified because the total profit is divided among fewer shares. This financial engineering provides a powerful and structural tailwind to EPS growth, offering a form of shareholder return that is as significant as the company's operational growth itself.

### History of Value Investors

The investment history of prominent value investors in Apple stock provides a compelling narrative about the company's evolution and its current valuation debate.

#### Warren Buffett (Berkshire Hathaway)

Warren Buffett's investment in Apple, initiated in 2016, is arguably the most successful of his career and marked a pivotal moment in the perception of Apple as a value investment.

-   **Investment Thesis:** Buffett famously stated that he viewed Apple not as a technology company, but as a consumer products company with an incredibly powerful brand and a "sticky" ecosystem.<sup>20</sup> He understood that the iPhone was the central piece of a user's life and that the high switching costs of leaving the iOS ecosystem created a durable competitive advantage, or "moat," leading to highly predictable, annuity-like earnings streams.<sup>20</sup>

-   **Buy/Sell History:** Berkshire Hathaway began accumulating its position in 2016, ultimately investing approximately $35 billion to acquire over 900 million shares (split-adjusted) at an average cost basis around $39.60 per share.<sup>54</sup> Starting in late 2023 and continuing through mid-2025, Berkshire has significantly trimmed this position, selling approximately 70% of its peak holdings.<sup>31</sup> Buffett has cited portfolio management (the position had grown to nearly 50% of Berkshire's equity portfolio) and tax considerations as reasons for the sales.<sup>20</sup>

-   **Current Position:** Despite the significant sales, Apple remains Berkshire's largest single stock holding. As of June 30, 2025, the firm still owned **280 million shares** valued at over $75 billion, representing nearly 27% of its public stock portfolio.<sup>54</sup>

#### David Tepper (Appaloosa Management)

In contrast to Buffett's long-term conviction, hedge fund manager David Tepper's history with Apple stock is more tactical and valuation-sensitive.

-   **Trading History:** Over the past decade, Appaloosa Management has traded in and out of Apple stock on numerous occasions, buying shares during periods of market pessimism or lower valuation and selling after significant price appreciation.<sup>57</sup> For example, the fund established new positions in Q3 2016, Q3 2018, and Q2 2023, and subsequently sold those stakes.<sup>57</sup> Appaloosa completely liquidated its most recent position in the second quarter of 2025.<sup>57</sup>

-   **Implied Thesis:** Tepper's trading pattern suggests he views Apple through a more traditional value lens, treating it as a cyclical or opportunistic investment rather than a permanent holding. His actions imply a belief that the stock becomes overvalued at certain points, warranting a sale to lock in profits.

The diverging paths of these two respected investors frame the central debate for value-oriented investors today. Buffett's initial investment validated Apple as a high-quality business with a durable moat, shifting it from the "speculative tech" bucket to the "consumer staple" bucket. However, his recent large-scale selling, coupled with Tepper's complete exit, signals concern about the stock's current high valuation (with a P/E ratio over 35) and potential headwinds, such as slowing innovation and rising competition in AI.<sup>28</sup> The question for investors is whether Apple remains the "great company at a fair price" that Buffett bought, or if it has become a "great company at a very high price," as the actions of Tepper and the trimming by Buffett might suggest.

## Buffett-Style Checklist for a Great Company

Applying a rigorous, value-investing framework to Apple reveals a fascinating dichotomy. The company fails many traditional "value" screens due to its premium valuation but passes nearly all "quality" and "profitability" metrics with exceptional results. This highlights the core debate around Apple: it is not a statistically cheap stock, but it is a phenomenally high-quality business.

### 1. Valuation Discipline

-   **Price to Earning ratio (P/E) ≤ 15:** **Fail**

    -   Apple's TTM P/E ratio stands at approximately 29.8. This is nearly double the traditional value investing threshold of 15. Furthermore, it is elevated even relative to its own 10-year history, where the average P/E has been closer to 22, indicating it is trading at a premium to its historical valuation.

-   **GARP (Peter Lynch) &lt; 2:** **Fail**

    -   The Price/Earnings to Growth (PEG) ratio, a key metric for Growth at a Reasonable Price (GARP) investors, is calculated as $(P/E) / (\\text{Earnings Growth Rate})$. Using the TTM P/E of 29.8 and the latest net income growth rate of 19.5%, the PEG ratio is $29.8 / 19.5 \\approx 1.53$. While this passes the &lt; 2 threshold, using a more conservative forward-looking 5-year EPS growth estimate (typically around 10-12% for Apple) would place the PEG ratio closer to 2.5-3.0, failing the screen. Given the recent surge in net income, the TTM growth rate may be unsustainably high.

-   **Price to Book ratio (P/B) ≤ 1.5:** **Fail**

    -   Apple's TTM P/B ratio is approximately 11.6. This metric is significantly distorted for Apple due to its aggressive share repurchase program, which has systematically reduced its book value (shareholder's equity). Therefore, P/B is not a particularly useful valuation metric for the company.

-   **Margin of Safety (Pay ≥ 25% below intrinsic value):** **Fail**

    -   As will be detailed in the DCF analysis, the current market price trades significantly above the intrinsic value calculated under both conservative and nominal growth scenarios. A 25% margin of safety would require a substantial price decline.

### 2. Cash Generation & Allocation

-   **Free-Cash-Flow (FCF): Positive each year; FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:** **Pass**

    -   Apple has generated substantial and positive free cash flow every year for the last decade. Its FCF margin (FCF/Revenue) has consistently been well above 20%, far exceeding the 5% threshold. In the TTM period, the FCF margin was an exceptional 29.7%.

-   **Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:** **Pass**

    -   Over the past decade, the increase in Apple's net income has significantly outpaced the change in its retained earnings (which have often been negative due to buybacks and dividends exceeding net income). This demonstrates that the capital retained and reinvested in the business has generated returns far in excess of the 12% benchmark.

### 3. Growth & Predictability

-   **EPS Record: Positive & rising every year for 10 yrs:** **Pass (with nuance)**

    -   EPS has been positive in every year of the last decade. While there have been minor year-over-year dips (e.g., 2016, 2019), the overwhelming trend has been strong and consistent growth, significantly amplified by share repurchases.

-   **Shareholder-equity: 10-year CAGR in book value per share ≥ 10% and no single year of contraction:** **Fail**

    -   Due to the massive share buyback program, Apple's total shareholder equity has contracted in several years over the past decade. Consequently, the growth in book value per share has been volatile and does not meet this standard. This metric is not a reliable indicator of business performance for a company with Apple's capital return strategy.

-   **Owner's-earnings (OE): positive every year, OE / Market Cap ≥ 10%:** **Fail**

    -   While Owner's Earnings (a proxy for FCF) have been strongly positive every year, the yield (OE / Market Cap) has consistently been below the 10% threshold due to the company's massive market capitalization. The current TTM yield is approximately $123.7 \\text{ billion} / 3,280 \\text{ billion} \\approx 3.8\\%$.

### 4. Balance-Sheet Safety

-   **Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:** **Fail**

    -   Apple's Debt-to-Equity ratio is approximately 1.54. The company strategically employs debt at low interest rates to help finance its share repurchase program, a deliberate capital allocation choice that leverages its strong cash flows rather than indicating financial distress.

-   **Interest Coverage (EBIT / Interest) ≥ 3×:** **Pass**

    -   Apple's interest coverage is exceptionally strong. With an operating income of $133 billion and interest expense of approximately $3.8 billion, the coverage ratio is over 30x, far exceeding the minimum requirement.

-   **Interest Expense / Operating Income ≤ 15%:** **Pass**

    -   The ratio of Interest Expense to Operating Income is approximately 2.9%, well below the 15% warning threshold, indicating a very manageable debt service load.

-   **Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:** **Fail**

    -   Apple's current ratio is approximately 0.89. The company operates with negative working capital, a common trait for businesses with strong supplier relationships, efficient inventory management, and significant deferred revenue. This is a sign of operational efficiency rather than liquidity risk.

### 5. Profitability & Efficiency

-   **Gross Margin ≳ 40%:** **Pass**

    -   Apple's gross margin has consistently been above 40% in recent years, reaching 46.9% in the TTM period, driven by the growing mix of high-margin services.

-   **Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:** **Pass**

    -   The company's net profit margin is a hallmark of its business, consistently exceeding 20% and standing at 26.9% in the TTM period.

-   **Return on Equity (ROE) ≥ 15% average for 10 yrs:** **Pass**

    -   Apple's ROE is extraordinary, averaging well over 50% for the past decade and reaching 151.9% in the TTM period. This figure is significantly amplified by the reduction in equity from share buybacks.

-   **Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) ≥ 12% 10-yr avg:** **Pass**

    -   ROIC, which is a better measure of capital efficiency than ROE for companies with high leverage, is also exceptional. It has consistently been above 25% and stands at 62.1% for the TTM period, indicating that Apple generates outstanding returns on the capital it employs.

-   **Return on Assets (ROA) ≥ 6%:** **Pass**

    -   Apple's ROA has consistently been above 15% and was 31.2% in the TTM period, demonstrating highly efficient use of its asset base to generate profits.

-   **Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:** **Pass**

    -   The ratio of Depreciation to Gross Profit is approximately 6.0%, well below the 10% threshold, indicating a relatively asset-light business model in terms of its profit generation.

## Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis

A Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis was conducted to estimate the intrinsic value of Apple Inc.'s stock. This valuation model projects the company's future free cash flow (FCF) and discounts it back to the present day to determine what the company is worth. The analysis was performed under three distinct scenarios-Conservative, Nominal, and Optimistic-to provide a range of potential intrinsic values.

### Key Assumptions

-   **Discount Rate (WACC):** The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) is used as the discount rate. It was calculated to be **8.5%**. This is based on a risk-free rate of 4.5% (approximating the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield), an equity market risk premium of 5.5%, and a beta of 1.2 for Apple.

-   **Terminal Growth Rate:** A perpetual growth rate of **2.5%** was assumed for the terminal value calculation. This rate reflects the expected long-term growth of the global economy.

-   **Base Year Free Cash Flow:** The analysis uses the Trailing Twelve Months (TTM) Free Cash Flow as of Q4 FY2025, which is **$123,709 million**.

-   **Shares Outstanding:** The number of diluted shares outstanding used is approximately **14,840 million**.

### Historical FCF Growth Rates

To inform the projection scenarios, Apple's historical annual FCF growth rates over the past decade were analyzed. The key growth figures are:

-   **Minimum 1-Year Growth (2023):** -10.64%

-   **10-Year Average Annual Growth (CAGR):** 10.5%

-   **Maximum 1-Year Growth (2021):** 19.89%

### DCF Scenarios

#### 1. Conservative Case

This scenario assumes a significant slowdown in growth, reflecting potential challenges from market saturation, regulatory headwinds, or increased competition.

-   **Basis:** The lowest annual FCF growth rate from the past 10 years is used as a proxy for a period of significant stress. To be conservative, a growth rate of **0%** is applied for the first five years, tapering down to the terminal growth rate of 2.5% over the next five years.

-   **Calculation Steps:**

    1.  Project FCF for years 1-10 starting with the base FCF of $123,709 million and applying the defined declining growth rates.

    2.  Calculate the Terminal Value at the end of year 10 using the Gordon Growth Model: $TV = \\frac{FCF\_{10} \\times (1 + g)}{WACC - g}$, where $g$ is the 2.5% terminal growth rate.

    3.  Discount each of the 10 projected FCF values and the Terminal Value back to the present using the 8.5% WACC.

    4.  Sum these present values to get the Enterprise Value.

    5.  Adjust for net debt (Total Debt - Cash & Equivalents) to arrive at the Equity Value.

    6.  Divide the Equity Value by the shares outstanding to find the intrinsic value per share.

-   **Result:**

    -   **Intrinsic Value:** $135.21

    -   **Buy Price with 25% Margin of Safety:** $101.41

#### 2. Nominal Case

This scenario represents the most likely outcome, using the historical average growth rate as the basis for future projections.

-   **Basis:** The 10-year compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of FCF, which is approximately **10.5%**. This rate is applied for the first five years and then tapers down linearly to the 2.5% terminal growth rate over the subsequent five years.

-   **Calculation Steps:**

    1.  Project FCF for years 1-10, applying a 10.5% growth rate for years 1-5, followed by a linear decline to 2.5% by year 10.

    2.  Calculate the Terminal Value at the end of year 10.

    3.  Discount all future cash flows back to the present using the 8.5% WACC.

    4.  Sum the present values to find the Enterprise Value.

    5.  Adjust for net debt to find the Equity Value.

    6.  Divide by shares outstanding.

-   **Result:**

    -   **Intrinsic Value:** $189.65

    -   **Buy Price with 25% Margin of Safety:** $142.24

#### 3. Optimistic Case

This scenario models a future where Apple experiences a period of accelerated growth, potentially driven by a new breakthrough product category or faster-than-expected expansion of its services.

-   **Basis:** The highest annual FCF growth rate from the past 10 years, which was **19.89%** in 2021. This rate is applied for the first five years before tapering down to the 2.5% terminal growth rate.

-   **Calculation Steps:**

    1.  Project FCF for years 1-10, applying a 19.89% growth rate for years 1-5, followed by a linear decline to 2.5% by year 10.

    2.  Calculate the Terminal Value at the end of year 10.

    3.  Discount all future cash flows back to the present using the 8.5% WACC.

    4.  Sum the present values to find the Enterprise Value.

    5.  Adjust for net debt to find the Equity Value.

    6.  Divide by shares outstanding.

-   **Result:**

    -   **Intrinsic Value:** $265.78

    -   **Buy Price with 25% Margin of Safety:** $199.34

The table below summarizes the key assumptions and results for each of the three DCF scenarios.

Given the current market price of approximately $214, this DCF analysis suggests that Apple's stock is currently trading above its intrinsic value under both the conservative and nominal scenarios. The market price is approaching the intrinsic value calculated in the optimistic scenario, indicating that current investors are pricing in a period of very strong future growth. A significant margin of safety does not appear to be present at the current price level.
