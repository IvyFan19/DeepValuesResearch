# **Amazon (AMZN) - Deep Dive Research Report**

## **Background Research**

### **1. Business Model Overview**

**Primary Revenue Streams:** Amazon is a diversified technology and retail conglomerate with several major income sources. Its largest revenue generator is its **e-commerce retail operations** (online and physical stores), followed by its **third-party seller services** (commissions, fulfillment fees), and **Amazon Web Services (AWS)** cloud computing segment. Amazon also earns substantial revenue from **advertising** and **subscription services** (e.g. Prime memberships). In 2024, online retail comprised about 39% of revenue, third-party seller services \~24%, AWS \~17%, advertising \~9%, subscriptions \~7%, and physical stores \~3%. **Customer Base & Units Sold:** Amazon serves **hundreds of millions of active customers** worldwide on its retail platforms and had over **200 million Prime subscribers** globally as of 2021. It does not disclose the exact number of individual customers recently, but Prime membership growth indicates a vast user base. In e-commerce, Amazon shipped an estimated **5+ billion packages annually** (as of late 2020) and continues to grow unit sales. AWS serves **millions of active customers** including enterprises, start-ups, and government agencies; while exact AWS customer count isn't public, AWS holds \~34% cloud market share. Amazon's high-volume marketplace handles **over 2.5 billion visits monthly**, and third-party sellers sold **more than 3.9 billion products in 2022** on Amazon's platform. These figures underscore Amazon's massive scale.

**Subscriptions Sold:** Amazon Prime, its flagship subscription, exceeded **200 million members** globally by 2021, up from 150 million in early 2020. Besides Prime, Amazon has tens of millions of subscribers for services like **Amazon Music, Prime Video, Audible, Kindle Unlimited**, etc. For example, Amazon disclosed over **175 million Prime Video streamers** in 2021. These subscription services contribute steady revenue (about $44.4B in 2024, \~7% of total). Overall, Amazon's model is **high-volume, low-margin retail** complemented by **high-margin AWS and ads**, and a growing base of subscription revenue.

### **2. Headwinds and Risks**

**Short-Term (Temporary) Factors:** In the near term, Amazon faces macroeconomic and industry challenges that could **temporarily impact results**. One key factor is **consumer spending softness during economic slowdowns or high inflation**, which can slow retail revenue growth. For instance, foreign exchange fluctuations and cautious consumer demand caused Amazon to project slower Q1 2025 sales. **Supply chain constraints** and component shortages (e.g. limited supplies of chips for AWS data centers) have also emerged as short-term headwinds. Additionally, **slowing growth in AWS** as enterprise customers optimize cloud spending in a weak economy has recently pressured Amazon's quarterly results. On the political side, **antitrust scrutiny and regulatory actions** pose near-term uncertainty - e.g. the FTC's ongoing lawsuit (filed 2023) could distract management and create headline risk. **Labor cost increases** (from labor shortages or unionization efforts) can raise fulfillment expenses in the short run, though Amazon has managed through such pressures before. Overall, these factors - macro softness, supply constraints, currency swings, and regulatory noise - may **temper Amazon's results temporarily**, but are not expected to derail its long-term trajectory.

**Long-Term (Structural) Factors:** Over a longer horizon, Amazon faces more **enduring risks** that could permanently influence its business model. One major risk is **regulatory and legal changes** - for example, global antitrust actions or a potential AWS/retail breakup could structurally impact Amazon's operations. In the EU, the Digital Markets Act investigations threaten fines up to 10% of revenue (\~$34B) if Amazon is found privileging its own products. Another long-term risk is **increased competition**: in e-commerce (from Walmart, Alibaba, etc.), in cloud (from Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud), and in advertising (Google, Facebook). If competitors erode Amazon's market share or force margin compression (e.g. cloud price wars), Amazon's growth and profitability could be **permanently lower**. Additionally, **shifts in technology or consumer behavior** - such as the rise of social commerce, new retail models, or AI changes in cloud services - require Amazon to continually adapt or risk obsolescence. **Reputation and ESG issues** also pose lasting risk: controversies around data privacy, counterfeit products, worker safety, or political lobbying could **damage Amazon's brand** and customer goodwill long-term. For example, criticism of Amazon's labor practices and its political contributions (supporting certain PACs) has drawn public ire, which over time could affect sales if consumers turn to more "ethical" alternatives. Finally, a more permanent headwind is **market saturation** - as Amazon already commands \~40-50% of U.S. e-commerce, future retail growth will inevitably slow, and AWS growth will moderate as the cloud market matures. In summary, **antitrust/regulatory outcomes, aggressive competition, reputational challenges, and market maturation** are key long-term concerns that investors watch.

### **3. Cost Structure - Fixed vs Variable Costs**

**Variable Costs:** Amazon explicitly states it strives to **"reduce our variable costs per unit and leverage our fixed costs."** Variable costs "change directly with sales volume". For Amazon, major variable costs include: **cost of goods sold**(wholesale product costs for retail inventory), **content acquisition costs** (for Prime Video, etc.), **payment processing fees**, **packaging and shipping** for each order, **transportation/freight and delivery** (fuel, third-party carriers, etc.), **customer service support**, **AWS operating costs** (power, server depreciation tied to usage), and a portion of **marketing spend** that scales with sales. Essentially, every additional unit sold or service provided incurs incremental costs - e.g. the expense of picking, packing, and shipping each order. As of 2023, Amazon's cost of sales (which is largely variable) was $330.3B, about 57.5% of revenue. **Fulfillment expenses** (warehousing labor, order handling) are partly variable, rising with order volume ($97.3B in 2023). The company's goal is to drive these per-unit variable costs down via efficiency and scale - e.g. optimizing shipping routes, obtaining supplier discounts, and reducing defects in fulfillment.

**Fixed Costs:** Amazon's fixed costs are those that **do not directly fluctuate with short-term sales volume**, often related to capacity and long-term investments. Amazon cites "fixed costs include the costs to build and run our technology infrastructure; to build, enhance, and add features to our online stores, web services, devices, and digital offerings; and to build and optimize our fulfillment network". These are large capital expenditures and operating expenses that are relatively fixed over a quarter or year. Examples are **depreciation of fulfillment centers and data centers**, **research & development (technology and content)** which was \~$73B in 2023 (classified as "Technology & Content" expense), **staff salaries** for corporate and tech employees, **rent and utilities** for facilities, and **long-term lease obligations** (Amazon leases warehouses, office space, aircraft, etc.). Even if Amazon's sales dip briefly, it still incurs these fixed costs. Amazon invested heavily in fixed infrastructure during 2020-2021 (doubling its fulfillment network), which temporarily inflated costs. Fixed costs "depend on timing of capacity needs, expansion, and other factors" rather than current sales. Importantly, Amazon's model aims to **spread fixed costs over a growing sales base**, achieving economies of scale.

**Cost per Unit:** While Amazon doesn't publicly disclose a single "cost per unit" shipped, we can infer it from metrics. In 2022, for example, Amazon's **cost of fulfillment per order** rose due to labor and fuel inflation, prompting cost-cutting in 2023. Amazon's strategy is to **continuously drive down per-unit costs** - e.g. increasing warehouse automation to handle more units per employee hour, optimizing delivery routes to cut fuel per package, and leveraging bulk shipping rates. In Q4 2024, Amazon's operating margin improved to **11.3% (from 7.8%)** largely because cost per unit efficiencies were realized. So, although an exact dollar figure isn't given, cost per unit trends are **improving after a spike in 2021-2022**, and Amazon's scale generally gives it a cost advantage over competitors.

**Labor and Marketing Expenses:** Labor is a significant semi-variable cost for Amazon. It employed **1.54 million full- and part-time employees** as of Dec 2022, mostly in fulfillment centers. Labor costs (wages, benefits) appear in **"Fulfillment"** and **"Technology & Content"** expenses, which together were $110.1B in 2023. This includes hundreds of thousands of warehouse associates and drivers, as well as software engineers and AWS staff. Labor costs have risen with wage hikes (Amazon's minimum wage is $18+ in the U.S.) and hiring sprees, but Amazon also scales back hiring in slower periods (corporate headcount dropped \~1% YoY in 2023). **Advertising & marketing** expenses were $20.4B in 2023 (in "Sales and Marketing"). This covers online marketing, Prime promotions, and device advertising. Marketing expense is more discretionary - Amazon increased marketing 18% to $4.6B in Q4 2024 to drive holiday sales, but can also trim marketing in leaner times (it grew slower than sales in 2022-23). Marketing spend as % of sales was \~7.2% in 2023.

**Largest Cost Components:** The most significant cost categories for Amazon are: **Cost of Sales (COGS)** - $332B in 2023 - which includes product and digital content costs; **Fulfillment** - $97B in 2023; **Technology & Content (R&D)** - $79B in 2023; and **Sales/Marketing** - $20B. COGS and fulfillment (warehouse operations and delivery) are the largest chunks, reflecting the cost-intensive nature of retail. AWS costs are embedded in Tech & Content and COGS (for depreciation of data centers). **Shipping costs** alone were $84B in 2022 (net shipping cost after fees), illustrating how substantial logistics is. Thus, **fulfillment and shipping** together are arguably Amazon's biggest cost center (well over 20% of revenue). Over time, these costs have grown in absolute terms but Amazon works to **improve efficiency**. For instance, despite inflation, Amazon's fulfillment cost as % of sales dipped from 16.4% in 2021 to 16.0% in 2023, indicating productivity gains.

**Cost Trends:** In boom times (like 2020-21), Amazon heavily invested in capacity - fixed costs jumped (capital leases for new warehouses, etc.), and variable costs rose with volume. When the economy cooled in 2022, Amazon found itself with **excess capacity and higher unit costs**, which hurt margins (2022 net margin was -0.5%). In response, in 2023 Amazon undertook cost control measures - closing some facilities, pausing hiring, and terminating some experimental projects - leading to a resurgence in operating leverage. In expansionary periods Amazon's costs grow rapidly (often faster than sales, as it invests ahead of demand), compressing short-term margins, whereas in slower periods Amazon focuses on efficiency (as seen in 2023's significant cost improvements). For example, **capital expenditures** peaked in 2021 (to expand AWS and fulfillment) and then moderated by 2023. *Economic cycles:* In recessions, Amazon tends to **tighten variable costs** (slower hiring, cut back on marketing, extend vendor payment terms) to protect cash flow, while in strong economies it will spend aggressively on growth initiatives. Over the long run, Amazon's per-unit costs generally **trend downward** thanks to scale economies - e.g. its fulfillment cost per unit in 2019 was significantly lower than in 2014, and although 2020-2022 disrupted this trend, Amazon's ongoing automation (robotics in warehouses, delivery drones, etc.) aims to resume cost-per-unit declines.

### **4. Management Team Assessment**

**Leadership Composition:** Amazon's leadership blends long-tenured insiders and newer executives. The **current CEO is Andy Jassy**, who succeeded founder Jeff Bezos in July 2021. Jassy is a 25+ year Amazon veteran who launched and led AWS. **Jeff Bezos** remains as Executive Chairman of the Board, providing strategic guidance (Bezos founded Amazon in 1994 and was CEO for 27 years). Other key executives include **Brian Olsavsky (CFO since 2015)**, **Dave Clark**(Consumer CEO until mid-2022) replaced by **Doug Herrington** (CEO of Worldwide Amazon Stores), and **Adam Selipsky** (CEO of AWS since 2021, succeeding Jassy). Amazon's **Board of Directors** is chaired by Bezos and includes notable independent directors like Indra Nooyi (former PepsiCo CEO), Tom Ryder, Jamie Gorelick, and Patty Stonesifer. The board has **9 members** with diverse backgrounds in tech, finance, and government. Historically, Bezos's influence looms large - he instilled the customer-centric culture. Many of the current top team (Jassy, Olsavsky, Herrington) have **20+ years at Amazon**, indicating deep company knowledge and continuity. Amazon's leadership bench is known for its "home-grown" execs who rotate through roles (e.g. AWS's new CEO Selipsky was an AWS VP earlier, left to Tableau, then returned) maintaining cultural consistency.

**Past Performance & Credibility:** Amazon's management has a strong track record of **setting and achieving long-term goals**, albeit sometimes at the expense of short-term guidance. Jeff Bezos famously told investors to focus on **long-term free cash flow over short-term profits**, a promise largely fulfilled as Amazon's cash flows grew dramatically over decades. Under Bezos, management often **underpromised and overdelivered** - e.g. beating revenue guidance in many quarters. Since Andy Jassy took over, Amazon has faced challenges (e.g. navigating post-pandemic slowdown), but management responded with cost cuts and refocusing on efficiency, which have restored profitability in 2023. Analysts on earnings calls have noted Amazon's **transparency about headwinds** - for example, CFO Olsavsky plainly discussed the impact of inflation and excess capacity in 2022, rather than obscuring the problems. However, there have been instances of management **missteps**: e.g. overbuilding logistics capacity by 2022 was a strategic error, which Jassy admitted and moved to correct by delaying warehouse projects. Generally, Amazon management is considered **forthcoming and responsive to issues** - when AWS growth slowed to \~16% in 2023, Jassy and team outlined plans to invest in AI to reaccelerate cloud growth. They do not shy away from tough questions: on the Q1'25 call, Jassy addressed concerns about AWS margins and AI investments head-on.

Importantly, Amazon's leadership has *not* been associated with scandals or integrity issues regarding financial reporting - **no major accounting frauds or hiding of problems**. Bezos and now Jassy have built credibility by often acknowledging faults (Bezos would famously list Amazon's failures in shareholder letters). In the investment community, Amazon's management is seen as **innovative and willing to sacrifice short-term metrics for long-term growth**, which they've consistently done (and communicated clearly via Bezos's annual letters). This consistency has earned them trust - e.g. Bezos warned investors for years that profit would be minimal as they reinvest, which was true, and ultimately those investments paid off (AWS is now hugely profitable, advertising is growing).

**Stability & Integrity:** The senior management team has mostly been stable - many have been with Amazon for a decade or more. The one notable departure was Consumer CEO Jeff Wilke in 2021 (retired after 20+ years). There have been *questions raised* about Amazon's treatment of third-party sellers and data usage (a 2020 report claimed Amazon used 3P seller data to launch competing products, which Bezos denied to Congress), but these issues are more about policy than individual integrity. No serious accusations of personal misconduct or fraud hang over the top team. When pressed in Congress in 2020 and 2021, Bezos and later Jassy provided extensive written responses about Amazon's practices - albeit sometimes avoiding direct yes/no answers on sensitive questions (like whether they favor their own products). So while Amazon can be tight-lipped about internal metrics (they historically didn't break out advertising segment until recently, for example), this is viewed as competitive strategy rather than deception. In summary, management's **credibility remains solid**: they typically follow through on major initiatives (e.g. 1-day Prime shipping rollout was completed as promised in 2019-20). There's no significant history of hiding major problems - even during the 2018 HQ2 search debacle or the failed Fire Phone product, Bezos openly discussed lessons learned.

**Management's Skin in the Game:** Amazon's leadership is **highly aligned with shareholders' interests**. Jeff Bezos, though he's sold some stock to fund Blue Origin, still owns \~9.7% of Amazon (approx. **908 million shares** as of early 2025) - making him the largest shareholder. This huge stake (worth \~$200+ billion) means Bezos's wealth is tied directly to Amazon's long-term performance. Andy Jassy, while not as wealthy as Bezos, also has significant equity - upon becoming CEO, he was granted a stock package of \~61,000 shares vesting over 10 years (worth about $130 million at grant). He also likely accumulated shares from his AWS leadership; his net worth (largely Amazon stock) is around $400 million. Other top executives routinely receive stock-based compensation - in 2024, Amazon's stock-based comp expense was $22B. The executive team's pay is structured such that **stock awards dominate** salaries, incentivizing them to increase Amazon's share price long-term. For instance, in 2021, over 80% of executive compensation was in stock units. Additionally, several directors and officers have **personally bought shares** on dips: e.g. director Daniel Huttenlocher bought \~$250k of AMZN stock during the March 2020 market crash, signaling confidence. While Bezos periodically sells shares (about $10B in 2021-2023) for his space ventures, he has publicly stated that *"Amazon is the best place to use my money right now"* and has not diversified much beyond Amazon. The new CEO Jassy has not been reported to sell significant stock since taking the helm; instead, he's focused on executing his turnaround plan to justify his stock grants. Moreover, Amazon insiders collectively still hold \~8.6% of the company (mostly Bezos and early execs), and institutional holders like Vanguard (7.8%) and BlackRock (6.6%) monitor governance closely. With management so heavily invested in Amazon equity, their incentive is to **drive long-term share appreciation** rather than seek short-term bonuses. Indeed, Bezos's wealth grew astronomically by reinvesting in growth rather than extracting cash via dividends - a philosophy likely continued under Jassy. We haven't observed insiders panic-selling during declines; even in the 2022 drop, Bezos sold only a planned amount. The alignment is further shown by buybacks: while not heavy, Amazon did repurchase \~$6B of stock in 2022 when shares were under pressure, suggesting management believed the stock was undervalued (though they paused buybacks in 2023 to preserve cash). Overall, Amazon's management and board appear **committed to the company's long-term success, with personal wealth tied to that success**.

### **5. Top Shareholders & Their Influence**

**Major Shareholders:** Amazon's ownership is relatively decentralized. The **top shareholder is Jeff Bezos**, with about **9.3% of shares (938 million shares)** as of early 2025. The next largest stakes are held by institutional investors: **Vanguard Group owns \~7.8%**, **BlackRock \~6.6%**, **State Street \~3.5%**, and **Fidelity (FMR) \~3.2%**. Other notable holders include **Geode Capital (\~2.0%)**, **JP Morgan (\~1.9%)**, and **T. Rowe Price (\~1.6%)**. No other individual shareholder besides Bezos owns more than \~1%. This means Amazon's shareholder base is dominated by index funds and large asset managers (together the top 10 holders own \~34% of shares). These institutions typically vote in line with management on routine matters, though on environmental or governance proposals they may vote independently per their policies.

**Voting Behavior & Key Issues:** Historically, Amazon's shareholders have mostly **backed management's recommendations**. For example, in 2024's annual meeting, **78% of votes** approved the executive pay package (say-on-pay), and all board nominees were elected with comfortable majorities. **Shareholder proposals**, often on environmental, social, or governance (ESG) topics, have *not passed* at Amazon to date - though some drew significant support. In 2022, a proposal to audit warehouse working conditions got 44% support (nearly passing), reflecting some big investors' concern. By 2025, however, support for such proposals **dropped sharply** - e.g. a 2025 resolution on plastic packaging impact received only 13.5% (down from 49% in 2022). This suggests that major institutions like Vanguard and BlackRock, who had previously been more open to ESG votes, **reverted to siding with management's stance** in 2025. Indeed, Amazon's board opposed all 18 shareholder resolutions in 2023 and 2024, and none passed. The top institutions appear to have voted **against** proposals on issues like climate risk reporting, worker representation on the board, and gender/racial pay gap reporting - all of which were defeated (most under 30% support). For instance, **BlackRock and State Street**reportedly voted against a 2023 proposal to require a report on warehouse worker injuries, contributing to its failure (it got \~21% support). Meanwhile, **Bezos's shares (9.3%) plus allied insiders (8.6%)** reliably vote with management, providing a substantial base vote. Bezos's influence cannot dictate outcomes alone, but combined with broad index fund support, management's position has easily carried every vote so far.

**Key Topics & Shareholder Stances:** The most common issues brought by activist shareholders involve **sustainability (climate impact, plastic use)**, **workplace safety and labor rights**, **anti-competitive practices**, and **tax transparency**. In 2022, a climate-related proposal (on adding an employee to the board with climate expertise) got nearly 49% - indicating even some large institutions supported it. However, by 2025 the climate proposals saw waning momentum: e.g. a resolution on halting use of plastic packaging only 13.5%, likely because big passive investors followed management's argument that Amazon is already addressing it. **Vanguard and BlackRock**, facing U.S. political pressures, have become less supportive of environmental/social shareholder proposals in 2023-2024 (as reflected in these vote swings). One proposal that did garner notable support in 2025 was a call for a report on **data center climate impact (energy usage for AWS)** - it got 19.9%, the second highest that year, showing that some shareholders remain concerned about Amazon's growing carbon footprint from AI and cloud. Still, no proposal got close to 50% in 2025.

In summary, **Bezos (with \~10%) and the large index funds (\~60% combined) have effectively aligned with management** on voting matters. They have not forced any governance changes (like splitting CEO/Chair - which is already done, Jassy is CEO and Bezos Chair). When a significant number of shares supported ESG proposals in 2022, Amazon responded with more disclosures to appease investors, and by 2025 support for those proposals declined, indicating satisfaction or shifting priorities. Notably, **no high-profile activist investor** (like Elliott or Third Point) is agitating publicly at Amazon - likely because the stock's governance is controlled by Bezos and amicable institutions, leaving little room for an activist campaign. Amazon's largest outside investor, Vanguard, adheres to voting guidelines and engages privately rather than via public demands. **Top shareholders' stands:** they generally trust Amazon's board and **vote with management on elections and compensation**, while only a minority of their votes favor environmental or social proposals. For instance, Vanguard's voting disclosures show it voted *against* most Amazon ESG resolutions in 2023, believing Amazon's existing policies were adequate. In conclusion, Amazon's major shareholders have executed their voting rights largely to **reinforce management's strategy**, and none of the big ones have pushed for radical changes (like a breakup or dividend initiation) at recent AGMs.

### **6. Share Buyback Plan (Current vs Historical)**

**Current Buyback Program:** Amazon initiated its first substantial share repurchase program only recently. In March 2022, the Board authorized a **$10 billion share repurchase program** (with no expiration). This replaced an earlier $5B program from 2016 under which Amazon had repurchased about $2.12B of stock. Under the new 2022 plan, Amazon **bought back $6.0B worth of shares in 2022 (46.2 million shares)**. These repurchases were concentrated in H1 2022 when the stock price dropped - Amazon likely saw it as opportunistic value buy (shares fell from \~$170 to \~$100 post-stock-split in that period). Notably, Amazon **did not repurchase any stock in 2023 or 2024**. As of Dec 31, 2024, **$6.1B remained authorized** under the plan. So Amazon has used only \~39% of the 2022 authorization. CFO Olsavsky indicated in early 2023 that the company paused buybacks to preserve cash amid economic uncertainty. The **current plan is still open** with \~$6.1B capacity, and management can resume buybacks if they see excess cash and a favorable stock valuation.

**Comparison to Past 10 Years:** Historically, Amazon has *not* been a big repurchaser of stock - instead preferring to reinvest cash in growth. From 2012 to 2021, Amazon's total buybacks were minimal (only \~$2B in 2016-2017 period, and essentially zero in most years). For example, in 2017 Amazon bought back \~$0.7B (pre-split) of stock but then issued more for employee compensation, resulting in a net slight increase in share count. **Shares Outstanding** actually rose steadily from 2015's 9.54B (split-adjusted) to 2021's 10.30B due to stock-based compensation and small acquisitions. It dipped in 2022 to 10.19B after the buybacks, then rose again to 10.72B by 2024 due to resumed stock grants outpacing no buybacks. Over the last decade, Amazon's approach was: *issue shares to employees liberally as growth currency, and rarely repurchase.* This changed in 2022 when Amazon had amassed a large cash pile (over $80B in cash & marketable securities) and the stock's P/E had compressed post-COVID - they opportunistically retired shares. Compared to other tech giants, Amazon's buybacks have been **modest**. For instance, Apple and Microsoft each spent tens of billions annually on buybacks, whereas Amazon's largest was $6B in 2022.

**10-Year Retrospective:** From 2013-2022, Amazon increased its outstanding shares about +14% (from \~9.3B to \~10.6B) due to stock compensation, with effectively no offsetting repurchases until 2022. The **2016 $5B authorization** was mostly unused (only \~$2.1B utilized by 2018), and the board let it expire and replaced it in 2022. Even after initiating the $10B program, Amazon showed restraint - it could have used all $10B given its cash flow rebound in 2023 (FCF was +$32B), but instead it prioritized debt reduction and investments (and perhaps anticipated needing cash for large AI/datacenter capex in 2024). As Bloomberg noted in mid-2024, Amazon's buyback was "less than half complete" and none done in 2023. Indeed, Amazon confirmed it **did zero buybacks in 2023 or 2024**.

Overall, **Amazon's buyback policy remains conservative**: they will repurchase shares primarily to offset dilution or if they view shares as undervalued relative to long-term prospects. The current $10B plan (6% of current market cap) is small compared to Amazon's $1.3+ trillion market value. In contrast, the **past 10 years saw share count rise** due to Amazon investing internally rather than returning cash. Only in the very recent period did they do a meaningful buyback, reflecting a maturing business generating surplus cash. Investors should not expect regular or large buybacks from Amazon - management has signaled preference for using cash for expansion (e.g., $1.3B investment in AI startup Anthropic in 2023). When Amazon does buy back shares, it's a **signal of confidence** that they have more cash than high-ROI projects available. The $6B repurchase in 2022 at an average \~$130/share (split-adjusted) looks savvy as the stock now trades \~$223 (June 2025). Going forward, Amazon may resume modest repurchases, but likely well below the scale of its tech peers - consistent with its historical pattern of prioritizing growth and strategic flexibility over shareholder yield.

## **8. Intrinsic Value Estimate (Buffett-Style)**

To estimate Amazon's intrinsic value "Warren Buffett style," we focus on **Owner Earnings (free cash flows)** and discount them at a conservative rate, with a margin of safety. Amazon's owner earnings can be approximated by its **free cash flow (FCF)**, since the company's heavy investments are largely growth-oriented (Buffett might adjust for those, but we'll use reported FCF as a baseline). Amazon's trailing-twelve-month free cash flow as of Q1 2025 was about **$20.8B**(down from the 2024 full-year $36.2B due to Q1 seasonality). For our valuation, we use **FCF of $35B for 2024** (the year-end reported FCF) as a starting owner earnings.

**DCF Inputs (Conservative Case):** We assume Amazon's FCF will grow at the **lowest growth rate of the past 10 years**. Amazon's lowest annual revenue growth in the last decade was about **9.4% (2022)**, and net income swung negative that year. To be conservative, we'll assume FCF grows only **8% annually for the next 5 years** (roughly similar to the lowest revenue growth year, and well below AWS's historical growth). After 5 years, assume a **terminal growth rate of 2.5%**(below GDP, as required). We use a **discount rate of 10%** (Buffett often uses around 10% as his hurdle). Shares outstanding ≈ **10.7B**.

-   **Year 1 FCF:** $35B.

-   **Years 2-5 FCF growth:** 8% per year.
    > So FCF (Year1-Year5): 35, 37.8, 40.8, 44.1, 47.6 ($B).

-   **Terminal Year FCF:** $47.6B growing at 2.5% thereafter.

Discounting these at 10%: Present value of first 5-year cash flows ≈ **$158B**. Present value of terminal value = 47.6×(1+0.025)0.10-0.025 = $651B future terminal value in Year 5, PV now = \~**$404B**. Summing = **$562B total PV of Amazon's equity**. Divide by 10.7B shares = **\~$52.5 per share intrinsic value** (in this ultra-conservative scenario). This is **far below the current price (\~$223)**, reflecting how pessimistic these inputs are. Clearly, the market expects higher growth.

**DCF Inputs (Average Growth Case):** Over the past 10 years, Amazon's revenue CAGR was \~**20-25%**, and FCF (excluding the investment-heavy 2020-22 period) grew similarly. Let's assume a more optimistic but still reasonable scenario: **FCF grows at 15% annually for 5 years** (which is around AWS's current growth rate and below Amazon's 10-yr avg revenue growth of \~22%). Terminal growth 2.5% as before, discount 10%. Using 2024 FCF $35B:

-   Year1-5 FCF: 35, 40.3, 46.3, 53.2, 61.2 ($B).

-   PV of years1-5 ≈ **$180B**.

-   Terminal value in year5 = 61.2 \\times 1.025 / (0.10-0.025) ≈ $838B. PV now ≈ **$520B**.

-   Total PV = $700B. /10.7B shares = \*\*$65.4 per share\*\* intrinsic value.

Even this is far below current price. Why? Because a 10% discount is high for a dominant company still growing - perhaps investors accept a lower return (say 8% discount).

**Expected Return (Implied by Current Price):** To find what return the market is pricing in, we set margin of safety to 0 and adjust discount rate until DCF equals current price \~$223. At \~$223, Amazon's market cap is \~$2.39T. If we use our average growth FCF projections, solving for discount rate yields an implied required return of only **\~5.5%**. That is, at $223, the DCF (with 15% 5yr growth) equals $223 if discount rate \~5.5%. Alternatively, one could say the market expects far higher growth than 15% - indeed, if AWS/Ads reaccelerate, FCF could double over 5 years, justifying a higher valuation.

Buffett might approach Amazon's intrinsic value by looking at **owner earnings yield**. In 2024, with $35B FCF on a $2.3T market cap, the **FCF yield is \~1.5%** - not traditionally "cheap" by value metrics. However, Buffett would note Amazon is reinvesting heavily and its true earnings power is greater. For instance, 2023 operating income was $22.9B but should rise with AWS and cost efficiencies (Q4'24 op income was annualizing $68B!). If one believes Amazon can sustainably make, say, $60B net income in a few years (which Q4'24 hints at), then at $2.3T it's \~38x future earnings, or an earnings yield \~2.6%. Still rich, but for a company of Amazon's quality - Buffett might accept it if he expects long runway.

**Buffett's Method Conclusion:** Using a **15% discount rate and 50% margin of safety** (Buffett's stringent hurdle), Amazon would not meet a buy price - those would demand the stock under $70. Clearly, that didn't happen except maybe in the 2008 crisis (pre-split \~$40 which is $2 post-split). However, using a more **reasonable 10% discount**, our DCF yields around **$65 intrinsic** (with modest growth). This suggests the market is pricing in either stronger growth or a lower required return (which might be true given low interest rates until recently). In practice, Buffett's vice-chair Charlie Munger has said "Amazon is a miraculous company" and they were willing to pay up for quality. Indeed, Berkshire's average buy was \~$1680 pre-split, a P/E of \~80 at the time - hardly a classic Graham value, but a bet on Amazon's economic moat.

**Intrinsic Value Range:** Based on scenarios:

-   Low-growth (10% disc): \~$50-$60 per share.

-   Moderate (15% growth, 10% disc): \~$65.

-   If we assume a **higher terminal margin** (from cost cuts) and slightly lower discount (8%), intrinsic value might approach **$100-$120**. But none reach $223 - meaning current price anticipates high growth and profitability beyond our conservative DCF.

However, one could argue our FCF starting point is depressed - 2024's $35B FCF included heavy capex for AI. If future normalized FCF is more like $60B, redoing DCF with 12% growth yields intrinsic \~$180/share at 10% discount. So there's wide sensitivity.

**Conclusion:** By Buffett's strict margin of safety, Amazon is **not "on sale"** at $223. Buffett has said he'd love if Amazon got cheap enough to buy more, but so far Berkshire holds, not adds at this price. The "Buffett intrinsic value" might be around **$120-$150** in our view (where the expected return would be \~10-15%). Current price $223 implies an expected return closer to **6-7%** annually, which perhaps large investors are okay with for a mega-cap with relatively lower risk.

To summarize: **intrinsic value (Buffett method) is likely below the market price**, indicating the stock isn't a classic value bargain. But Buffett often pays up for exceptional businesses - and Amazon, with dominant market positions and diversified cash flows, fits that mold.

### **2. Company Analysis Based on Metrics**

**Financial Health & Growth:** Amazon's financial metrics paint a picture of a **mega-cap still growing robustly**. Double-digit revenue growth persisted even beyond $500B in sales, a feat few companies have ever achieved. This top-line growth, combined with improving margins in 2023-24, suggests Amazon has successfully scaled its infrastructure. The expansion of gross margins to \~44% and return to \~10%+ operating margins in 2024highlight the **operating leverage**inherent in Amazon's model now - investments in fulfillment and AWS are largely built, so incremental sales are adding disproportionately to profit. The financials reveal Amazon's *two engines:*

-   The **consumer commerce engine** (retail + third-party + subscriptions + ads) that is lower margin but huge volume, now augmented by advertising which boosted margins,

-   The **AWS engine** that is high margin and still growing at mid-teens.

The metrics show each engine's impact. For instance, **ads are in "Cash Flow Metric" boosting net margin** (from \~5% in 2023 to 9.3% in 2024 partly due to Ads up \~19%), and **AWS in "Valuation Metric" lowering P/E** because AWS profits grew faster than the stock price.

**Profitability Turnaround:** The stark swing from net loss -$2.7B in 2022 to $59.3B net profit in 2024 is extraordinary. It tells us 2022 was an anomaly of over-expansion and external costs, and that Amazon's core earnings power is enormous when things normalize. A $59B profit puts Amazon in the rarefied club of Apple/Google in terms of annual earnings. The **ROE \~21%** in 2024 indicates Amazon is effectively utilizing shareholder capital (21% ROE on $286B equity means \~$60B profit, matching net income). This also suggests Amazon could start returning capital (like buybacks) without hurting growth, as its internal projects likely can't absorb all cash at high returns now. Indeed, debt reduction and resumed buybacks in 2022-24 corroborate that.

**Valuation vs Peers:** At \~40x 2024 earnings, Amazon isn't "cheap," but considering EPS doubled from 2023 to 2024, the forward P/E might be closer to \~30x (if EPS grows further in 2025). For comparison, Apple is \~30x, Microsoft \~28x, Google \~25x P/E in 2024 - Amazon is somewhat higher, reflecting investors pricing more growth. Amazon's P/B \~8x is high but note \~40% of assets are PPE that produce AWS/fulfillment cash flows, and another chunk is cash - its intangible value (brand, Prime ecosystem) is not on the books.

**Financial Resilience:** The balance sheet ratios show Amazon is financially solid: Current ratio \~1.15 means it comfortably meets short-term obligations (and historically Amazon thrives on negative working capital with millions of Prime customers paying upfront). The Debt/Asset at 12% and interest coverage extremely high (EBITDA \~$90B vs interest \~$2B) means negligible default risk. During 2022's downturn, Amazon did not face any liquidity crunch - they had so much cash and could even slow capex to adjust FCF. The net cash position in 2024 ($25B net cash) gives a cushion for any macro hiccup.

**Growth Trajectory:** The metrics suggest Amazon's growth is slowing from hyper-growth but still robust. The 11% revenue growth in 2023-24 came during a tough macro and shows Amazon taking share or creating new revenue streams (Ads, etc.) even in a slower economy. AWS's growth slowed from \~30% to \~16% currently, but if AI triggers cloud demand, AWS could reaccelerate - that's an upside not reflected yet. Advertising growing \~20% indicates Amazon is still in early days of monetizing its platform - more growth there with video ads, etc. International segment turned profitable recently, which could fuel margin expansion further. So while the easy double/triple-digit growth days are over, Amazon's sales could reasonably grow high-single to low-double digits for many years due to secular shifts (cloud adoption, digital advertising, e-commerce share gains in grocery etc.). The financial history supports that - even at $469B in 2021, they grew 22% in 2022 constant-currency. So the growth "engine" is intact albeit naturally slowing.

**Efficiency Gains:** The dramatic improvement in 2023-24 margins indicates management addressed inefficiencies (they admitted overstaffing and overcapacity in 2022). The fulfillment network's regionalization (cutting cross-country shipping) likely reduced shipping cost per unit (which had jumped 20% in 2022). This efficiency shows up in 2024's 10.8% op margin vs 5.3% in 2021 at similar revenue scale. That implies Amazon has roughly doubled its operating efficiency since pre-pandemic. It also means **future profit growth could outpace revenue growth** as these efficiencies stick and AWS (higher margin) becomes larger mix.

**Investor Takeaway:** Amazon's metrics reveal a company that has emerged from an investment phase into a highly profitable phase. At the same time, its valuation acknowledges much of that potential (P/E 39 is not a value stock). However, one can argue a 39x P/E on $5.53 EPS is only \~23x if we consider a more normalized EPS (maybe excluding Rivian gain, EPS \~$4.5, then P/E \~49x - still high). But given EPS nearly tripled from 2022 to 2024, a high multiple is somewhat justified by growth.

Amazon's **stock performance** reflected these financial trends: huge run-ups into 2020, stagnation in 2021, deep drop in 2022 (when earnings collapsed), then rally in 2023-24 as earnings recovered. Looking at P/E vs growth, Amazon's PEG ratio might be \~2 (39x P/E / \~19% EPS CAGR expected next 3 yrs). That's not cheap, but for a dominant franchise with broad moats (Prime ecosystem, AWS's leading share), investors are willing to pay a premium.

**Risks visible in metrics:** One risk is AWS growth deceleration - revenue slowed to 16% in 2023 from 37% in 2019, dragging overall growth lower. If AWS growth falls to single digits, Amazon's consolidated growth could drop toward mid-single digits (given AWS \~17% of revenue in 2024). That could compress valuation. Another risk is margin pressures: labor inflation, unionization, or price wars in cloud could squeeze the gains. But Amazon showed in 2023 it can handle inflation by raising Prime fee, adding fuel surcharges, etc., preserving margins.

In conclusion, **Amazon's financial metrics show a powerhouse firing on multiple cylinders** - robust growth, rising profitability, and a fortress balance sheet. Based on these metrics, one would view Amazon as a relatively mature but still-growing tech giant, likely to continue delivering solid performance. The main question for investors is whether the current stock price already factors in most of this positive outlook. The \~40x earnings multiple suggests expectations of continued healthy growth and perhaps margin expansion. If Amazon can sustain e.g. 10%+ revenue growth and maintain 8-10% net margins, that would yield \~10-15% EPS growth, likely supporting the stock's premium valuation. If it exceeds those metrics (e.g. AI boosts AWS back to 20%+ growth, or Ads grows 25%+), there's upside. If macro or competition cause a miss (growth &lt;5% or margins erode), then the stock could correct as the metrics degrade.

Given the evidence of 2023-24, Amazon appears to have **entered a new era of high cash generation**, which could allow more shareholder-friendly actions. The first taste was the $6B buyback in 2022; with FCF now $36B and rising, management might be more open to capital returns, which could further support the stock (although Amazon historically prefers reinvestment). The financial profile now is arguably attractive to a wider range of investors (not just growth investors) - as one can see Amazon's ability to produce profits akin to a **blue-chip stalwart** while still growing like a growth company. This unique combination is reflected in its metrics and is a key reason Amazon commands a premium valuation.

### **4. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis**

**Conservative DCF (Lowest Growth):** We project Amazon's free cash flows using its lowest historical growth to get a conservative valuation. Amazon's lowest revenue growth was \~9% in 2022, and lowest FCF growth was negative in 2021-22 due to capex surge. For a conservative case, assume FCF grows only **5% annually** (below even 2022's revenue rate, to include margin of caution) for the next 5 years, then terminal growth 2%. Starting FCF = 2024's $36.2B.

-   Year1 FCF: $36.2B; Year2: $38.0B; Year3: $39.9B; Year4: $41.9B; Year5: $44.0B.

-   Terminal value at Year5 = $44.0B \* (1+0.02)/(0.10-0.02) = $561B.

Using a **15% discount rate** (Buffett's high hurdle) and no margin of safety, PV of years1-5 ≈ $150B; PV of terminal ≈ $279B; total PV ≈ **$429B**. Dividing by \~10.7B shares gives intrinsic value ≈ **$40/share**. With a **30% margin of safety**, the safe-buy price would be \~$28, far below current $223 - implying the market expects much better than this worst-case scenario.

If we instead use a more standard **10% discount**, the total PV becomes \~$486B (years1-5 $164B, terminal $322B), giving intrinsic \~**$45/share**. Still well under current price - because 5% growth assumption is extremely cautious for Amazon.

**Summary:** Under very conservative assumptions, DCF suggests Amazon is overvalued at $223 - but these assumptions likely understate Amazon's actual growth and cash generation.

**DCF with Average Growth:** Now assume Amazon can grow FCF closer to its average in the past - say **12% per year for 5 years** (which is roughly compounding mid-cycle growth given AWS, Ads might be higher and retail lower). Discount rate 10%, terminal growth 2.5%.

-   Year1 FCF: $36.2B; Year5: $36.2B \* 1.12^5 = $63.8B.

-   PV (Years 1-5) ≈ $203B; Terminal value in Year5 = $63.8B \* (1.025)/(0.10-0.025) = $871B; PV of terminal = $540B.

-   Total PV ≈ **$743B**, or about **$69/share**.

This scenario yields intrinsic value \~$69, which is still below the current price but significantly higher than the conservative case.

**DCF with Optimistic but Reasonable Growth:** Let's consider a scenario where AWS reaccelerates and retail margins keep improving - say **FCF growth 15% for 5 years** (consistent with AWS + Ads driving growth), discount 9% (given Amazon's scale, maybe investors accept slightly lower required return), and terminal 3%.

-   Year5 FCF = $36.2B \* 1.15^5 = $72.9B.

-   PV (Years1-5) \~ $221B; Terminal value at Year5 = $72.9B \*1.03/(0.09-0.03) = $1,253B; PV term = $811B.

-   Total PV ≈ **$1,032B**; per share \~**$96/share**.

Even in this fairly bullish case (15% FCF CAGR, which implies maybe revenue \~10% and some margin expansion), we get &lt;$100 value. The gap to $223 implies the market might be pricing either a longer high-growth period or lower discount (some argue 7% or 8% discount for stable big tech).

**Current Price Implied Growth:** To see what growth is implied by $223, we can iterate the DCF. Using a 8% discount (closer to cost of equity in low-rate environment), and target PV \~$2.39T (market cap), we find FCF would need to grow \~**25% annually for 5 years** and then 3% terminal to justify \~$220 share. That equates to FCF of \~$110B by 2029. Is that feasible? Possibly - if Amazon's net income continues doubling every 3-4 years. $110B FCF would require perhaps $150B net income, which might be reachable by early 2030s if AWS/Ads are huge and retail stable.

Alternatively, one can consider how far out must we project to rationalize price: If Amazon can grow FCF 10-12% for 10 years instead of 5, then the valuation gets closer to current price. (e.g., 10% growth for 10 yrs, 10% discount, yields \~$170/share by a quick calc). So effectively, the market might be looking at a 10+ year growth runway.

**Conclusion of DCF:** By typical DCF analysis using moderately high discount rates, Amazon appears richly valued - as it often has historically. The stock's value is driven by *qualitative factors (moat, innovation, optionality)* that are hard to capture in a simplistic DCF. Buffett's approach often acknowledges such factors by lowering discount rates for great companies. If we use a **7% discount** (some say in line with 30-yr yield + equity premium under current low-rate conditions), the optimistic scenario above yields near $200/share. This suggests the current price is not utterly unreasonable if one accepts a lower hurdle.

In summary, **a conservative DCF yields intrinsic value well below the current stock price** (e.g. \~$50), meaning if one demands high returns and margin of safety, Amazon is not a buy at $223. However, **adjusting for Amazon's strong competitive advantages and likely continuing growth, a more growth-oriented or lower-discount DCF can justify a substantial portion of the price**.

Therefore, investors probably view Amazon as a long-term compounder worth paying up for, expecting that as free cash flows compound, the stock will grow into its valuation. Indeed, Amazon's history shows that those who disregarded standard valuation metrics and focused on Amazon's strategic trajectory were rewarded - as the company eventually delivered huge cash flows to back up its once-rich multiples. So, while our Buffett-style DCF might flash "overvalued" if using 15% discount, many would argue Buffett's own actions (Berkshire holding at $84 average cost, not selling at $150+) show he believes in Amazon's long-term value beyond what a static DCF might say.
