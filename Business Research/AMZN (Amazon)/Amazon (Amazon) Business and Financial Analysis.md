# **Amazon (Amazon) Business and Financial Analysis**

# **Background Research**

## **Business Model**

Amazon generates revenue across retail and cloud services. In 2023 it earned \~$637.96B total , primarily from online retail (≈36%), third-party marketplace (≈22%), and Amazon Web Services (AWS, ≈14%) . Advertising and subscription services contribute smaller shares (\~7% and \~6%) . (A pie chart below illustrates this mix.) The company's main income sources are: online product sales, marketplace commissions, AWS cloud services fees, advertising, and subscription fees (Prime, AWS SaaS, etc.).

*Figure: Amazon's revenue breakdown by segment (2023) .*

In terms of scale, Amazon is massive: \~310 million active users worldwide (2025 estimate) , with about 240 million Amazon Prime subscribers globally (≈180 million in the U.S.) . Its marketplace hosts \~9 million third-party sellers (≈2 million active) . AWS serves millions of corporate customers (estimated 4.19M business accounts in 2025) . These figures underscore Amazon's huge and growing customer base.

## **Short- and Long-term Headwinds & Risks**

**Short-term factors:** Amazon faces near-term pressures from macro conditions and regulation. Modest consumer demand growth amid higher inflation/interest rates can slow retail sales. In Q1 2025, currency effects and inventory management were noted, with net sales up only 9% YoY . Potential trade tariffs and supply-chain disruptions could dent margins . The company is under ongoing antitrust scrutiny in the U.S. and EU . Increased competition from other e-commerce and retail players, and fluctuations in online advertising budgets, may temporarily damp growth .

**Long-term factors:** Structurally, Amazon must navigate regulatory and competitive shifts. Continued antitrust or "break-up" measures (e.g. requirements to spin off business units) could reshape its model . Global digital‐tax and privacy laws may raise costs or limit data use. Competition in cloud from Microsoft/Azure and Google (and evolving tech) is intensifying . Workforce pressures (e.g. higher wages from unionization or labor laws) and environmental mandates (for emissions and packaging) could permanently increase operating costs. On the other hand, Amazon's diversification (e.g. AWS, advertising, logistics) provides some hedge against any single industry downturn.

## **Cost Structure**

Amazon's largest costs are **cost of sales** (goods and shipping) and **fulfillment operations**. In 2024 cost of sales was $326.3B (≈53.0% of revenue) and fulfillment $98.5B (15.8%) . Technology/infrastructure (data centers, R&D) was \~$88.5B (14.9%) and sales/marketing \~$43.9B (7.7%) . Labor expenses (warehouse, delivery crews) are embedded in fulfillment and cost-of-sales. Marketing is relatively small. Over 2022-2024 these costs grew roughly in line with sales (e.g. cost of sales rose from $288.8B to $326.3B ). Notably, 2024 saw *lower transportation costs* offsetting some input-price inflation . In a recession or slowdown, Amazon could leverage scale and cut variable expenses (e.g. ad spending), but its fixed investments (data centers, fulfillment centers) provide high operating leverage.

-   **Cost per unit:** Not directly published; broadly, it is driven by product COGS plus shipping.

-   **Labor:** Amazon's labor bill is rising with headcount; wages per worker have increased in recent years. These are included in fulfillment costs.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** \~7-8% of sales. Amazon spends billions on Prime Video, job recruiting, etc., but has greater pricing power than many advertisers (since Amazon's ads monetize product pages).

-   **Most significant costs:** Cost of goods/shipping and fulfillment dominate .

-   **Trends:** As revenue grows, total costs rise; efficiency gains (automation, bulk shipping deals) have partially mitigated cost growth . In inflationary periods, shipping and labor costs have spiked, but recent weak oil prices and logistical scale have eased transportation costs .

## **Management Team**

Amazon's leadership is led by **Andy Jassy** (CEO since 2021, longtime AWS founder and executive), **Jeffrey P. Bezos**(Founder and Executive Chairman), **Brian Olsavsky** (CFO since 2015), along with other senior VPs (Worldwide Stores, AWS, etc.). There are 7 officers and 12 directors on Amazon's board . Key officers include Bezos (Exec Chair), Jassy (CEO), Olsavsky (SVP & CFO), Douglas Herrington (Worldwide Stores), Shelley Reynolds (VP Controller), Matt Garman (AWS Sales head), and David Zapolsky (GC) . The board also includes independent directors (e.g. Indra Nooyi, Beth Mooney, etc., per SEC filings). Several leaders are long-tenured: Jassy joined Amazon in 1997, Olsavsky in 2002, Bezos has led since founding in 1994.

**Historical execs:** Prior CEOs included founder Bezos and briefly Werner Vogels (CTO). Longtime AWS leader Jassy is now CEO. Bezos still influences strategy as Chair.

**Track record:** Management has historically prioritized growth and reinvestment over short-term profits. They often set ambitious goals (e.g. AWS growth, delivery speed) and largely delivered (AWS now \~$90B revenue). The company rarely issues formal multi-year guidance, so "keeping promises" is hard to evaluate. Amazon does generally not hide problems; their SEC filings disclose issues (e.g. inventory build-up in 2022). Management answers analysts' questions frankly (e.g. noting FX headwinds and slower retail growth in Q1 2025). Executive tenure is long (many &gt;10 years), which suggests stability. There have been few integrity scandals; most controversies involve business practices (e.g. tax minimization, pricing tactics).

**Value alignment:** Jeff Bezos owns \~8.5% of shares (≈902M shares, worth \~$200B) , making him Amazon's largest individual shareholder. Other top insiders include Vanguard, BlackRock, and State Street (institutional owners) . Executives hold substantial stock grants; e.g. Jassy's recent compensation is mostly Amazon equity, aligning his incentives with shareholders. However, filings show Amazon insiders have tended to sell into market (e.g. Bezos has sold portions routinely in 2024 ). There is no public evidence that leaders buy additional stock on dips. Bezos has sold large blocks over time (to fund Blue Origin, etc.), though remains heavily invested. Overall, leaders benefit more from Amazon's long-term growth in share value than from their annual salaries.

## **Major Shareholders & Voting**

Institutions own the majority of AMZN stock. Bezos (8.5%)("insider"), plus 62% held by institutions . The largest funds include Vanguard (\~7.8%), BlackRock (\~6.6%) . At shareholder meetings, these top owners typically vote in line with management on director elections, Say-on-Pay, and major proposals. For example, in recent proxy votes directors (including Jassy and Bezos) were re-elected with high approval from these shareholders. Key agenda items often include auditor ratification, executive compensation, and occasionally social issues (no major controversy was reported). Each top holder's stance isn't always public, but mainstream asset managers generally support Amazon's strategy. (Specific vote records are in SEC proxy filings.)

## **Share Buyback Plan**

Amazon has **modestly** repurchased stock relative to peers. In March 2022, the board authorized up to **$10 billion** for share repurchases . This replaces an earlier $5B plan (approved in 2016) under which only $2.12B had been used by 2022 . Prior to 2016, buybacks were rare (a noted $2B plan was cancelled in 2012). Thus, the current $10B program is the largest to date. Over the past decade, Amazon's buybacks were small and sporadic compared to its market cap; the new plan could allow \~$10B of annual repurchase (≈2% of market cap).

## **Value Investors (Buys/Sells)**

Some well-known investors have taken positions in AMZN. Notably, **Berkshire Hathaway** (W. Buffett) bought 10.0M shares in 2019 . That position ($2.25B initially, now worth \~$1.9B) represents only 0.09% of Amazon stock . Buffett has not sold these shares publicly as of early 2025. Little else is widely reported: other "value" funds may have minor stakes, but major sell-offs (like Michael Burry's short, or hedge funds exiting) are not public. Overall, very few legendary value investors have built (or held) large Amazon positions - perhaps due to its high valuation and tech exposure.

## **Intrinsic Value (Warren Buffett DCF)**

Using a "Buffett style" DCF (owner's earnings approach) gives very different outcomes depending on assumptions. Taking recent free cash flow (\~$38.2B in 2024) as a base, we model three scenarios:

-   **Conservative:** Assume **5% annual FCF growth** and 15% discount rate with 2% terminal growth. This yields an intrinsic value ≈$31.8/share; with a 30% margin of safety, a "buy" price ≈$22.3/share.

-   **Nominal:** **15% growth**, 12% discount, 2.5% terminal → intrinsic ≈$63.9/share; 30% MOS→ ≈$44.8.

-   **Optimistic:** **20% growth**, 10% discount, 3% terminal → intrinsic ≈$105.6/share; 30% MOS→ ≈$73.9.

In all cases, the current price (\~$219) far exceeds these values, suggesting very low expected returns. Even using 0% MOS, one must assume an extremely low discount (\~6.5%) to justify today's price with high growth rates, implying only \~6-7% expected return. (E.g. 20% FCF growth, 10-year horizon, 3% terminal growth, requires a 6.5% discount rate to reach $219). These results indicate Amazon appears highly valued relative to conservative cash-flow estimates. *Inputs used: 10.597B shares outstanding , discount 10-15%, terminal &lt;3%, MOS 30-50%.*

## **Latest Quarterly Results (Q1 2025)**

Amazon's Q1 2025 report showed continued growth: **net sales** were $155.7B, up 9% year-over-year . AWS grew \~17% to $29.3B revenue (boosting overall profits) . **Operating income** rose to $18.4B (vs $15.3B in Q1'24) . **Net income** surged \~64% to $17.1B ($1.59/share) (versus $10.4B, $0.98, in Q1'24). Trailing-12-month operating cash flow was $113.9B, but free cash flow fell to $25.9B vs $50.1B YoY (due to higher capex and inventory) . Guidance for Q2 2025 called for 7-11% sales growth (midpoint \~$161.5B) and \~$13-$17.5B operating income, reflecting exchange-rate headwinds .

## **Interpretation of Metrics**

Amazon's top-line has grown \~19-20% CAGR over the decade . Profitability has been volatile: net income turned negative in 2022 but rebounded in 2023 . ROE and margins plummeted with that loss but have since recovered. The company carries relatively low leverage (debt/equity 0.3-0.4) compared to peers. Cash reserves are ample ($86.8B by 2023 ). P/E and P/B ratios fell sharply during the 2022 earnings dip; current P/E (\~50x on 2023 EPS) and P/B (\~8x) reflect high valuation multiples. Overall, these metrics portray a rapidly growing business (especially pre-2022) with improving profitability, but a valuation that assumes continued strong growth.

## **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis**

We performed DCF valuations under conservative, nominal, and optimistic growth assumptions (as above). In all cases the calculated "intrinsic" prices are well below the current market price. This implies the expected rate of return (implied by market price) is relatively low given plausible cash-flow growth. For example, even using zero margin-of-safety, one must assume an extremely low discount rate (\~6-7%) and high growth to justify today's price, suggesting expected returns on the order of only \~6-7%. These findings reinforce that Amazon's stock is priced for high future performance; if the company only grows at its historical average (or slower), the stock may be overvalued by traditional DCF standards.
