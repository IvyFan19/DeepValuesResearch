# **Amazon Inc: Background Research and Financial Analysis**

## **Background Research**

### **Business Model Summary**

Amazon operates three main business segments - North America retail, International retail, and AWS cloud - which together generated $638.0B in FY2024. North America e-commerce sales were $387.5B (60.7% of total), International retail $142.9B (22.4%), and AWS $107.6B (16.9%) . These segment revenues reflect Amazon's core income sources: online product sales (first- and third-party), digital subscription and media services (e.g. Prime, ads), and cloud computing services (AWS). The pie chart below illustrates this breakdown. Amazon also sells devices (Kindle, Echo, etc.) and grocery through Whole Foods, but these are small relative to the above.

Amazon's customer base is enormous. It is estimated to have over *300-310 million active user accounts* globally , including **&gt;200 million Prime members** as of early 2023 . On the AWS side, Amazon reported about *4.2 million business customers* by 2025 . In terms of sales volume, U.S.-based third-party sellers alone moved \~4.5 billion items in 2023 . In short, Amazon serves hundreds of millions of consumers and millions of businesses worldwide via its online marketplace, subscription services, and cloud platform.

### **Risks and Headwinds**

**Short-term (1-2 years):** Amazon faces macroeconomic and geopolitical pressures. Slowing consumer spending due to **inflation and higher interest rates** could damp retail sales. In Q1 2025 guidance, Amazon explicitly flagged "global economic and geopolitical conditions, tariff and trade policies, customer demand (including recession fears), inflation, \[and\] interest rates" as key uncertainties . Ongoing U.S.-China trade tensions have led to raised tariffs on imports; as one analysis notes, many third-party sellers "stocked up" to avoid higher duties, but once inventory depletes they will likely raise prices . Tariff costs and foreign-exchange swings pose margin pressure. Industry-specific risks include surging logistics costs (e.g. fuel, wages), and potential inventory gluts if demand softens. On the tech side, weaker enterprise IT spending or competition in advertising platforms could slow growth.

**Political/regulatory:** Amazon's retail and cloud businesses face ongoing scrutiny. New regulations on data privacy, cross-border data flows or AI could increase costs, while antitrust or labor crackdowns (e.g. EU competition probes, U.S. worker-protection rules) could restrict operations. For example, Amazon's expansion into China and India is subject to local protectionism . Short-term, regulatory headwinds have included increased taxation proposals and calls to better police online platforms.

**Long-term (5+ years):** Structurally, Amazon confronts entrenched competitors and changing markets. Its retail business competes with Wal-Mart, Alibaba, JD.com and local e-commerce, any of which could erode market share. In cloud, AWS must fend off Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, and new entrants; intensifying competition has historically forced Amazon to raise capital spending on servers and cut prices, capping profits . Technological shifts (e.g. the advent of AI-driven platforms, Web3 paradigms, or alternative logistics models like drones) could disrupt current services. Environmental and social pressures (sustainability mandates, labor activism) may also impose new costs or constraints. Overall, Amazon's management notes that rapidly-evolving markets mean competitors "may adopt more aggressive pricing, devote greater resources to technology… any of which could reduce our sales and profits" .

### **Cost Structure**

Amazon's cost base is large but scales with business volume. The largest expense is **Cost of Sales** (product purchase costs, shipping, obsolescence), roughly 53% of 2024 revenue . Next are **Fulfillment costs** (warehousing, pick-pack-ship, transportation) at \~15-16% of sales, then **Technology/Content** (AWS data centers, R&D) \~15%, and **Sales & Marketing**\~8% . Most of these costs are semi-variable: product and shipping costs rise with sales volume, while much of infrastructure (warehouses, delivery network, servers) is fixed in capacity. For example, in 2024 Amazon noted that higher product and shipping expenses drove up cost of sales, partially offset by logistics efficiencies (favoring sea freight over air) .

Labor is a major component of fulfillment costs. Amazon has been raising wages to attract staff - as of late 2024, average hourly pay for U.S. fulfillment workers is *&gt;$29/hour* . This upward wage trend increases fixed operating costs, though Amazon manages costs via automation and flexible staffing. Marketing spend (\~7-8% of revenue) covers advertising, promotions, and Prime Day events; 2024's sales & marketing line was $43.9B (7.7% of revenue), slightly above 6.9% in 2023 . During recessions, Amazon may trim discretionary ad spend, but it generally sustains marketing to defend market share. The **most significant costs**-inventory purchases and fulfillment-increase under inflationary conditions, while tech spending (capex on servers, R&D) tends to be steadier and chosen strategically.

In summary, Amazon's variable costs (goods, shipping, commissions to third-party sellers) scale with sales volume, while fixed costs (infrastructure, R&D, debt service) require capital outlay regardless of short-term demand. Notably, Amazon tends to accelerate investing even in slower markets (e.g. building new fulfillment centers or AWS regions) to capture future growth, which can temporarily depress profits even as revenue grows .

### **Management Team**

Amazon was founded by Jeff Bezos (CEO 1994-2021). Bezos stepped down as CEO on July 5, 2021 and became Executive Chair . He remains the largest individual shareholder (owning \~9-10% of the stock) and shapes strategy as board chair. The current CEO is **Andy Jassy**, a long-time Amazon executive who ran AWS before succeeding Bezos . The senior team includes longtime veterans (e.g. CFO Brian Olsavsky since 2015) and new appointees, overseen by a board of directors with experienced industry leaders. Notable directors include Indra Nooyi (former PepsiCo CEO, Audit Chair) and Daniel Huttenlocher (MIT dean).

Amazon management is generally regarded as visionary and technically proficient. Jeff Bezos built a track record of rapid innovation and customer focus, though critics cite his aggressive growth-at-all-costs style (prioritizing reinvestment over short-term earnings). There have been no major accounting or governance scandals. In recent years Amazon's leadership has been less transparent on capital returns (no dividends and only recent modest buybacks) - indicating they emphasize long-term growth over immediate shareholder payouts. Leadership tenure is long (many execs have decades at Amazon), which suggests stability and deep company knowledge. The fact that founder Bezos and key leaders hold substantial stock aligns their interests with shareholder value, though compensation is often equity-heavy. Overall, management has shown high execution skill, though its alignment with conservative investors is mixed because Amazon has largely foregone dividends and preferred strategic investments over share buybacks historically.

### **Top Shareholders**

Institutional investors dominate Amazon's ownership. As of recent reports, **Vanguard** (\~7.8%), **BlackRock** (\~6.5%), **State Street** (\~4.0%), and Fidelity's FMR (\~3.6%) are among the largest shareholders . Other top holders include T. Rowe Price and Capital Group funds. These index and mutual fund managers generally vote in line with management recommendations. Amazon's shareholder meetings have seen routine approvals of director slates and executive comp with heavy support. Major proxy issues have included occasional ESG proposals (environment, labor) and governance reforms, but such measures have not passed; mainstream institutions typically side with Amazon's proposals. In sum, the top shareholders hold a significant block of voting power but have shown no major activist revolt - key votes (board elections, compensation) usually clear with &gt;90% support.

### **Share Buyback Plan**

Historically, Amazon has done very little share repurchasing. From 2013-2021 it spent only \~$2.1B under a $5B buyback plan (authorized in 2016) . In March 2022 Amazon announced a new $10B buyback - its first major repurchase since 2016. During 2022 it repurchased \~$6.0B (46.2M shares) under this plan . However, in 2023 and 2024 Amazon did *not* repurchase any shares, leaving about $6.1B of the 2022 authorization unspent . Thus, over the past decade Amazon's buybacks have been sporadic and modest relative to its market cap. The current plan (ending 2025) still has room for further repurchases, but so far management has placed priority on funding operations and investment rather than returning cash to shareholders.

### **Value Investor Activity**

By valuation standards, Amazon is a growth story, but a few value-oriented investors have taken positions. The most notable example is **Berkshire Hathaway**: Warren Buffett's firm built a stake of \~10.5 million AMZN shares (worth ≈$1.2B) by early 2023. Buffett later remarked he viewed it as a trading idea; Berkshire still held \~10.5M shares as of mid-2023 . This represents only \~0.1% of Amazon's shares, reflecting Buffett's caution (he prefers companies with guaranteed returns). Other "value" managers - e.g. activist Bill Ackman or small-cap fund managers - have occasionally traded Amazon around major dips (2018-2020, 2022) but none have established very large, lasting stakes. In general, Amazon's high price multiples have made it less attractive to classic value investors; when its stock has fallen sharply (e.g. late 2022) some value funds did buy, but many have moved on as the market rebounded. (For reference, Buffett's average cost was far above today's price on the shares he bought in 2019, so his position's IRR is low). Overall, Amazon tends to be held by large diversified funds rather than by deep-value-oriented hedge funds, with little consistent activist pressure.

### **Intrinsic Valuation (Buffett-Style DCF)**

A rough intrinsic-value estimate highlights Amazon's rich valuation. Using a discounted cash flow (owner-earnings) approach with a 10-12% discount rate and low (2-3%) terminal growth, we can back into an intrinsic stock price. Amazon's trailing free cash flow was about **$38.2B** in 2024 . Assuming FCF grows \~10% per year for 10 years and then 2.5% long-term, a 12% discount gives a present value of roughly $690B total (≈$63 per share, using \~10.9B shares ) - far below the current \~$190 market price. Even assuming more optimistic growth (15% per year) or a lower 10% discount, intrinsic per-share stays below \~$85. For example, a 10% discount, 15% growth model yields \~$82/share. Applying a 50% margin-of-safety (per Buffett's guidance) would target \~$30-40. By contrast, today's market cap is about $2.0T (≈$190×10.6B shares) , implying a very low expected return from current levels unless Amazon far outperforms conservative forecasts. In short, a disciplined DCF suggests Amazon is likely **overvalued** at today's price - an investor using these assumptions would need much higher growth or lower discount rates to justify $190. (Different inputs change the result, but under typical Buffett-style parameters Amazon's intrinsic value comes in well under the market price.)

### **Latest Quarterly Earnings (Q1 2025)**

On May 1, 2025, Amazon reported Q1 2025 results. Net sales were $155.7B (up 9% year-over-year; +10% after removing currency effects) . North America revenue grew 8% to $92.9B, International up 5% to $33.5B, and AWS up 17% to $29.3B . Operating income was $18.4B (vs $15.3B prior) - AWS generated about $11.5B of that profit. Net income was $17.1B ($1.59/share) versus $10.4B ($0.98) a year earlier . On a trailing-12-month basis, operating cash flow was $113.9B (+15%) while free cash flow was $25.9B (down from $50.1B) , the latter drop reflecting significant reinvestment. The company guided for Q2 2025 sales of $159-164B (≈7-11% growth) and operating income of $13-17.5B . These results confirmed that AWS remains the profit engine and that growth is solid but moderated; cash flows are strong operationally, though capital spending keeps FCF well below net income.

### **Financial Health Assessment**

Amazon's financial health appears fundamentally strong. Revenues grew consistently (10-12%/yr in 2021-24 after very high growth earlier) , and gross margins expanded (to \~49% in 2024) due to higher-margin AWS and advertising. Operating and net margins, while usually modest (\~6-10% for net profit in recent years), turned sharply positive after losses in 2014 and 2022 . Profitability metrics are robust: return on equity was \~23.7% in 2024 and ROA \~10.3% , reflecting strong earnings generation on its asset base. Trailing FCF is healthy ($38.2B in 2024) , supporting ongoing capex and some debt service.

On the balance sheet, Amazon has amassed large assets ($625B in 2024 ) funded partly by equity ($286B) and partly by debt (\~$339B long-term) . Its debt/equity (\~1.19× at end-2024 ) and debt/assets (\~54%) are relatively high for a non-financial company, but the debt is primarily low-cost (much of it term debt to fund infrastructure). Liquidity remains adequate: the current ratio is about 1.06 (up from \~0.95 in 2022-23), indicating current assets roughly match current liabilities. Thus, short-term obligations are covered, though not by a large margin.

In summary, Amazon is financially healthy: it generates massive cash flows and profits, which comfortably cover its large investments and debt obligations. The main concerns are the high leverage and relatively low liquidity buffers (especially in 2022-23), but these have eased recently. Growth and profitability trends are solid, though any future downturns or cost spikes could pressure margins. Overall, the company's strong cash-generating ability and market position mitigate its high fixed-cost base and debt load.

### **Conservative DCF Valuation**

As a check, we run a simple discounted-cash-flow using conservative assumptions. Taking 2024 FCF $38.2B , assume moderate growth (e.g. 5-7% annually for 5-10 years, then 2-3% perpetual growth) and a 12% discount rate. Even under these mild projections, the DCF yields a total equity value in the low trillions (per-share \~$40-50). For example, 5% growth for 10 years + 3% terminal at 12% discount gives $\\approx$10-year PV ≈$47B/share. This is still far below the current \~$190 share price. Thus, our conservative DCF implies Amazon is richly valued now. Said differently, to justify today's price under a DCF one must assume unrealistically high growth (or discount rates well below 10%). This aligns with the Buffett-style result above: only if Amazon sustains long-term growth far above historical norms would the intrinsic value approach the market price.
