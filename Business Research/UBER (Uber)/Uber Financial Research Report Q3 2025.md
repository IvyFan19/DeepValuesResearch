## **Latest Quarter Earnings**

Uber released its third‑quarter FY‑2025 results on 5 November 2025. Trips increased **22 %** year‑over‑year to **3.5 billion**and monthly active platform consumers (MAPCs) rose **17 %**. Gross bookings were **$49.7 billion**, up **21 %** YoY, and revenue climbed **20 %** to **$13.5 billion** . Income from operations was **$1.1 billion**, net income attributable to Uber reached **$6.6 billion**, and adjusted EBITDA was **$2.3 billion** . Net cash provided by operating activities totaled **$2.3 billion** and free cash flow was **$2.2 billion** . The company ended the quarter with **$9.1 billion** of unrestricted cash, cash equivalents and short‑term investments . For Q4 2025 management guided for gross bookings of **$52.25-$53.75 billion** and adjusted EBITDA of **$2.41-2.51 billion** .

## **Background Research**

### **Business Model and Revenue Sources**

Uber operates a two‑sided platform connecting riders with drivers, eaters with restaurants and couriers, and shippers with carriers. Its three reportable segments are **Mobility** (ride‑hailing and related services), **Delivery** (Uber Eats and grocery) and **Freight**. According to Business of Apps, mobility generated roughly **$25 billion** in revenue in 2024, delivery **$13.7 billion**, freight **$5.1 billion**, and other/advertising the remainder . Mobility therefore comprised about **57 %** of revenue in 2024, delivery **31 %**, freight roughly **10 %**, and advertising/other less than 2 %. ### **Customers, Drivers and Units**

Uber's network is vast. Business of Apps reports that the company served **156 million users** in 2024 and facilitated **11.27 billion trips** . Quarterly MAPCs grew from 19 million in Q1 2016 to **180 million** in Q2 2025 . Backlinko notes there were **8.8 million drivers and couriers** globally in Q2 2025 and over **36 million members** (primarily Uber One) who accounted for more than 40 % of gross bookings . Uber had **31,100 employees**, with 58 % located outside the U.S. .

### **Competitive Moat**

Uber's moat arises from multi‑sided network effects: passengers attract drivers and vice versa, while merchants and couriers attract diners in the delivery segment. The company benefits from a trusted brand, a globally scaled technology platform, proprietary data for routing/pricing, and cross‑selling opportunities (e.g., "Uber One" membership covers ride‑hailing and delivery). Regulators in many markets have granted ride‑hailing licenses or enacted regulatory frameworks that raise barriers to entry. However, competition remains intense with Lyft (U.S.), DiDi (China), Grab (Southeast Asia), DoorDash (delivery) and traditional taxi services.

### **Headwinds and Risks**

**Short‑term factors**: demand can be sensitive to macroeconomic conditions, fuel prices and consumer spending; delivery volume may normalise post‑COVID; freight is cyclical. Uber must continuously attract drivers and couriers; shortages or strikes temporarily raise rider wait times and incentives. Additionally, the company faces regulatory risks around the classification of drivers as independent contractors. In the 2024 Form 10‑K, Uber warns that reclassifying drivers as employees could increase cost of revenue, reduce flexibility, and harm financial performance . Competition requires ongoing promotions and marketing.

**Long‑term factors**: regulatory changes (e.g., labour laws, price controls), litigation, autonomous vehicle adoption, and evolving consumer preferences could permanently affect the business. The 10‑K lists legal challenges in jurisdictions seeking to restrict or ban ride‑hailing, and notes that increased insurance and safety costs might persist . Macroeconomic recessions could reduce discretionary travel and delivery spending. Finally, a low margin freight business may weigh on profitability.

### **Cost Structure**

Uber's cost of revenue primarily consists of driver/courier payments, insurance and credit card processing fees. The Q4 2024 earnings supplement shows the following GAAP cost categories for FY‑2024: cost of revenue **$8.9 billion**, operations & support **$2.0 billion**, sales and marketing **$2.2 billion**, research & development **$3.2 billion**, and general & administrative **$3.1 billion** . Capital expenditures were modest at $242 million . Because drivers are treated as independent contractors, labour expenses flow through cost of revenue rather than payroll. Advertising and marketing spend ($2.2 billion) is used to acquire riders and eaters. R&D spending ($3.2 billion) funds the technology platform and autonomous initiatives . Over time these costs have trended upward with revenue but as a percentage of revenue many categories (e.g., sales & marketing) have declined, demonstrating operating leverage.

### **Management Team and Governance**

Uber is led by CEO **Dara Khosrowshahi** (since 2017) with **Andrew Macdonald** as President & COO and **Prashanth Mahendra‑Rajah** as CFO. Other executives include Jill Hazelbaker (CMO & Public Affairs), Nikki Krishnamurthy (Chief People Officer), Tony West (Chief Legal Officer), Susan Anderson (Head of Delivery), Gus Fuldner (SVP Safety & Core Services), Albert Greenberg (VP Platform Engineering), Sarfraz Maredia (Head of Autonomous Mobility & Delivery) and Pradeep Parameswaran (Head of Mobility) . The board is chaired by **Ronald Sugar** and includes **Ursula Burns**, **John Thain**, **David Trujillo**, **Amanda Ginsberg**, **Robert Eckert**, **Revathi Advaithi**, **Alexander Wynaendts**, **Turqi Alnowaiser** (representing Saudi Arabian Public Investment Fund) and CEO Khosrowshahi . Founders Travis Kalanick and Garrett Camp are no longer involved in management or board positions.

Management's past performance is mixed: the team has delivered strong revenue growth, achieved positive net income in 2023-2024, and executed the first share repurchase program; however, Uber has historically missed profitability targets and faced cultural scandals during prior leadership. The current leadership appears more disciplined, emphasising profitability and free cash flow. According to the proxy statement, insiders own less than 1 % of shares and CEO compensation is mostly stock‑based. This limited insider ownership may reduce alignment with common shareholders, but large grants tie compensation to performance. There is no evidence of management hiding problems from investors; the 10‑K and earnings calls openly discuss regulatory risks and competition. Board tenure is relatively recent (most directors joined post‑2017) and there have been no significant integrity concerns.

### **Top Shareholders and Voting Trends**

Uber's ownership is dominated by institutions: **Vanguard** (8.05 %), **BlackRock** (6.69 %) and **FMR LLC** (5.93 %) . A Simply Wall St summary notes that institutional investors collectively own **83 %** of shares, insiders hold less than 1 % and the top 19 shareholders control 50 % . At the 2024 annual meeting the board recommended shareholders vote **FOR**all management proposals (election of directors, advisory say‑on‑pay, auditor ratification and officer exculpation) and **AGAINST** a stockholder proposal for an independent audit on driver health and safety . These proposals were adopted as recommended. No activist campaigns were noted. The Public Investment Fund of Saudi Arabia (via Turqi Alnowaiser) owns \~3.5 % of shares . There is little evidence of large value investors trading the stock-most buying and selling is through broad index funds.

### **Share Repurchase Program**

In February 2024 Uber announced its first ever **$7 billion share repurchase authorization** . Management indicated that buybacks would be used to offset dilution from stock‑based compensation and to return excess capital to shareholders. On 6 January 2025 the company executed a **$1.5 billion accelerated share repurchase (ASR)**, paying Bank of America $1.5 billion and receiving 18.6 million shares immediately, with the remainder to be settled based on volume‑weighted average price later in Q1 2025 . The CFO said the shares were undervalued and expected the ASR to retire more than 1 % of the market cap. This program is significant because Uber had not repurchased stock previously; historically the share count increased from \~426 million in 2017 to \~2.15 billion in 2024 .

### **Value Investor Activity**

Public filings do not indicate meaningful positions by high‑profile value investors (e.g., Berkshire Hathaway). Most ownership resides with passive index funds and institutional asset managers. Because the company only recently turned profitable, traditional value investors may still be cautious. The share buyback signals that management believes the stock is undervalued; however the presence of heavy growth investors and limited insider ownership suggests value investors have not yet embraced Uber.

### **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis**

DCF valuations were computed using the trailing twelve‑month free cash flow (FCF) of **$6.895 billion** (FY‑2024). Four scenarios assume different FCF growth rates for the next 10 years and a terminal growth rate of 2 % thereafter. We used a 10 % discount rate and the latest share count of 2.151 billion.

| **Scenario**     | **Growth assumption (yrs 1‑10)** | **Intrinsic value per share** | **30 % MoS buy price** | **Commentary**                                                                       |
|------------------|----------------------------------|-------------------------------|------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **No‑growth**    | FCF stays flat at $6.895 B       | **$37.88**                    | $26.52                 | Provides conservative floor; implies market is moderately overvalued.                |
| **Conservative** | FCF grows **10 %**p.a.           | **$79.22**                    | $55.45                 | Reflects slower growth than historical average; buy price below current stock price. |
| **Nominal**      | FCF grows **20 %**p.a.           | **$165.95**                   | $116.17                | Aligns with recent revenue/FCF CAGR; suggests significant upside if growth persists. |
| **Optimistic**   | FCF grows **30 %**p.a.           | **$340.60**                   | $238.42                | Highly bullish; requires sustained network expansion and margin gains.               |

DCF results highlight the sensitivity of valuation to growth assumptions. The stock trades around $60 per share (Nov 2025). Under a conservative scenario the intrinsic value is roughly $79 per share; applying a 30 % margin of safety yields a buy price near $55, slightly below the market. Thus the current price appears fair relative to moderate growth expectations.

## **Conclusion**

Uber has transformed from a cash‑burning startup into a profitable, cash‑generative platform. Its core ride‑hailing and delivery businesses benefit from network effects and generate strong free cash flow. However, the stock trades at a premium to its book value and has only recently achieved profitability. Deep‑value investors may view Uber as too expensive, but growth‑oriented investors could see meaningful upside if the company continues to expand and control costs. Regulatory and competitive risks remain, and prudent position sizing is essential.
