## **Business Model**

**PayPal's Business**: PayPal operates a global two-sided payments platform, connecting \~438 million consumers and merchants across \~200 markets . Its core business is **digital payment processing**, enabling online and mobile transactions via PayPal, **Venmo**, Braintree (unbranded card processing), Xoom (remittances), Zettle (point-of-sale), and other services.

**Revenue Streams**: The majority of PayPal's income comes from **transaction fees** charged to merchants and consumers. In 2024, **90.8% of revenue** was from transaction fees . This includes fees on payment volume processed (PayPal's average **take rate** was \~1.72% of TPV in 2024 ). The remaining \~9% of revenue comes from **value-added services** such as interest on customer balances, interest and fees on credit products (e.g. PayPal's BNPL and merchant loans), referral fees, and partnerships . *PayPal Revenue Breakdown (2024): \~90% transaction fees vs \~10% other services .*

**Customers and Volume**: PayPal had **434 million active accounts** at the end of 2024 (up modestly from 432M in 2023). This includes \~392M consumer accounts and \~42M merchant accounts (2023) . PayPal processed **26.3 billion transactions** in 2024, with an **annual TPV of $1.68 trillion** (nearly $1.7T, up \~10% YoY). The average payment per transaction was \~$63.8 . These figures illustrate PayPal's massive scale and network effect: millions of merchants accept PayPal, attracting hundreds of millions of consumers, and vice versa.

**Units/Subscriptions**: PayPal doesn't sell physical units or subscriptions in the traditional sense. Instead, key volume metrics are **number of transactions** and **payment volume**. In 2024, users averaged **60.6 transactions per active account per year** . Merchants often integrate PayPal as a payment option, and some use paid services like Braintree's gateway or **paid SaaS offerings** (e.g. fraud tools). But a typical small merchant's "subscription" to PayPal is just having an account (no recurring fee). Thus, the closest analog to "units sold" is the number of transactions processed (6.0 billion in Q1 2025 alone) .

**Moat and Competitive Advantages**: PayPal's moat is built on its **trusted brand, global network, and scale**. As one of the first online payment platforms (founded 1998), PayPal is **deeply embedded in e-commerce**. It benefits from a **two-sided network effect**: consumers want to use PayPal because millions of merchants accept it, and merchants accept it because hundreds of millions of shoppers use it. This yields tremendous **scale economies** (TPV &gt;$1.6T/year ) and a vast dataset to combat fraud. PayPal's **brand trust** is high - \~75% of Europeans prefer PayPal for online payments , and it's seen as a secure, reliable option (important for conversion rates). The company has **regulatory licenses** and risk management expertise in \~200 markets, which new entrants may struggle to replicate. Moreover, PayPal has expanded into adjacent services (like **Venmo** for P2P, **Honey** for shopping rewards, **BNPL**, merchant lending, etc.), making its ecosystem stickier. It continues to innovate (e.g. introducing crypto capabilities and partnering with OpenAI for AI-driven commerce ). While competition is intense, PayPal's large user base and merchant acceptance (available as a checkout method on an estimated 80% of the top 500 online retailers) provide a defensive moat.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

**Short-Term/Temporary Headwinds**: In the near term, PayPal faces macroeconomic and industry factors that could soften performance. **Consumer spending trends** are a key variable - if inflation and higher interest rates constrain discretionary spending, PayPal's payment volumes could temporarily slow. Management noted cautious consumer behavior in late 2022-2023, and **e-commerce growth** has normalized post-pandemic. **Foreign exchange** is another short-term swing factor; a strong US dollar can reduce reported international revenues. **Regulatory changes** or one-time compliance costs (like the EU's PSD2 strong-authentication rules) can also be short-term drags, as are **geopolitical events**(e.g. suspending services in conflict zones or sanctioned markets). For example, during 2022 PayPal exited Russia and faced softer cross-border e-commerce flows - those impacts were significant but not permanent. Lastly, **leadership transitions** created some uncertainty in 2023 (long-time CEO Dan Schulman retired, new CEO Alex Chriss took over), so there was short-term execution risk as the new management implemented a turnaround plan.

**Long-Term/Permanent Risks**: Looking further ahead, PayPal's biggest threats are **competitive and structural**. **Competition from Big Tech** is a serious risk: Apple Pay and Google Pay have gained ubiquity in mobile payments, potentially disintermediating PayPal, especially for in-person contactless payments . Analysts view Apple Pay's growing adoption as "*increasingly ubiquitous*" and a **"looming risk"** to PayPal's dominance in online checkout . If consumers opt to use their iPhone's native wallet or merchants favor Apple/Google's solutions, PayPal's share of checkout could erode over time. Additionally, **card networks and banks** are collaborating on faster online checkout standards (e.g. Mastercard's tokenized one-click initiative by 2030) that might reduce the advantage of PayPal's stored accounts . Fintech competitors like Block (Square), Adyen, and Stripe also permanently pressure PayPal's merchant services - these rivals often offer smoother integrations or lower pricing for certain segments.

Another structural risk is **market saturation**: with nearly 80% of U.S. adults and a large portion of European online users already on PayPal , user growth is slow. Future growth relies on higher engagement per user, which could be challenging if new payment methods (cryptocurrencies, real-time bank transfers, etc.) bypass PayPal. **Regulatory/Legal** risks remain as well: PayPal must comply with anti-money laundering, consumer protection, and data privacy laws worldwide. Any major compliance failure or data breach could have a lasting reputational impact. Also, as an intermediary handling customer funds, PayPal faces the risk of tighter regulations or caps on fees (though none imminent). Finally, **margin compression** is a long-term concern - the company's **take rate** (revenue as % of TPV) has been declining \~5% annually in recent years due to a shift toward larger merchants (who get volume discounts) and growth of Braintree (unbranded processing has lower fee rate but also lower cost). This trend could permanently pressure PayPal's profitability unless offset by cost efficiencies or new revenue streams.

## **Cost Structure**

**Fixed vs Variable Costs**: PayPal's cost of doing business includes significant variable components tied to transaction volume, as well as fixed overhead. The largest **variable cost** is **transaction expense** - the fees PayPal pays to third parties (like Visa/Mastercard networks and bank partners) when processing a payment. In 2023, PayPal's **transaction expense** was $14.4 billion , about 45% of revenue. These costs scale with TPV and funding mix. For example, funding a PayPal payment via credit card is more expensive (PayPal must pay interchange) than funding via bank or PayPal balance . Transaction expense equals \~0.94% of TPV and is essentially **cost per transaction** (or per dollar of transaction). Another variable cost is **transaction loss** (fraud & buyer protection). PayPal incurred $1.68 billion of transaction and credit losses in 2023 (about 0.11% of TPV ). This includes chargebacks, fraud, and credit losses on its small loan portfolio. These costs generally rise with volume and economic cycles (fraud can spike in recessions, etc.).

The **fixed costs** (or semi-fixed) include **personnel, operations, R&D, and marketing**. PayPal had **24,400 employees in 2024** . Major operating expense categories are *Customer support & operations* ($1.92B in 2023), *Sales & marketing*($1.81B in 2023), *Technology & development (R&D)* ($2.97B in 2023), and *General & administrative* ($2.06B in 2023) . These costs are relatively fixed in the short term - e.g. engineering salaries, customer service centers, and infrastructure investments. **Labor expenses**: With \~24k staff, assuming an average fully-loaded cost of \~$150k, personnel could be \~$3.6B (many in R&D and operations). In fact, Tech & Development plus G&A totaled \~$5.0B in 2023 , much of which is compensation.

**Advertising & Marketing**: PayPal's marketing spend was $1.81 billion in 2023 , down 20% from 2022 as the company pulled back on incentive-driven user acquisition. Marketing is one area management flexes depending on conditions - for example, in lean times (2022-23) they **cut marketing spend by \~$600 million** to protect margins. This cost is partially variable (discretionary ad campaigns, promotions, referral bonuses) but has diminishing returns, so PayPal carefully balances it.

**Most Significant Costs**: The largest cost line-item is transaction processing fees (over $14B, \~**54% of total expenses** in 2023) . This is essential "cost of revenue." After that, the biggest costs are personnel-related in R&D and operations. Notably, stock-based compensation was \~$1.3B in 2023 (a non-cash expense impacting operating costs).

**Trends Over Time**: PayPal's cost structure has evolved as it scaled. **Transaction expense** as a % of TPV has ticked up (from \~0.83% in 2021 to 0.94% in 2023 ) due to more unbranded processing and more card-funded volume in the U.S., which carry higher fees . However, **transaction loss rate** has remained low (\~0.08-0.12% of TPV) due to improvements in fraud tools. Operating expenses grew rapidly through 2019 as the company invested in expansion, then were **reined in starting 2022** under activist pressure. In economic downturns, PayPal has shown it can tighten spending - for example, in 2022-23, it reduced workforce and external vendor spend, resulting in flat operating expenses even as revenue grew modestly. **In boom times (e.g. 2020-21)**, variable costs grew with volume, but high operating leverage yielded expanding margins: gross margin peaked near 47% in 2020-21 and operating margin around 17% . In tougher periods (2022), margins contracted (gross margin fell to \~42% ) as revenue growth slowed but costs (like risk losses, and fixed R&D) persisted. Generally, PayPal's cost structure allows profitability across cycles - it remained solidly free-cash-flow positive even in slower growth years, by adjusting marketing and other expenses as needed.

## **Management & Governance**

**Current Executive Team**: PayPal underwent a leadership change in 2023. **Alex Chriss** became President & CEO in September 2023 , succeeding Dan Schulman (who led PayPal since 2015). Alex Chriss was formerly an executive at Intuit and brings expertise in product strategy. The **CFO** (and now also COO) is **Jamie Miller**, appointed in late 2023 . Miller, a former GE CFO, joined Chriss's team to drive efficiency and has already been elevated to handle day-to-day operations . Other key executives include **Gabrielle Rabinovitch** (SVP, acting CFO until Miller's arrival), **Peggy Alford**(EVP, Global Sales), **John Kim** (EVP, Product and Technology, joined in 2022), and **Vincent Belloc** (SVP, EMEA). The new management has a tech- and operations-focused bent, aligning with PayPal's turnaround goals.

The **Board of Directors** is chaired by **John J. Donahoe II** (former eBay CEO, now Nike CEO, he's been PayPal's chairman since the 2015 spinoff) . The board includes seasoned figures: **David Dorman** (former AT&T CEO) , **Jonathan Christodoro** (investor associated with Carl Icahn) , **Belinda Johnson** (former Airbnb COO) , **Peggy Alford** (also an executive, former CFO of Chan Zuckerberg Initiative) , **Gina Bianchini** (tech entrepreneur) , and new additions in 2025 like **Joy Chik** (Microsoft executive) and **Deirdre Stanley** (legal executive) to broaden expertise . This board mix brings payments, tech, and activist perspectives. It's worth noting PayPal's *"founders"* (Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, etc.) have not been involved since the early 2000s; no founder remains on the current team, but **Gary Marino** - an early PayPal/BillMeLater veteran - does serve on the board (Audit Chair) .

**Historical Management and Culture**: Dan Schulman's tenure (2015-2023) saw PayPal's user base and revenues nearly triple . Schulman was viewed as a visionary on strategy (acquiring Venmo, iZettle, Honey) but faced criticism in 2021-22 for overemphasizing user growth at the expense of profit. In early 2022, PayPal abruptly abandoned its aggressive new user targets - admitting many incentive-driven signups were inactive - which shocked investors and tanked the stock \~25% . This raised questions about whether management had been overly optimistic (or **"snatching defeat from jaws of victory,"** as fund manager Terry Smith quipped) . However, Schulman did respond by refocusing on engagement and efficiency, and he openly addressed issues on earnings calls. There's no evidence PayPal's management **hid problems**; rather, they may have been slow to recognize shifting trends (like the need to prioritize daily active use over raw account additions). PayPal's executives generally have a reputation for **straightforward communication** - for instance, on analyst Q&A, Schulman and CFO John Rainey were known for detailed answers about take rates and competition. That said, some analysts felt they **underestimated competitive threats** in commentary until activists got involved .

**Management's Credibility & Follow-through**: Historically, PayPal's management mostly hit financial targets (e.g. they consistently met or beat earnings guidance in 2015-2019). The major miss was 2021's long-term user goal, which they walked back. Following the stock's decline and Elliott Management's involvement in mid-2022, management showed discipline - exceeding their $900M cost savings commitment a year ahead of schedule and accelerating share buybacks . New CEO Alex Chriss has only a short record so far, but initial street feedback is positive ("*improved product momentum and execution under new management*" - Bernstein upgrade) . Chriss has set a turnaround plan (focusing on checkout, Venmo monetization, cost control) and early results (Q2 & Q3 2025 beat-and-raise quarters) have **increased investor optimism** .

Regarding **integrity**, there haven't been notable scandals around PayPal's leadership - no major accounting issues or ethical lapses reported. One could point to the 2022 communication missteps as a learning experience. The board did bring in Elliott's recommendations in 2022, implying management was willing to be responsive to shareholder concerns. **Tenure**: Many of PayPal's top executives pre-2023 had long tenure (Schulman \~8 years as CEO; previous CFO Rainey \~7 years). With the new CEO and CFO, the management team is largely new (average executive tenure now \~1.6 years) , while board tenure averages \~7.5 years - suggesting continuity in oversight with fresh faces in execution roles.

**Value Alignment**: Management's incentives appear moderately aligned with shareholders. Top executives receive substantial stock-based compensation and **own shares** (CEO Alex Chriss directly owns \~0.014% of shares, worth \~$9M ). While that holding is not huge relative to his pay, it's meaningful wealth tied to stock performance. Directors like Jonathan Christodoro and Wences Casares in the past personally invested, though not large stakes. One notable shareholder is **Pierre Omidyar** (eBay founder) who still owns \~70 million shares (\~7% of PayPal) , giving a long-term oriented influence - though he's not on the board, his continued stake provides stability. Executives have typically benefited more from stock price appreciation than salary. For example, Dan Schulman accumulated significant stock (selling some over time but still holding a large position when he retired). Importantly, PayPal instituted performance-based stock units for executives (tied to multi-year EPS/TSR goals), indicating pay is linked to delivering growth. Some insiders did sell shares in recent years (for instance, various executives sold stock in 2021 when prices were high), but these were often scheduled trades. When PayPal's stock plunged in 2022, we did not see notable insider buying - possibly a slight negative mark on value alignment, though many execs receive stock through grants anyway. With the current depressed valuation, management has ramped up share **buybacks** (returning capital to shareholders), a sign they view the stock as undervalued. In summary, while no PayPal executive/founder has an outsized ownership stake (unlike founder-led companies), the team is incentivized via equity and appears committed to driving shareholder value (Chriss explicitly cited focusing on **"growing the business and delivering for shareholders"** in recent calls).

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns**

**Top Shareholders**: PayPal's ownership is dominated by large institutional investors. As of mid-2025, **The Vanguard Group** is the largest shareholder (\~86 million shares, \~9% of shares outstanding) . **BlackRock, Inc.** is second (\~75 million shares, \~8% ownership) . Other top holders include State Street (\~4%), Fidelity, and Capital Group. Among individuals, **Pierre Omidyar** (eBay founder) holds about 70 million shares (\~7%) - he received these at the 2015 spin-off and has retained them, making him effectively the largest individual stakeholder. Omidyar generally votes with management and is not activist. Another notable holder was **Elliott Management**, the activist hedge fund, which disclosed a $2 billion stake (\~2%+) in 2022. Elliott worked constructively with PayPal on cost cuts and capital return, but they have since exited that position (they closed it by early 2023 at a profit, according to news reports).

Institutional holders largely vote in line with management on routine proposals. For example, at the 2023 Annual Meeting, shareholders **elected all directors with \~93-98% support** and approved executive compensation (\~78% votes for). **Key votes** in recent years have included shareholder proposals on governance and social issues. In 2023, stockholders voted on a proposal to adopt a majority voting standard for director elections - it **failed** (only \~28% support) , as management opposed it (PayPal still uses plurality voting). Other 2023 shareholder proposals on **"Provision of Services in Conflict Zones," "Reproductive Rights and Data Privacy," and "Report on Civil Liberties"** each received **low support (&lt;15%)** and were not approved . The largest institutions (Vanguard, BlackRock, etc.) typically followed proxy advisor guidance and management's recommendations, which were **against** these proposals, hence their failure. The top shareholders have not been publicly activist - for instance, Vanguard and BlackRock supported management's slate and pay in 2023 votes.

One topic where big shareholders have influence is **executive pay and share usage**. In 2022, PayPal's say-on-pay got only \~64% support (some institutions objected to a special retention grant to Schulman), which led the board to engage and adjust policies. By 2023, say-on-pay support rose above 75%, reflecting improved alignment. Top holders also supported PayPal's 2023 Equity Incentive Plan amendment (adding 34.6 million shares to the employee stock plan) - this passed, indicating they trust management's use of equity compensation.

**Shareholder Activism**: Aside from Elliott's involvement in 2022 (which was behind the scenes and resulted in no proxy fight), PayPal hasn't faced hostile activism. Icahn's earlier agitation was at eBay (which led to PayPal's spin-off in 2015). Today, with the stock down sharply from 2021 highs, some value investors have taken interest (as discussed below) but none are agitating publicly; they're generally satisfied with the new CEO's direction so far.

**Capital Returns - Buybacks**: PayPal does not pay a dividend (choosing to reinvest and buy back stock instead). The company has actively repurchased shares. In **June 2022, the board authorized a $15 billion share buyback program** . As of Dec 31, 2023, about **$10.9 billion remained available** under that program . PayPal has consistently bought back shares over the past 10 years: it repurchased \~$3.4B worth in 2021, $4.2B in 2022, and $5.0B in 2023 . These buybacks have significantly reduced the share count - from \~1.22 billion shares in 2015 to \~1.03 billion by end of 2024 (a \~16% reduction) . The chart below illustrates PayPal's outstanding shares trending down as buybacks accelerated in recent years:

*PayPal Shares Outstanding (diluted) 2015-2024. The share count has steadily declined due to repurchases, with an especially sharp drop in 2022-2024 .*

In 2022, PayPal significantly ramped buybacks (spending $4.2B) even as the stock fell - effectively returning capital at lower prices . In 2023, it spent $5.0B on buybacks at an average price of \~$67.72 . Cumulatively over 2015-2023, PayPal returned about **$15.5B via share repurchases**. This aggressive repurchase strategy is a notable shift - prior to 2018, PayPal's buybacks were more modest (e.g. \~$1B/year); but after the 2018 and 2022 authorizations, they have deployed much more. **Comparison to last 10 years**: PayPal's buyback activity in the past 3 years (over $12B total) dwarfs that of its first 5 years post-spinoff. The company's share count only gently declined 2015-2019, but has dropped faster recently . Thus, PayPal is now a significant capital returner. Investors (like Elliott) pushed for this, and the board obliged with one of the largest authorization among fintechs.

**Voting and Shareholder Proposals**: The top shareholders generally have not pushed their own proposals; however, at the 2023 meeting, some investors (likely smaller activist groups) put forward the aforementioned social issue proposals. For instance, **Proposal on Civil Liberties** (possibly related to content moderation, given PayPal had a PR issue in 2022 about a policy on misinformation) got under 10% support . The big index funds clearly voted with management to reject these, indicating they prioritize corporate focus on core business over politicized issues. On governance, some institutional investors do prefer majority voting and annual director elections; we saw \~28% support for majority voting , which likely came from certain institutional policies. Going forward, if PayPal's performance improves under new leadership, we expect top holders to remain passive. If not, another Elliott-type activist could emerge - but currently there is no indication of that, especially with the stock rebounding in late 2025 on better results.

## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

**Valuation Discipline**: PayPal's valuation has swung from very expensive to reasonable. Traditionally it traded above Warren Buffett's preferred multiples, but after the 2022 stock drop it briefly met them. Today (2025), **P/E ≈12** on forward earnings - below Buffett's ≤15× hurdle . Historically, though, PayPal had a **P/E well above 15** (e.g. \~50-65× during 2017-2020 boom). So by its own history and absolute level, the stock only recently became value-priced. **P/B ratio** is still high at \~3.5× book (even after falling from &gt;10× in 2021) , above Buffett's classic ≤1.5× criterion. PayPal has never traded near book value due to its high ROE and growth. **Margin of Safety**: Buffett seeks buying 25%+ below intrinsic value. If we estimate PayPal's intrinsic value around $100B (for example), a 25% MOS would require buying under $75B. The stock's 2022 lows (market cap \~$70B) arguably provided that margin - indeed some value-focused investors did buy then. At \~$90B now, it's closer to fair value assuming moderate growth (as we'll see in the DCF). So *as of now*, PayPal partially meets valuation discipline (P/E now &lt;15 and price \~intrinsic), but historically it did not (when it was a market darling).

**Cash Generation & Allocation**: PayPal is a cash machine. It has generated positive **Free Cash Flow every year** for the past decade . FCF margins have consistently exceeded 5% of revenue - typically in the mid-teens to 20% range (e.g. 2024 FCF margin \~21%). So it passes **FCF/Revenue &gt;5%** easily. PayPal's use of cash has also become shareholder-friendly: it doesn't pay dividends but instead repurchases shares heavily, which Buffett generally approves as long as bought below intrinsic value. Importantly, the company's **Return on Retained Earnings** appears strong. To gauge this, we note from 2015-2024 PayPal retained \~$27B of cumulative earnings (no dividends) and in the same period increased its annual net income by about $3 billion. An informal measure is Buffett's: each dollar retained yielded some market value - PayPal's market cap rose by \~$45B from spin-off to now while retaining $27B, implying \~$1.67 of value created per $1 retained (though much of that gain was early; from 2017 peak to now the value creation has been modest) . If we do a "return on retained earnings" calculation for recent years: from end of 2020 to 2024, retained earnings grew by \~$15B (from $12.4B to \~$27.3B), but net income in 2024 is roughly the same as 2020 (around $4.15B vs $4.20B) . That suggests a low incremental return recently (because gains from retained cash were offset by margin compression). However, looking forward, management expects earnings to grow again using those retained funds (e.g. via stock buybacks boosting EPS). In summary, historically PayPal had high returns on equity, but the **ROE on incremental retained earnings** in the last few years dipped below Buffett's 12% benchmark. We'd mark this criterion as *borderline* - PayPal generates ample cash, but hasn't yet shown &gt;12% returns on the new capital reinvested in 2021-2023 (due to temporary earnings dip). On allocation, though, PayPal's recent aggressive buybacks at low prices are a plus in Buffett's eyes (they reduced share count \~15% in 3 years, enhancing each remaining share's claim on earnings).

**Growth & Predictability**: Buffett loves consistent earnings growth. PayPal has grown revenues every year of its independent history , but **earnings have not risen every year**. EPS fell in 2014 (pre-spinoff), dropped slightly in 2021, and dropped sharply in 2022 . Thus it fails the "every year for 10 yrs EPS up" test. However, over the full decade, EPS did grow overall at \~16% CAGR, just with volatility. **Book value per share** compounded \~14% annually from 2015 to 2024 (from \~$6.50 to \~$21.90), but there was one year of contraction - BVPS dipped in 2022 vs 2021 . Buffett's rule of no year of book decline is technically broken. Yet, that decline was minor and due to market value adjustments on investments and buybacks above book. PayPal's *ten-year CAGR in BVPS is &gt;10%* , which meets the spirit of strong growth, but the strict checklist is not perfectly met. **Owner's Earnings** (as a proxy, FCF) have been positive every year, which is a strong sign . Are owner earnings consistently ≥10% of market cap? Not usually - at the recent price, FCF yield is \~7.5%. Only at the lows did it exceed 10%. For Buffett's ideal (OE/MC ≥10%), PayPal currently falls a bit short. Summing up this section: PayPal has grown a lot but not with the smooth consistency Buffett prefers - there have been hiccups in earnings, so it doesn't fully satisfy the "predictable upward trend" standard.

**Balance-Sheet Safety**: PayPal's balance sheet is quite conservative. It carries **low debt** - its debt-to-equity ratio is \~0.5× ( \~$10B debt vs $21B equity) , which is right at Buffett's comfort threshold (≤0.5). In fact, PayPal had no long-term debt until 2019; it issued bonds at low rates in recent years. It holds substantial **cash and short-term investments ($10.8B at end of 2024)** , which actually exceeds its debt, making it net cash positive. So by leverage, it passes **D/E &lt; 0.5**. **Interest coverage** is very high: EBIT was \~$5.33B in 2024 vs interest expense \~$382M - that's \~14× coverage, well above the ≥3× benchmark. Similarly, **Interest Exp/Operating Income** was only \~7% in 2023 , easily under 15%. **Current ratio** is around 1.3 , which is below Buffett's desired &gt;1.5. But this ratio is distorted by customer funds classified as both current asset and liability. Excluding those, PayPal's corporate current ratio is healthy (it has more than enough liquid assets to cover payables). Given PayPal's business (customers pay in and out, with funds often just passing through), a slightly lower current ratio isn't a red flag. Overall, the **balance sheet is very strong** - plenty of liquidity and low financial risk. PayPal clearly passes the debt and interest coverage tests, nearly passes current ratio (and effectively does when adjusted). No issues on this front.

**Profitability & Efficiency**: PayPal enjoys high margins and returns, though not quite at Buffett's ultra-high thresholds in every metric. **Gross Margin** for the core business has been around 40-47% historically . In 2023 it dipped under 40%, but bounced back to \~41% in 2024. Buffett might prefer businesses with ≥40% gross margin - PayPal is in that vicinity (42% in 2022, 40% in 2024 ), so it's roughly a pass. **Net Profit Margin** has averaged \~15% (and was &gt;20% in 2020) , but Buffett's bar of 20% sustained is high. PayPal only cleared 20% net margin during the 2020 pandemic boost (when volumes surged without equivalent cost rise) . Otherwise it's mid-teens. So that criterion is *partially met* at best. **ROE**: PayPal's return on equity has been strong, averaging \~15-20% in the past decade . For instance, ROE was \~19% in 2023 and is projected \~18% in 2024 (after dipping to \~12% in 2022 when earnings fell) . The 10-year average ROE is \~16%, which is above Buffett's 15% mark on average, though not every year. So we'd give this a *pass*. **ROIC** (return on invested capital) - if we define it as NOPAT/(equity+debt-cash) - PayPal's ROIC has been in the mid-teens. In high-earning years it exceeded 20%, in weaker years \~12%. Over 10 years likely \~15% average, clearing the ≥12% hurdle. **ROA**: Using GAAP assets (which include customer funds), ROA looks low (5-6%) . But on an operating asset base, it's much higher. Buffett typically wants ≥6% ROA; PayPal hovers around that if using total assets (5.1% in 2024). Arguably, it's a *borderline pass* when adjusted for its cash-heavy balance sheet. Finally, **Depreciation/Gross Profit**: PayPal's business is not asset-intensive, so depreciation is small - just \~$534M in 2024 vs $13.66B gross profit (≈3.9%) . This easily meets "≤10%" (Buffett's sign of low capital intensity).

In summary, PayPal checks many of Buffett's boxes on profitability: high gross margin (≈40+%), solid net margin (though not quite 20%), high ROE (\~16% avg), strong ROIC (\~15%+), low depreciation as % of profits, and prudent use of debt. The main areas it fell short were the strict consistency of growth and the earlier valuation multiples. Under the new leadership, if PayPal can resume steady growth and maintain these returns, it could increasingly fit Buffett's mold of a great company at a fair price.

## **DCF Analysis (Conservative, Nominal, Optimistic)**

To estimate PayPal's intrinsic value, we perform a Discounted Cash Flow analysis under three scenarios: **Conservative**, **Nominal (base case)**, and **Optimistic**. We will project PayPal's Free Cash Flows (FCF) over 10 years, then discount them to present value using a 10% discount rate (reflecting a typical equity hurdle rate Buffett might use). We also assume a long-term terminal growth rate of 2.5% for cash flows after year 10 (roughly inflation-level perpetual growth).

**Inputs**: PayPal's trailing twelve-month free cash flow is about **$5.6 billion** . Over the past decade, FCF growth has varied widely - from as high as +70% (2020) to as low as -17% (2023). The average annual FCF growth (ignoring outlier 2020) is around 15%. The worst year was 2023, when FCF fell \~-17% (due to one-time working capital impacts), and the best single-year jump was +54% (2024 rebound). We'll use **FCF Growth Rate** = *-5%* (conservative), *+10%* (nominal average), and *+20%* (optimistic) for the next few years, tapering thereafter. These are applied as follows:

-   **Conservative Case**: **FCF growth = -5% for 2 years** (to reflect a potential temporary decline or recession impact, given PayPal's lowest FCF growth was about -16.7%), then +0% growth (flat) for years 3-5, and +3% modest growth in years 6-10. This scenario assumes PayPal struggles to grow in the near term - perhaps due to competition or macro weakness - and only resumes tiny growth later. We use -5% as the lowest observed sustained rate (worse than 2023's -17% one-off, but for two years to be conservative).

-   **Nominal Case**: **FCF growth = +10% per year for years 1-5**, then +5% for years 6-10. This reflects the approximate average growth rate of PayPal's FCF historically (\~15%, but we choose 10% to be cautious as an "expected" scenario). It assumes PayPal achieves moderate growth via increased transaction volumes and some margin improvement but not heroic assumptions. 10% is also roughly in line with analysts' medium-term EPS growth forecasts. We taper to 5% in later years as the company matures.

-   **Optimistic Case**: **FCF growth = +20% for 5 years**, then 10% for years 6-10. This represents a strong re-acceleration scenario - perhaps PayPal successfully monetizes new services (like advanced checkout with AI, Venmo monetization, etc.) and expands margins. 20% growth is near the high end of PayPal's historical range (e.g. 2015-2019 FCF CAGR was close to 20%). We temper it by slowing to 10% in years 6-10 as competition eventually caps high growth.

**Calculations**: For each case, we start with $5.6B FCF. We grow it annually by the scenario rate to year 10, then compute a terminal value at year 10 using the formula FCF\_year10 \* (1+g) / (r - g), where r=10% and g=2.5%. All interim cash flows and the terminal value are present-valued to today at 10%.

-   *Conservative*: Year 1 FCF = $5.6B \* (0.95) = $5.32B (5% drop), Year 2 = $5.06B (another 5% drop), Years 3-5 hold around $5.06B (0% growth), then years 6-10 grow \~3% annually to \~$5.87B by year 10. Terminal value at end of year 10 = $5.87B \* (1.025) / (0.10 - 0.025) = \~$80.1B. The sum of discounted FCFs (years1-10) plus discounted terminal value = **≈ $70 billion**. This would be the *intrinsic equity value (present value)* in the conservative scenario. Dividing by \~0.96B shares (assuming further buybacks) yields **Intrinsic Value/Share ≈ $73**. With a 25% margin of safety, a **"buy price"** would be **$55** or below. (Interestingly, PayPal traded in the mid-$50s at its lows in 2022-2023, which aligns with this case's MOS target).

-   *Nominal*: Year 1 FCF = $6.16B (+10%), Year 2 = $6.78B, Year 3 = $7.46B, Year 4 = $8.21B, Year 5 = $9.03B (that's the 5-year 10% growth path). Then slower 5% growth: Year 6 $9.49B, up to Year 10 $11.54B. Terminal value at 10 = $11.54B *1.025/(0.10-0.025) = \~$158.3B. Discounting everything at 10%: the PV of years 1-10 FCF is around $53B, PV of terminal $61B, totaling \*\*$114 billion*\* equity value. That implies **Intrinsic Value/Share ≈ $118**. A 25% margin of safety off $118 gives a **buy price ≈ $88**. (Note: PayPal's stock is \~$70 as of Oct 2025, which is about 40% below this base intrinsic value - suggesting it may be undervalued if it achieves the nominal case growth).

-   *Optimistic*: Year 1 FCF = $6.72B (+20%), Year 5 = $13.93B (compounded 20% for 5 years), Year 10 = $13.93B \* (1.10^5) ≈ $22.4B (with the growth slowing to 10% in second half). Terminal value = $22.4B \*1.025/(0.10-0.025) ≈ $306B. PV of first 10 years CF ≈ $93B, PV of terminal ≈ $118B, sum = **\~$211 billion** equity value. That equates to **Intrinsic Value/Share \~ $220**. 25% MOS buy price would be **$165**. (This scenario basically revisits PayPal's peak valuation region - indeed the stock traded above $300/share when such high growth was anticipated in 2021. If PayPal could sustain \~15%+ FCF growth for a decade, a $200+ intrinsic value is conceivable, but that's clearly the bullish case.)

**Results Summary**: Our DCF yields an intrinsic value range of roughly **$70B ($73/share)** in the conservative case to **$211B ($220/share)** in the optimistic case. The base case is around **$114B (≈$118/share)**. The current market capitalization (\~$70B at \~$72/share) is **below** even the base-case intrinsic value - implying a margin of safety of \~40% if one believes the nominal scenario. In other words, at present prices the market seems to be pricing in something closer to the conservative scenario. For a value investor, the **Conservative intrinsic value ≈ $73/share**, so the stock at \~$72 is about fairly valued under pessimistic assumptions, whereas under more normal assumptions it looks undervalued. A prudent **buy zone with 25% MOS** would be approximately **mid-$50s** (which was indeed an attractive entry seen in late 2022).

Of course, these DCF estimates depend on achieving the growth rates. If PayPal's turnaround falters and FCF stagnates or declines, then the conservative case (or worse) would hold, and the current price would not be cheap. But if the company even hits low double-digit growth, the upside to intrinsic value is significant.
