# The $100,000 H-1B Fee: A Comprehensive Analysis of the Presidential Proclamation and its Implications for U.S. Immigration, Business, and the Economy

## I. Executive Summary

On September 19, 2025, the Trump administration initiated a seismic shift in United States immigration policy by issuing a Presidential Proclamation that imposes a new $100,000 annual fee on H-1B visas for skilled foreign workers.<sup>1</sup> This measure, set to take effect on September 21, 2025, represents one of the most dramatic and financially significant overhauls of a high-skilled immigration program in modern history. The fee applies to new H-1B petitions for individuals outside the U.S. and to current visa holders seeking re-entry after international travel, fundamentally altering the calculus for American companies seeking to hire global talent.<sup>3</sup>

The administration's stated rationale is to protect American jobs, curb perceived abuses of the H-1B program by companies hiring foreign workers at lower wages, and compel employers to invest in training the domestic workforce.<sup>1</sup> The policy is explicitly designed to render the sponsorship of entry-level or "trainee-level" foreign professionals economically unviable, thereby reserving the H-1B visa for only the most senior and highly compensated individuals.<sup>2</sup>

However, this report finds that the proclamation's structure and its implementation create a series of profound disruptions that extend far beyond its stated objectives. The immediate lack of a payment mechanism effectively institutes a *de facto* entry ban, creating widespread uncertainty. The policy's broad application, which fails to exempt critical sectors such as higher education and nonprofit research, threatens to cripple American innovation and scientific leadership.<sup>4</sup> For industries like technology and IT consulting, the financial burden is transformative, likely accelerating the offshoring of high-skilled jobs and R&D functions-the very outcome the policy ostensibly seeks to prevent.<sup>8</sup>

Furthermore, the proclamation is expected to face immediate and vigorous legal challenges questioning the constitutional authority of the executive branch to impose what amounts to a punitive tax without congressional approval.<sup>2</sup> When viewed alongside the simultaneous introduction of wealth-based "Gold Card" and "Platinum Card" visas, the H-1B fee signals a broader, more transactional philosophy of immigration-one that prioritizes direct financial payments over human capital. Ultimately, this analysis concludes that the $100,000 H-1B fee, while framed as a protectionist measure, is a blunt and legally tenuous instrument that risks inflicting long-term damage on U.S. economic competitiveness and its status as the world's premier destination for talent.

## II. The Presidential Proclamation of September 19, 2025: A Detailed Analysis

The legal instrument at the heart of this policy shift is the Presidential Proclamation titled "Restriction on Entry of Certain Nonimmigrant Workers".<sup>10</sup> Its provisions are direct and far-reaching, establishing a new financial paradigm for the H-1B program while creating significant operational ambiguity for employers and foreign nationals.

### Core Mandate: The $100,000 Payment

The central provision of the proclamation is the requirement of a $100,000 payment to the U.S. government to accompany or supplement H-1B petitions.<sup>5</sup> This payment is not a one-time charge but an

**annual fee**, payable for each year an individual holds H-1B status under a petition subject to the rule.<sup>1</sup> Given that H-1B status is granted for an initial period of up to three years and can be extended for a total of six years, the potential cost per employee could reach $600,000.<sup>6</sup> This fee is levied in addition to all pre-existing government filing fees, which typically amount to several thousand dollars per application.<sup>10</sup> The administration has framed this cost as a mechanism to ensure that only companies hiring "very sophisticated" and "impressively detailed" executives and engineers will find the program economically viable.<sup>2</sup>

### Applicability and Scope: Defining Who Is Affected

The proclamation's applicability is tied directly to the physical act of entering the United States. The fee and the associated entry restriction apply to any foreign national seeking to enter the U.S. as an H-1B worker on or after the policy's effective date.<sup>3</sup> This broad scope encompasses several key scenarios:

-   **New H-1B Petitions:** Any new H-1B petition filed for an individual who is outside the United States will be subject to the fee. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) is directed to suspend decisions on such petitions until the payment is made.<sup>3</sup>

-   **Visa Stamping:** Individuals abroad who need to apply for an H-1B visa stamp at a U.S. consulate or embassy will be unable to secure the visa unless their sponsoring employer has paid the fee.<sup>3</sup>

-   **Re-entry of Existing H-1B Holders:** Perhaps most disruptively, current H-1B visa holders who are lawfully employed in the U.S. will trigger the fee requirement for their employer if they travel internationally and seek to re-enter the country.<sup>4</sup>

Crucially, the policy does **not** apply to immigration filings for H-1B workers who remain physically present within the United States. This includes petitions for extensions of stay, amendments to employment terms, or transfers to a new employer.<sup>4</sup> This distinction creates a stark divide in the H-1B population, effectively trapping a large cohort of existing workers within U.S. borders if they or their employers wish to avoid the prohibitive new cost. The proclamation is set to remain in effect for an initial period of 12 months, after which it may be extended.<sup>11</sup>

### Implementation, Enforcement, and Unanswered Questions

The proclamation's rollout has been characterized by its speed and a notable lack of operational detail.

-   **Effective Date:** The policy is scheduled to take effect at **12:01 AM EDT on Sunday, September 21, 2025**, leaving employers and immigration attorneys with less than 48 hours to react to the announcement.<sup>2</sup>

-   **Enforcement:** DHS and the Department of State are jointly responsible for enforcement. The proclamation directs them to verify payment has been made before approving a petition or issuing a visa and to deny entry to any H-1B worker whose employer has not complied.<sup>3</sup>

-   **Implementation Ambiguity:** A critical flaw in the rollout is the complete absence of an established mechanism for employers to make the $100,000 payment.<sup>14</sup> Without a defined process, compliance is impossible. This logistical vacuum transforms the fee requirement into an immediate, if temporary,
    > *de facto* ban on all H-1B entries, a reality underscored by law firms immediately issuing travel alerts advising H-1B workers against leaving the country.<sup>14</sup>

-   **National Interest Exemption:** The proclamation includes a provision granting the Secretary of Homeland Security the discretion to exempt individuals, specific companies, or entire industries from the fee if their entry is deemed to be in the "national interest".<sup>3</sup> However, the administration has not released any guidance on the criteria for this exemption or the process for applying for it, adding another layer of profound uncertainty.

The very design of the proclamation-its sudden announcement, immediate effective date, and lack of a clear compliance pathway-suggests that its primary initial purpose may be disruption rather than orderly policy implementation. The ambiguity surrounding the payment process and the national interest waiver creates a chilling effect, paralyzing corporate decision-making and achieving the administration's goal of restricting H-1B admissions even before the first dollar is collected. This approach effectively bifurcates the H-1B workforce into a "safe" domestic group, whose global mobility is now severely curtailed, and a prohibitively expensive international group, whose entry is indefinitely stalled. This creates a "gilded cage" for existing H-1B employees, potentially increasing their dependence on a single employer and amplifying long-standing criticisms of the program's potential for indentured servitude.<sup>7</sup>

## III. Context and Precedent: The Evolution of H-1B Visa Costs

To fully appreciate the magnitude of the $100,000 annual fee, it is essential to place it in the context of the existing H-1B cost structure. Prior to the proclamation, H-1B fees, while complex and substantial, were rooted in a cost-recovery model designed to fund the operations of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). The new fee represents a radical departure from this principle, shifting from an administrative charge to a prohibitive levy.

### The Pre-Proclamation Fee Landscape (Post-April 1, 2024)

The fee structure in place immediately before the September 2025 announcement was itself the result of a recent overhaul. A Final Rule published by USCIS on January 31, 2024, adjusted most immigration filing fees for the first time since 2016, with the new rates taking effect on April 1, 2024.<sup>15</sup> The stated purpose of these increases was to allow the agency to recover its operating costs, expand humanitarian programs, and support timely processing.<sup>15</sup>

For a typical H-1B petition filed by a large employer, the key government fees included:

-   **H-1B Registration Fee:** A $10 fee per beneficiary to enter the annual H-1B lottery. USCIS had finalized an increase to $215, but deferred its implementation until after the March 2024 lottery for Fiscal Year (FY) 2025.<sup>15</sup>

-   **Form I-129, Petition for a Nonimmigrant Worker:** The base filing fee for the main H-1B petition increased by 70% from $460 to **$780**.<sup>15</sup>

-   **Asylum Program Fee:** A new **$600** fee was added to most employment-based petitions, including the Form I-129, to help fund the processing of asylum applications. A discounted rate of $300 was available for small employers and non-profits.<sup>15</sup>

-   **Fraud Prevention and Detection Fee:** A **$500** fee required for all initial H-1B petitions and for petitions involving a change of employer.<sup>21</sup>

-   **American Competitiveness and Workforce Improvement Act (ACWIA) Fee:** A fee of **$1,500** for employers with 26 or more full-time employees ($750 for smaller employers). This fee funds scholarships and training programs for U.S. workers.

-   **Public Law 114-113 Fee:** A supplemental fee of **$4,000** for companies with 50 or more employees where more than 50% of the U.S. workforce is in H-1B or L-1 status. This primarily affects large IT consulting and outsourcing firms.

-   **Premium Processing Fee (Optional):** For employers seeking expedited adjudication within 15 business days, the optional fee for Form I-907 was increased to **$2,805** effective February 26, 2024.<sup>20</sup>

Excluding optional or conditional fees, the mandatory government cost for a large employer to file an initial H-1B petition was approximately $3,390. The new proclamation adds $100,000 per year to this baseline.

## IV. Stated Rationale vs. Potential Motivations: Deconstructing the Administration's Justification

The administration has presented the $100,000 H-1B fee as a necessary corrective measure to protect the domestic labor market. However, a critical examination of this official justification, when weighed against economic data and the policy's design, suggests that its motivations may be more political than economic.

### Official Justification: Protecting the U.S. Workforce

The central theme of the administration's messaging is that the H-1B program has been systematically abused to the detriment of American workers. The text of the proclamation asserts that the program has been "deliberately exploited to replace, rather than supplement, American workers with lower-paid, lower-skilled labor".<sup>5</sup> Officials have consistently echoed this line, arguing that the policy is designed to achieve several key objectives:

-   **Curbing Abuse:** The primary stated goal is to end what the administration sees as the "overuse" of the H-1B visa, particularly by companies that hire foreign workers for entry-level or "trainee-level" positions to suppress wages.<sup>1</sup>

-   **Incentivizing Domestic Training:** By making it "not economic anymore" to sponsor foreign workers for junior roles, the administration contends that companies will be forced to "train Americans" graduating from U.S. universities instead.<sup>2</sup> Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick stated, "If you're going to train people, you're going to train Americans".<sup>6</sup>

-   **National Security:** The White House has also linked H-1B abuse to national security, arguing that the displacement of American workers disincentivizes them from pursuing careers in critical STEM fields, thereby risking long-term U.S. technological leadership.<sup>5</sup>

### Countervailing Data and Arguments

The administration's narrative of widespread wage suppression and displacement is not fully supported by independent economic research, which presents a far more nuanced picture.

-   **Wage Data:** The claim that H-1B workers are universally "lower-paid" is challenged by wage statistics. In 2021, the median wage for an H-1B worker was $108,000, more than double the median wage of $45,760 for all U.S. workers.<sup>9</sup> This suggests that, on average, the program is not being used to import cheap labor.

-   **Impact on U.S. Workers:** Multiple studies have found little to no adverse impact on U.S. workers. One analysis covering 2005 to 2018 concluded that a greater presence of H-1B workers in an occupation was associated with *lower* unemployment rates and *faster* earnings growth for college-educated American workers in that same field.<sup>24</sup>

-   **Firm-Level Effects:** Some research does lend partial support to the administration's claims, but with important caveats. A study using H-1B lottery data found that winning additional visas led to a "crowd out" effect, where H-1B hires displaced other potential workers (including other foreign nationals) rather than increasing overall firm employment. The same study found some evidence that additional H-1Bs led to lower average employee earnings while increasing firm profits.<sup>25</sup> This complicates the narrative, suggesting that while H-1B workers may benefit the firm's bottom line, the gains are not always shared broadly with the entire workforce.

### Policy as Political Signaling

The disconnect between the administration's sweeping claims and the more complex economic reality, combined with the policy's design, points toward a primarily political motivation. The proclamation is a blunt instrument. By imposing a flat $100,000 fee that applies to all H-1B positions regardless of salary, skill level, or industry, the policy fails to surgically target the "lower-skilled training positions" it claims to be its focus.<sup>6</sup> A policy genuinely aimed at filtering for skill would more likely rely on raising the mandatory prevailing wage levels-a reform the administration has also proposed but which is distinct from the fee.<sup>8</sup> A flat fee is a purely economic barrier, not a meritocratic filter. A non-profit university seeking to hire a Nobel laureate researcher from abroad would face the same $100,000 cost as a tech company hiring a mid-level programmer.

This design suggests the policy is not a finely tuned economic tool but rather a powerful piece of political messaging. As one former USCIS official described it, the move appears to be "fan service for immigration restrictionists".<sup>2</sup> It delivers a decisive, easily understood action that resonates with a political base concerned about immigration's effect on the job market. The additional claim that the generated revenue will be used to reduce national debt and taxes further frames it as a populist measure.<sup>7</sup> The primary function of the proclamation, therefore, may be to generate headlines and create a jolt of panic, achieving a political victory regardless of whether the policy ultimately survives judicial review or achieves its stated economic goals.

## V. Sector-by-Sector Impact Analysis

The proclamation's sweeping and indiscriminate application of a $100,000 annual fee will have profound and varied consequences across the U.S. economy. While some sectors may be able to adapt, others face existential challenges to their talent acquisition models.

### The Technology Sector: A Bifurcated Impact

The tech industry, a primary user of the H-1B program, will experience a sharply divided impact.

-   **Large Corporations:** Companies like Amazon, Microsoft, and Google are the largest sponsors of H-1B visas.<sup>8</sup> While they possess the financial resources to absorb the $100,000 fee for mission-critical, high-level talent, the sheer volume of their H-1B workforce means the aggregate cost will be immense. This will inevitably force a strategic reassessment of their global hiring. Rather than simply hiring more U.S. workers-who may not possess the niche skills required-these multinational firms are more likely to shift R&D centers, engineering teams, and other functions to their foreign affiliates in countries with more favorable immigration policies, such as Canada and India.<sup>8</sup> The administration's assertion that "big tech loves the idea" is sharply contradicted by the immediate criticism from some industry trade groups, who warned the move would slam the door on top talent and hurt the U.S. in the global race for AI dominance.<sup>2</sup>

-   **Startups and Small/Medium Enterprises (SMEs):** For the innovation economy's smaller players, the impact is likely to be "devastating".<sup>7</sup> Startups often rely on a small number of highly skilled foreign engineers, data scientists, and researchers to develop their core technology. A recurring annual fee of $100,000 per employee is a prohibitive, and often impossible, expense for a pre-revenue or early-stage company. This policy threatens to crush their ability to compete for global talent, effectively acting as a barrier to entry for new, innovative firms and potentially stifling the next generation of American technological breakthroughs.<sup>26</sup>

### IT Consulting and Outsourcing Firms

The business model of large IT consulting firms, particularly those based in India such as Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), and Wipro, is an explicit target of the proclamation.<sup>2</sup> These companies have historically been among the top recipients of H-1B visas, which they use to place junior and mid-level engineers on-site for U.S. client projects.<sup>6</sup> The $100,000 fee makes this model economically unsustainable. It threatens to cripple a key revenue stream for India's $250-billion IT services industry and will force a rapid pivot away from on-site staffing in the U.S. toward remote service delivery from offshore locations.<sup>27</sup>

### Higher Education and Research Institutions

One of the most significant consequences of the proclamation stems from what it omits. Universities, nonprofit research organizations, and government research labs have traditionally been classified as "cap-exempt" employers, meaning they are not subject to the annual statutory cap of 85,000 H-1B visas.<sup>32</sup> This exemption is critical for their ability to attract a global pool of faculty, post-doctoral researchers, and scientists.

The Presidential Proclamation **provides no specific exemption for these cap-exempt institutions**.<sup>4</sup> Unless they can successfully navigate the undefined process to secure a "national interest" waiver, they will be subject to the same $100,000 fee as for-profit corporations. For budget-constrained universities and research centers, this is a catastrophic development. As noted by the American Council on Education, it will severely restrict their ability to hire the best people for high-need faculty positions, especially in STEM fields.<sup>7</sup> The administration's rhetoric has focused almost exclusively on abuses by IT firms, but the broad legal text of the proclamation ensnares the very institutions that form the bedrock of American research and innovation. This represents either a monumental policy oversight or a deliberate decision to apply the fee universally, with potentially disastrous consequences for U.S. scientific and technological leadership.

### The H-1B Workforce and Prospective Applicants

For foreign professionals, the policy erects a formidable new barrier to working in the United States. The impact will be felt most acutely by the nearly 75% of H-1B recipients who come from India.<sup>1</sup> Opportunities for junior and entry-level professionals will be drastically curtailed, as few companies will be willing to incur such a high cost for less experienced talent.<sup>23</sup> The immense cost and uncertainty will likely trigger a "brain drain" away from the U.S. The world's brightest students and professionals may increasingly view the U.S. as an unstable and unwelcoming destination, opting instead for countries like Canada or in Europe that are actively courting skilled immigrants.<sup>8</sup> This could reverse a decades-long trend of the U.S. attracting the best global talent, with lasting negative effects on the nation's innovative capacity.

## VI. The Specter of Litigation: An Assessment of Executive Authority and Legal Challenges

The proclamation's legal foundation is highly precarious, and it is expected to face immediate and multifaceted legal challenges that will ultimately determine its fate. The central question is whether the President can unilaterally impose such a transformative fee without explicit authorization from Congress.

### Presidential Authority vs. Congressional Power

The core of the legal debate will revolve around the separation of powers in setting immigration policy.

-   **The Administration's Position:** The administration will likely argue that the President has broad authority to regulate the entry of non-citizens into the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The proclamation is carefully framed not as a new visa fee but as a "restriction on entry," with the $100,000 payment acting as a condition to overcome that restriction.<sup>5</sup> This phrasing is a legal tactic designed to place the action under the President's national security and foreign affairs powers.

-   **The Challengers' Position:** Opponents will argue that the power to create visa categories and set the fees associated with them is a power explicitly granted to Congress.<sup>7</sup> While executive agencies like USCIS can set fees through rulemaking, this authority is generally limited to recovering the administrative costs of providing a service.<sup>34</sup> A fee that is thousands of times greater than the processing cost and is explicitly intended to discourage use of the program and raise general revenue functions as a punitive tax, a power reserved exclusively for the legislative branch.

### Likely Avenues for Legal Challenge

Litigation against the proclamation is expected to proceed along several key legal fronts:

-   **Unconstitutional Tax:** The primary argument will be that the $100,000 payment is not a "fee" for services rendered but an unconstitutional tax. Because its purpose is to raise revenue for the U.S. Treasury and to regulate corporate behavior by making a legal activity prohibitively expensive, it falls under Congress's taxing power.

-   **Administrative Procedure Act (APA):** Lawsuits will almost certainly be filed under the APA, which governs federal rulemaking. Challengers will argue that the proclamation is "arbitrary and capricious" and an "abuse of discretion" because it is a blunt, overreaching instrument that is not rationally connected to its stated goals of targeting low-skilled workers.

-   **Violation of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA):** Opponents will contend that the proclamation directly conflicts with the existing statutory framework for the H-1B program established by Congress in the INA. They will argue that the President cannot use a proclamation to effectively rewrite a law passed by Congress.

The consensus among many legal experts is that the proclamation rests on dubious legal ground. Doug Rand, a former senior official at USCIS, characterized the action as "ludicrously lawless" and "not real policy".<sup>2</sup> This perspective suggests that the administration may be fully aware of the policy's legal vulnerabilities. The hasty rollout and lack of implementation details support the view that the proclamation's primary objective may be political rather than legal. By issuing a legally questionable order, the administration can energize its base, create a powerful chilling effect on legal immigration through the ensuing uncertainty, and force a protracted legal battle. In this scenario, the litigation itself, and the disruption it causes, becomes a central part of the policy's execution.

## VII. Concurrent Immigration Overhauls: The "Gold Card" and Broader Policy Shifts

The introduction of the $100,000 H-1B fee did not occur in a vacuum. It was announced concurrently with a set of new, wealth-based immigration pathways, and together, these actions signal a fundamental reorientation of U.S. immigration philosophy.

### The "Gold Card" and "Platinum Card" Programs

Alongside the restrictive H-1B measure, the administration unveiled two new programs designed to attract wealthy foreigners.<sup>1</sup>

-   **The Gold Card:** This program offers an expedited path to permanent residency (a Green Card) for individuals who, after passing security vetting, make a **$1 million "gift"** to the U.S. Treasury. A corporate version is available for a **$2 million** payment, allowing a company to sponsor an employee.<sup>2</sup>

-   **The Platinum Card:** This option requires a **$5 million payment** and grants the holder the ability to reside in the U.S. for up to 270 days per year without being subject to U.S. taxes on their non-U.S. income.<sup>2</sup>

The administration has made ambitious claims about the revenue these programs will generate, projecting over $100 billion from the Gold Card and $1 trillion from the Platinum Card-figures that many analysts have described as "dubious".<sup>7</sup> Like the H-1B fee, these programs are expected to face legal challenges on the grounds that only Congress can create new visa categories.<sup>7</sup>

### The Great Pivot to "Pay-to-Play" Immigration

The simultaneous announcement of these policies is not a coincidence. It represents a deliberate and coherent ideological shift. By making skills-based immigration prohibitively expensive while creating a straightforward path for wealth-based immigration, the administration is explicitly redefining what it means to be a "valuable" immigrant to the United States.

This new paradigm marks a pivot away from a system that, at least in principle, valued human capital, specialized skills, and long-term contributions to innovation and the economy. In its place is a transactional, "pay-to-play" model where the primary criterion for entry is the ability to make a large, direct financial contribution to the U.S. Treasury. This approach fundamentally changes the character of the U.S. immigration system, moving it away from its traditional image as a meritocracy that attracts global talent and toward a system that openly sells access and status. This shift has drawn criticism even from some immigration restrictionist groups, who have characterized the Gold Card program as tantamount to "selling citizenship to the highest bidder".<sup>7</sup>

## VIII. Strategic Outlook and Recommendations

The proclamation instituting the $100,000 H-1B fee creates an immediate and complex set of challenges for all stakeholders. Navigating this new landscape requires urgent action, strategic planning, and a clear understanding of the long-term risks to the U.S. economy.

### For Employers (Corporations, Universities, Hospitals)

-   **Immediate Actions:** The most urgent priority is to mitigate the risk posed by the re-entry provision. Employers should conduct an immediate audit of all H-1B employees to identify any planned international travel. All non-essential travel should be postponed indefinitely to avoid triggering the $100,000 fee upon return.<sup>4</sup>

-   **Strategic Planning:** Companies must begin budget forecasting to account for a potential $100,000 annual cost for any future hires requiring H-1B sponsorship from abroad. This necessitates a fundamental re-evaluation of global talent acquisition strategies. Companies should accelerate plans for expanding foreign subsidiaries, establishing R&D centers in more immigrant-friendly countries, and formalizing remote work policies to access global talent without needing a U.S. visa.

-   **Legal and Policy Engagement:** Organizations should immediately engage with immigration counsel and industry associations to monitor the inevitable litigation that will challenge the proclamation. Joining or supporting such lawsuits will be critical. Simultaneously, sectors like higher education, healthcare, and nonprofit research must begin aggressive advocacy efforts to secure a "national interest" exemption from the Secretary of Homeland Security.

-   **Alternative Visas:** Employers must now aggressively explore and utilize alternatives to the H-1B visa. These include the **O-1 visa** for individuals with extraordinary ability, the **L-1 visa** for intracompany transferees, and the **TN visa** for Canadian and Mexican professionals in specific fields.<sup>10</sup>

### For Foreign Nationals

-   **Current H-1B Holders:** Individuals currently in the U.S. on an H-1B visa should postpone all international travel until there is legal clarity. The priority should be to maintain continuous legal status from within the U.S. by timely filing for extensions or, if eligible, pursuing permanent residency (a Green Card).

-   **Prospective Applicants:** Those outside the U.S. hoping to secure an H-1B visa face a near-total freeze on their prospects. They should actively explore professional opportunities in other countries with more stable and welcoming skilled immigration systems or seek employment with multinational companies that can offer roles in their home country or a third country.

### Long-Term Economic and Innovation Outlook

The proclamation, if it withstands legal scrutiny and is implemented as written, poses a grave, long-term threat to American economic and technological leadership. By effectively closing the door to a significant portion of the world's skilled workforce, the policy risks starving the very industries-including artificial intelligence, biotechnology, engineering, and medical research-that are critical to future U.S. prosperity.<sup>7</sup>

For decades, the United States has maintained its competitive edge by attracting the best and brightest minds from around the globe. A significant portion of U.S. innovation, patent creation, and startup formation has been driven by immigrants.<sup>9</sup> This policy disincentivizes this vital inflow of human capital. The result will not be a simple substitution of foreign workers with American workers; rather, it will be the migration of innovation, investment, and high-value jobs to other nations that are actively courting the talent the U.S. now seeks to deter.<sup>30</sup> The final conclusion of this analysis is that the perceived short-term political benefits of the proclamation are likely to be far outweighed by the lasting damage it could inflict on the long-term economic health and innovative capacity of the United States.
