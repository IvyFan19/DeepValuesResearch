# **TSMC Financial Research Report**

## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. (TSMC) delivered robust results in its latest quarter. **Q2 2025** revenue was **NT$933.8 billion** (\~US$30.07 billion), up **38.6% year-on-year**, with net income **NT$398.3 billion** (EPS NT$15.36, \~US$2.47/ADR) up **60.7% YoY** . Gross profit margin expanded to **58.6%**, and operating margin reached **49.6%**, reflecting strong cost control; net profit margin for the quarter was **42.7%** . Management attributed the outperformance to "**continued robust AI and HPC-related demand**" driving high utilization of leading-edge 5nm/3nm capacity . In fact, shipments of 3nm chips alone contributed 24% of wafer revenue in Q2 . Sequentially, revenue rose 11.3% QoQ and net profit 10.2%, indicating accelerating growth momentum . TSMC also modestly raised its full-year outlook, expecting a mid-30%s revenue growth for 2025 and sustaining high profitability . Overall, the latest quarter showcased **TSMC's resilience and pricing power** amid an industry upcycle in AI chips, with record profit levels and upbeat guidance reinforcing investor confidence.

## **Business Model**

**Core Business & Income Sources:** TSMC pioneered the pure-play foundry model, fabricating integrated circuits exclusively for client companies. Its **sole source of income is contract chip manufacturing services** - i.e. making semiconductor wafers to customer designs at scale. TSMC does **not** market any chips under its own name (upholding a long-standing policy to never compete with customers) . Revenue streams are primarily categorized by end-market platform: **High-Performance Computing (HPC)** and **Smartphone** chips dominate. In 2024, HPC applications (e.g. processors for data centers, AI, GPUs) contributed **51%** of total revenue, while smartphone chips contributed **35%** . Smaller segments include **Internet of Things (IoT)** (\~6%), **Automotive** (\~5%), **Digital Consumer Electronics (DCE)**(\~1%), with remaining \~2% from other specialty tech and mask-making services . **Customers & Volume:** TSMC's client base is broad yet highly consolidated at the top. It serves **500+ customers worldwide** , including virtually all major fabless chip designers - e.g. **Apple, AMD, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Broadcom, MediaTek** - as well as some IDMs like **Intel** who outsource certain chips . In 2024, TSMC manufactured **11,878 different products for 522 customers** , shipping an enormous **12.9 million** (12-inch equivalent) wafers over the year . This volume was up from 12.0M in 2023, reflecting growth in demand. Notably, **Apple is TSMC's largest single customer**, accounting for \~25% of revenue in recent years (e.g. Apple's A-series and M-series chips are exclusively made by TSMC). Other top customers by revenue share have included AMD (\~10-12%), NVIDIA (\~10%), MediaTek, Qualcomm, etc. . Such clients sign multi-year agreements, ensuring high utilization of TSMC's cutting-edge fabs.

**Business Moat:** TSMC enjoys a formidable **competitive moat** built on technological leadership, manufacturing excellence, and customer trust . The company is consistently the **first to commercialize each new semiconductor node**(e.g. 7nm in 2018, 5nm in 2020, 3nm in 2022) , outspending peers on R&D (over US$4B annually) to stay 1-2 generations ahead. This **technology edge** allows TSMC to command premium pricing and high volume from chip designers seeking the smallest transistors. Secondly, TSMC's **manufacturing scale and know-how** - the "GigaFab" model - yields industry-leading yields and efficiency. Its massive fabs in Taiwan (Fab 12, 14, 15, 18, etc.) each produce &gt;100K wafers/month, driving economies of scale that competitors struggle to match. High volume also enables a virtuous cycle of learning - **manufacturing excellence** honed over decades yields superior quality and reliability . Thirdly, TSMC has earned **deep customer trust** by strictly adhering to a *no-competition, pure-play foundry policy*: since 1987, it has never designed or sold its own chips, so clients are confident sharing their IP . This neutrality, combined with a robust ecosystem of design support (the **Open Innovation Platform**), makes TSMC the preferred partner for chip companies. Finally, enormous **capital requirements** act as a moat. Building a leading-edge fab now costs $10-20+ billion and takes years; TSMC's willingness and financial strength to invest heavily in capacity (e.g. &gt;$36B CapEx in 2022) keeps it ahead while deterring new entrants. In summary, TSMC's **moat is characterized by cutting-edge technology, scale economies, impeccable execution, and a trustworthy foundry partnership model** - factors that competitors (like Samsung Foundry or Intel's fledgling foundry effort) find extremely challenging to replicate .

## **Headwinds & Risks**

Despite its strengths, TSMC faces several headwinds in both the near term and longer term:

**Short-Term / Cyclical Factors:** In the short run (1-2 years), TSMC's results can be impacted by the **cyclical nature of semiconductor demand** . For instance, during a smartphone or PC down-cycle, customers may run excess inventory and temporarily **cut wafer orders**, as seen in early 2023 when TSMC's revenue dipped \~5% YoY amid a global chip downturn . Such **inventory corrections** and consumer demand swings (e.g. due to macroeconomic slowdowns or COVID-related fluctuations) can lead to underutilization of some capacity in the short term. TSMC must also contend with **seasonality** - typically Q1 is a seasonal low and Q3 a peak due to consumer electronics launch cycles . Additionally, **foreign exchange volatility** is a short-term factor: TSMC reports in NT dollars, so a stronger TWD vs USD can modestly reduce reported revenue/profit (most sales are USD-denominated). Another current headwind is **higher input costs**(materials, utilities) amid global inflation, which could pressure margins until TSMC adjusts prices. However, TSMC's pricing power and cost management have largely preserved profitability (Q3 2023 gross margin still \~59.5% ). Finally, periodic **industry disruptions** - such as a temporary **overcapacity in older nodes**, or short-term **export restrictions** (e.g. sudden U.S. bans on certain Chinese customer orders) - could weigh on one or two quarters of revenue.

**Long-Term / Structural Risks:** Over the longer horizon, TSMC faces strategic risks that could permanently impact its business model. Foremost is **geopolitical risk** surrounding Taiwan. TSMC's operations are concentrated in Taiwan, and rising cross-strait tensions (China's claims over Taiwan) pose a tail-risk of conflict or blockade. Western governments have explicitly called TSMC's dominance a "single point of failure" for the tech supply chain . An extreme event (e.g. military action or severe sanctions) could disrupt TSMC's fabs, which would be catastrophic for the company and industry. While such worst-case scenarios are low-probability, the **geopolitical overhang** leads some investors (even Warren Buffett) to shy away despite TSMC's strong fundamentals . In response, TSMC is diversifying manufacturing with new fabs in Arizona and Japan, but these will only handle a small fraction of output by late-decade.

Another structural challenge is **technological and competitive pressure**. As chipmaking reaches atomic scales (3nm, 2nm), the cost and difficulty of advancement rise sharply. **Rival foundries** (Samsung, and potentially Intel Foundry Services) are investing heavily to catch up in advanced process technology. Samsung is introducing 3nm and targeting 2nm by \~2025-2026. If a competitor were to leapfrog TSMC technologically or offer significant capacity at comparable tech nodes, TSMC's market share (currently &gt;60% in foundry ) could erode. At present, TSMC maintains a lead, but **tech leadership is a continual race** requiring sustained capital and R&D - any execution stumble could permanently affect its dominance.

Additionally, **trade and regulatory changes** create long-term headwinds. The U.S.-China tech rivalry has led to export controls on advanced semiconductor technology to China. While TSMC is complying (e.g. not selling its most cutting-edge chips to certain Chinese firms), stricter curbs could **reduce access to the China market** (which accounted for \~10% of TSMC's sales by customer HQ in 2024 ) or limit TSMC's ability to source critical equipment (like EUV lithography machines, which currently come from ASML in the Netherlands - export restrictions here could slow TSMC's expansion). There is also **political pressure on customers to "second-source"** away from TSMC for supply chain security. For example, some U.S. chip designers might shift a portion of orders to domestic fabs (Intel or GlobalFoundries) if encouraged by government incentives, potentially trimming TSMC's long-term growth.

**Cost escalation and talent constraints** are further long-run concerns. Each new fab generation is more expensive; TSMC's aggressive capacity build-out (Arizona, Japan, Taiwan expansions) requires managing thousands of new engineers and contractors. There is a risk of **rising costs (CAPEX, labor)** outpacing the price customers are willing to pay, which could compress margins in the future. For instance, the company has noted that its Arizona fab will have considerably higher operating costs than its Taiwan fabs, due to differences in labor and construction costs. Over time, if such overseas fabs run at lower efficiency or utilization, it could drag on profitability.

In summary, **geopolitical uncertainty, emerging competition, global trade policies, and the ever-increasing cost/complexity of leading-edge manufacturing** are the key long-term risks that could fundamentally alter TSMC's growth trajectory. The company mitigates many of these through proactive strategy (geographic diversification, relentless R&D investment, deep government relationships), but they remain critical watch points for investors.

## **Cost Structure**

TSMC's cost structure reflects the capital-intensive nature of chip fabrication, with a mix of high fixed costs and variable operating expenses:

-   **Fixed Costs:** The most significant fixed cost is **depreciation of fab equipment** and facilities. Building a state-of-the-art fab (e.g. 5nm/3nm) costs tens of billions of dollars, which TSMC depreciates over several years. This depreciation is a major component of Cost of Revenue (manufacturing cost). In 2024, TSMC's depreciation and amortization was \~**NT$657 billion** (US$21B) - roughly 23% of revenue. These costs are incurred regardless of wafer output, creating high operating leverage. Another fixed cost is **R&D and engineering staff**. TSMC employs over **76,000 people** (2023) , many of them highly-skilled engineers and technicians. Core engineering salaries (and the annual \~$4-5B R&D expense) are relatively fixed in the short term; TSMC tends not to cut R&D even during downturns, to maintain technology roadmaps. For example, in 2019-2020, amid a mild industry slump, TSMC still increased R&D spending by \~+15% . **Factory overhead** (maintenance, cleanroom utilities, etc.) also falls under fixed costs - these massive fabs must run climate controls and ultra-clean environments 24/7 irrespective of output.

-   **Variable Costs:** Key variable costs include **raw materials and chemicals** (silicon wafers, gases, chemicals, photoresists), **utilities** (electricity and water usage scales with output), and some portion of **direct labor** (e.g. overtime or subcontracted workers can be adjusted with volume). While TSMC's cutting-edge processes are highly automated, there are material inputs that scale with wafer production. For instance, each wafer layer requires deposition/etch chemicals and masks - more wafers produced means higher material cost. Another semi-variable cost is **packaging and testing** (some customers have TSMC package the chips; these costs scale with volume). **Logistics** and shipping costs for delivering wafers are minor but variable.

-   **Cost per Unit:** TSMC doesn't publicly disclose exact cost per wafer, which varies by process node. However, industry estimates suggest a 300mm wafer on 5nm costs several thousand dollars to produce. Smaller nodes have higher cost per wafer due to more process steps and expensive EUV lithography. TSMC's high gross margins (\~54-59%) indicate it prices wafers well above unit cost . For example, at 5nm, a single wafer (which can hold \~600 chips of a certain size) might cost \~$16K to the customer, while cost to TSMC could be around half of that. **Economies of scale** and high yields are critical: once a fab is full, the incremental cost per wafer is lower, boosting margins. Conversely, at less than full utilization, the allocated fixed cost per wafer rises substantially.

-   **Labor Expenses:** As noted, TSMC had **76,000+ employees** as of 2023 , up from \~56,800 in 2020 . This includes thousands of R&D engineers, fab operators, and support staff. The company's labor cost was about **NT$146 billion**in 2022 (wages and benefits) according to its annual report, which is \~6.5% of revenue - relatively low due to automation and scale. TSMC benefits from Taiwan's large pool of engineers and relatively moderate wage levels (though it competes for top talent globally). It also has profit-sharing bonuses; notably, employees receive profit-sharing equivalent to \~**15%** of annual profits , aligning incentives. While headcount has grown (especially with overseas fabs), labor cost remains a smaller portion of total costs compared to depreciation and materials.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** TSMC's marketing expenditure is minimal. Being a B2B foundry with a near monopoly at the high-end, TSMC doesn't need mass-market advertising. Any marketing is mainly industry events, technical forums, and customer support - likely well under 1% of sales. For instance, the 2022 SG&A (Selling, General & Admin) expense was only **NT$71.5 bn** (\~3.2% of revenue) , which covers all admin, sales support, and customer engineering. Thus, pure "advertising" cost is negligible. TSMC's **brand reputation** comes from its technology leadership rather than marketing campaigns.

-   **Major Cost Drivers & Trends:** The **most significant costs are depreciation (fab investments)** and **capital expenditures**, followed by materials. In essence, **Capital Expenditure = future Cost of Goods**, since today's capex turns into tomorrow's depreciation expense. TSMC's CapEx has grown dramatically: e.g. US$14.9B in 2019 , ballooning to \~$30B in 2021 and \~$36B in 2022, to build new 5nm/3nm capacity. This front-loaded spending increases future fixed costs, but TSMC expects commensurate revenue growth (management targets \~20% long-term revenue CAGR) . During boom times (high demand), these fixed costs are well absorbed, yielding &gt;50% gross margins. During downcycles, utilization dips - e.g. in early 2019 or 2023 - causing gross margin to fall (it bottomed around 46% in 2019's downturn ). However, even in softer years TSMC's gross margin stayed well above 40%, indicating a relatively **resilient cost structure**. In recessionary conditions, TSMC can trim some variable costs (slower hiring, lower overtime, negotiate material pricing), but it maintains critical investments and thus accepts temporary margin compression . Conversely, in tight markets, it can raise wafer prices (as seen in 2021-22 when capacity was maxed out, prices rose \~10-20%). TSMC's *per-unit cost* tends to drop over time on each node due to yield learning and economies of scale - but when it moves to a new node (e.g. 3nm), cost/wafer initially jumps due to expensive new equipment. This results in a continuous **cycle of cost optimization**: older nodes become cheaper (thus higher margin or lower price), while new nodes start costly but gradually improve. Overall, TSMC's costs trend upward in absolute terms (due to more fabs and higher depreciation), but it has so far preserved high margins through scale and pricing power.

*(In summary, TSMC's cost structure is **high-fixed-cost, high-operating-leverage**. The company must invest heavily ahead of demand, but once capacity is utilized, incremental profits are large. This amplifies earnings in booms and, to a lesser extent, softens them in busts. TSMC's efficient cost management and discipline in capital spending help it navigate various economic conditions.)*

## **Management & Governance**

**Executive Leadership:** TSMC's leadership team blends decades of industry experience with continuity from its founding. The company's legendary founder, **Dr. Morris Chang**, led TSMC from 1987 until his retirement in 2018, establishing a strong culture and governance ethos. After Chang, TSMC was chaired by **Dr. Mark Liu** (2018-2024) alongside CEO **Dr. C.C. Wei** . In June 2024, Mark Liu retired and **Dr. C.C. Wei** (who has been CEO since 2013) assumed the role of Chairman as well, becoming the combined **Chairman and CEO** . Dr. Wei, age 70, is a long-time TSMC veteran (joined 1998) credited with steering TSMC's technology development for years; under his leadership, TSMC successfully ramped 7nm, 5nm, and began 3nm volume production. Supporting Dr. Wei is a seasoned **executive team** including: **Wendell Huang (CFO)**, **Y.P. Chin (VP of Operations)**, **Marvin Liao (R&D)**, among others - many of whom have 20+ year tenures at the company . This deep bench provides operational stability. TSMC's **Board of Directors** (10 members) is notable for its strong independent representation and global expertise . Besides Chairman C.C. Wei, the board includes a government representative (Dr. Chun-Hsien Yeh of Taiwan's National Development Fund) and **seven independent directors** such as former industry CEOs (e.g. Sir Peter Bonfield, ex-BT; Michael Splinter, ex-Applied Materials), technology leaders (e.g. Dr. Chenming Hu - renowned professor/engineer; Dr. Fei-Fei Chuang "Chuan" Lin), and business figures like **Ms. Ursula Burns** (ex-CEO of Xerox) . This diverse board brings international perspective and oversight, and it upholds high governance standards. For example, **TSMC's board is actively involved in major decisions (capex, expansions)** and maintains committees for audit, compensation, and risk.

**Historical Leadership & Culture:** TSMC's management culture is often described as **disciplined, transparent, and execution-focused**. Founder Morris Chang instilled a philosophy of focusing on long-term trust and integrity - famously declaring that TSMC would "never compete with customers" and emphasizing that **"our word is our bond"** in dealings. This ethos continues: the current board explicitly states it inherits Chang's governance philosophy, taking a *"serious and forthright approach"* with **highest integrity, financial transparency, and timely disclosure** . Indeed, TSMC has a record of honest communication: for instance, it provides **monthly revenue reports** - an unusual level of disclosure, but one that keeps investors informed of business trends (e.g. it warned of a 5% sales drop in Q1 2023 well ahead, preparing the market) . Management also does not shy away from discussing challenges. In early 2019, when smartphone demand softened, TSMC management openly acknowledged the downturn and took blame for any inventory issues rather than masking them. Similarly, in the 2020 annual report they candidly discussed macro "uncertainties" and the need to plan capacity prudently . There is **no known history of accounting scandals or major governance lapses** at TSMC - it consistently receives high corporate governance ratings in Taiwan. Executives have generally followed through on promises, such as delivering on technology roadmaps (e.g. 3nm was launched on schedule in 2022) and hitting long-term financial targets (TSMC targeted &gt;50% gross margin and &gt;25% ROE through cycles, which it has achieved ). When issues arise - e.g. a minor chip defect incident in early 2019 or a virus outbreak that temporarily halted some tools in 2018 - management promptly informed stakeholders and addressed the problems, rather than hiding them.

**Investor Interaction:** On quarterly earnings calls, TSMC's executives (often the CEO and CFO) answer analysts' questions thoroughly, though in a measured and conservative tone. They tend not to over-promise; for example, CEO Wei often provides a balanced outlook and avoids giving overly bullish forecasts even in boom times. This conservatism sometimes leads to *positive surprises* when results exceed the cautious guidance (as seen multiple times in 2020-2021). Analysts generally commend TSMC's transparency - the company provides guidance on next-quarter revenue, gross margin, and capex plans each call, and has detailed Q&A on capacity, demand by platform, etc. There is a sense that **management anticipates challenges and addresses concerns proactively**.

**Tenure and Continuity:** Many of TSMC's top managers have been with the company for decades, leading to excellent continuity. For instance, previous CEOs **Rick Tsai** and **Lora Ho** (also former CFO) were long-serving executives who mentored successors. Dr. Mark Liu (a former R&D head turned co-CEO/chair) spent over 30 years at TSMC. This continuity has provided stability through leadership transitions. Notably, when Morris Chang retired, he carefully orchestrated succession, first with a dual CEO structure (Mark Liu and C.C. Wei as co-CEOs) and then handing off to Wei fully - a plan that was clearly communicated and executed without disruption . This suggests **management depth and a robust succession pipeline**. In 2024, the latest transition (Mark Liu's retirement and C.C. Wei becoming Chairman) was likewise smooth and expected .

**Integrity and Reputation:** TSMC's management has generally not had their integrity questioned. If anything, they are sometimes criticized for being *too conservative* or *risk-averse* (for example, TSMC was initially cautious about expanding overseas, concerned about maintaining its quality outside Taiwan). But there have been no significant ethical controversies. The company's communications are viewed as credible. Even Warren Buffett, after divesting TSMC (for geopolitical reasons), remarked that TSMC is **"one of the best-managed companies"** and "**a fabulous enterprise**" - high praise for the management's capability and integrity.

**Management Incentives & Ownership:** Alignment of management's interests with shareholders appears strong. While no current executive owns a very large stake (founder Chang's family held some shares but he has donated much to charity; Mark Liu and C.C. Wei have relatively modest holdings &lt;0.1%), TSMC uses performance-based compensation. Executives and employees receive bonuses tied to profits (profit-sharing) and there are stock-based award programs. For instance, the board approved issuance of restricted stock units to employees in 2022 and 2023, aligning their rewards with company stock performance . The profit-sharing culture - a significant portion of annual pay is variable - means management is incentivized to increase **long-term shareholder value** (as profitability and thus bonuses depend on sustainable success). Importantly, **TSMC returns substantial cash to shareholders via dividends** (see next section), which benefits executives as well, since many have shares or options. Management has occasionally bought shares on market dips indirectly (through the employee ownership programs), though we haven't seen notable insider purchases or sales in public filings - likely because much of their stock compensation vests automatically.

In terms of focus on value creation, TSMC's capital allocation decisions reflect shareholder interests: the company only invests in projects likely to earn good returns (e.g. requiring &gt;\~20% IRR on new fabs), and excess cash is paid out. The **Board's oversight** also helps here - independent directors and representatives from major shareholders (e.g. the National Development Fund) have every incentive to ensure TSMC's cash is used wisely. In fact, an interesting note: as part of receiving U.S. CHIPS Act incentives for its Arizona fab, TSMC **agreed to forego share buybacks for 5 years** , directing more cash to investment - a move the board accepted because it aligns with long-term growth and government partnership, even though it limits short-term stock boosting.

Overall, **management's track record is excellent**: they consistently meet or exceed goals (both technological and financial), have not misled investors, and maintain high accountability. Their interests are largely aligned with shareholders through profit-sharing and a culture of returning cash (dividends). The current leadership under Dr. C.C. Wei is widely regarded as competent and steady, continuing the legacy of Morris Chang with the same core values. No significant concerns have been raised about the integrity or capability of TSMC's management team, which is relatively rare in an industry as volatile as semiconductors.

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns**

**Major Shareholders:** TSMC's ownership is relatively dispersed, with a mix of government stake and global institutional investors. As of early 2024, the largest individual shareholder is Taiwan's **National Development Fund (Executive Yuan)** with about **6.38%** ownership . This is effectively the Taiwanese government's holding, reflecting the strategic importance of TSMC to Taiwan's economy. The second-largest is **BlackRock**, the world's largest asset manager, with \~**5.1%** . Other top shareholders include **Capital Research & Management (Capital Group)** \~3.6%, **GIC - Singapore's sovereign wealth fund** \~3.3%, and **Norges Bank (Norway's sovereign fund)** \~1.6% . Rounding out the top 10 are major U.S. and global institutional investors like **Fidelity** (\~1.4%), **Vanguard** (\~1.3%), **New Labor Pension Fund (Taiwan)**(\~1.3%), **Yuanta Investment** (\~1.0%), and **J.P. Morgan** (\~0.8%) . Notably, **over 75% of TSMC's shares are held by foreign investors** (mainly via ADRs), while domestic Taiwanese investors (including government) hold the rest . This international ownership underscores TSMC's position as a globally followed stock.

**Shareholder Voting & Engagement:** At shareholder meetings, these large institutions typically vote in line with management's proposals, as there hasn't been activist agitation. For example, in the 2024 Annual Shareholders' Meeting, shareholders elected the new board (including several new independent directors) with broad support . Major topics voted on often include approval of employee stock compensation plans, dividend distributions, and board elections . In 2024, one proposal was to issue restricted stock awards to employees - shareholders (including the government fund and BlackRock) approved this, recognizing the need to retain talent . Another focus has been board composition: institutions generally favor TSMC's move to increase independent directors (the 2024 AGM elected 3 new independents, like former MIT president L. Rafael Reif, which was supported by institutional proxy votes) . There haven't been contentious votes on things like climate resolutions or corporate governance changes - partly because TSMC already complies with high governance standards, and partly because the National Dev Fund (with 6%) effectively aligns with management's direction, providing a stable anchor vote.

It's worth noting that **the government's role as a shareholder** is generally supportive rather than interfering - the state fund aims for TSMC's long-term success and has not pushed any agenda contrary to minority shareholders. In fact, having government stake likely insulates TSMC from certain political risks. Large foreign shareholders like BlackRock and GIC engage via dialogue: for instance, some have urged TSMC to continue improving ESG disclosure and to carefully manage overseas expansions (but these are collaborative discussions, not adversarial). According to a case study by engagement firm Hermes EOS, global investors pressed for and supported **board refreshment** in 2024 (bringing in new independent directors with diverse backgrounds) - a sign that shareholders do influence governance in positive ways. Overall, top shareholders have been **aligned with management on key votes**, focusing on ensuring TSMC's strategic investments and shareholder returns remain on track. There have been no hostile takeovers or proxy fights; all major voting items (director elections, dividends, stock splits, etc.) in the past decade passed comfortably.

**Shareholder Returns - Dividends & Buybacks:** TSMC has been a strong returner of capital, primarily through **dividends**. It began paying regular dividends in the early 2000s and shifted to **quarterly dividends** in recent years. The dividend has grown steadily: for example, the cash dividend per share was **NT$10.0 in 2019**, rising to **NT$13.0 in 2022**and **NT$17.0 in 2023** . In 2024, TSMC paid **NT$14.0 per share** (notably, 2023 had an extra special payout making it higher) , and indicated at least NT$17.50 total in 2025. Over 2004-2024, TSMC distributed **NT$3.1 trillion** (\~US$102 billion) in cumulative cash dividends - a massive return to shareholders. The current dividend yield is modest (\~1.5% at the 2025 stock price) because the stock price has appreciated, but dividend per share has consistently increased (TSMC's policy is to **increase or at least maintain the dividend each year** ). This reflects management and board commitment to sharing profits.

On **share buybacks**, TSMC has been far more restrained. Historically, it executed only a few buyback programs. The most notable was in **2008**, when TSMC bought back \~$1B of shares (500M shares) to facilitate Philips Electronics' exit from its stake - effectively helping to manage a large shareholder's divestment without hurting the stock price. TSMC also conducted small buybacks around 2011 and 2015 mainly to offset dilution from employee stock issuance . But in general, **TSMC has not regularly repurchased shares**. This is partly cultural (Morris Chang believed in reinvesting in growth and paying dividends rather than buybacks) and partly due to Taiwan regulations that historically made buybacks less flexible. More recently, as mentioned, TSMC agreed to **avoid buybacks for several years (through late 2020s) as a condition of receiving U.S. subsidies** for its Arizona fab . Instead, the company prioritizes using cash for capital expenditure and dividends.

**Comparison over 10 years:** In the past decade, TSMC's dividend per share has roughly **tripled**, whereas share buybacks have been negligible. The share count has remained fairly stable (\~25.9 billion common shares) , with slight dilution from employee awards but offset by occasional minor buybacks (for example, a tiny 0.01% reduction in 2020) . So unlike many U.S. tech firms, TSMC hasn't done huge repurchases to shrink its float or boost EPS - it hasn't needed to, given organic growth. Instead, it plows earnings into new fabs for growth and returns excess via dividends. Notably, **TSMC's dividend payout ratio** was \~50% of free cash flow on average in recent years , leaving the rest to fund expansion. This balanced approach has been well-received by shareholders, as the stock's appreciation has delivered most of the returns (TSM's price rose \~5× in the last 10 years), supplemented by a rising dividend income.

In summary, **TSMC's top shareholders are predominantly long-term institutional investors and government stakeholders, all of whom generally support the company's strategy**. There's no activist pressure, and votes have aligned with management (e.g. election of a strong, diverse board in 2024, and approval of employee incentive plans and capital budgets). The company has favored **dividends over buybacks** in returning capital; regular dividend hikes demonstrate shareholder-friendly policy, while minimal share repurchases indicate management's preference to invest in growth projects (and certain external constraints). For shareholders, the focus remains on TSMC's execution and growth, with the confidence that management will continue to share profits via dividends rather than hoard cash or excessively dilute the stock.

## **Buffett‐Style Checklist**

Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger have famously outlined criteria for a "wonderful company at a fair price." Below we evaluate TSMC against several Buffett-style yardsticks - in categories of **Valuation, Cash Generation, Growth, Balance Sheet, and Profitability** - based on the 10-year history above:

**1. Valuation Discipline:**

-   **P/E ≤ 15 (vs history):** *Fail.* TSMC's trailing P/E has usually been above 15× in recent years except during market dips. Over the past decade, its average P/E was \~21× . It dipped to \~12× in 2022's trough when the stock fell , but otherwise ranged \~18-27×. For example, end-2021 P/E was \~26.9, and even a decade ago in 2013-2014 it was \~10-11× (low at that time, but since then TSMC re-rated higher). So currently (\~30× as of Oct 2025) TSMC trades well above the classic ≤15 threshold. Buffett would likely consider this **overvalued on earnings** relative to his ideal, unless one assumes extraordinary future growth.

-   **P/B ≤ 1.5:** *Fail.* TSMC consistently trades at a much higher Price-to-Book. Its P/B was \~4-5× during 2015-2018 and rose to \~7-8× during the 2020-21 boom . Even at the 2022 low, P/B bottomed around 4×. Currently it's about 7.4× book . This is far above Buffett's rule of thumb (which comes from Ben Graham's era). However, high P/B can be justified by high ROE - and indeed TSMC's ROE (\~25-30%) warrants a premium. But strictly speaking, it fails the ≤1.5 test by a wide margin.

-   **Margin of Safety (25% below intrinsic):** *Probably Fail.* Buffett seeks to pay significantly below a conservative intrinsic value. We perform a simple intrinsic value estimate via DCF in the next section; our "Nominal" scenario (average growth case) yielded an intrinsic value around $176 per ADR, implying a buy target \~&lt;$132 (with 25% MOS). TSMC's current market price near $300 is far above that【59†】. Only under a very optimistic growth assumption does the intrinsic value exceed the current price. In fact, Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway **did buy TSMC in late 2022** when the price was around $68-$72 (perhaps seeing intrinsic value well above that) , but then sold by early 2023 when it rose to \~$74-$80 - indicating even Buffett might have estimated intrinsic in roughly the $90-100 range and took the quick profit due to geopolitical worries . At $300, TSMC is trading with little margin of safety relative to reasonable intrinsic valuations.

**2. Cash Generation & Allocation:**

-   **Positive Free Cash Flow (FCF) each year & FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:** *Pass.* TSMC has generated positive free cash flow every year for decades. Despite enormous capex, its operating cash flow is even larger. In the last 10 years, FCF margins ranged \~5%-30% . In weaker years like 2018 or 2021, FCF margin dipped to mid-single-digits (e.g. \~3-5% when capex peaked) , but still positive. Most years it exceeded 5%; across 2013-2022, average FCF/Revenue was \~15% (and it hit 30%+ in 2024) . So TSMC clearly generates ample cash. Moreover, it has been a good allocator: it **pays out \~70% of earnings via dividends and buybacks** cumulatively (with dividends being the main vehicle) and reinvests the rest at high returns. Buffett likes prodigious cash generators that don't squander the cash - TSMC fits this, as it hasn't made value-destructive acquisitions or diversions; it reinvests in its core business where it earns 20%+ ROIIC, and returns the remainder to shareholders.

-   **Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:** *Likely Pass.* This measure looks at how effectively retained profits are turned into additional earnings. One way to gauge is: over 2013-2023, TSMC retained roughly $70B+ (after dividends) and grew annual net income by about +$20B over that period. That's roughly a \~28% return on retained capital - very robust. A formal Buffett-style RORE calculation (increase in EPS divided by cumulative earnings retained) would yield a high number for TSMC. From 2015 to 2024, EPS rose from NT$9.97 to NT$45.25 , an increase of \~NT$35.3. In that time, about NT$110 in earnings per share was retained (cumulative). So roughly (35.3/110) = **32% return on retained earnings** (in line with TSMC's average ROE). This exceeds the 12% hurdle by a wide margin. Essentially, TSMC has **reinvested its retained earnings at very high returns**, creating tremendous shareholder value - exactly what Buffett wants to see (each $1 retained generated more than $0.32 of additional annual earnings, far above the $0.12 desired).

**3. Growth & Predictability:**

-   **Earnings per Share (EPS) positive and rising every year for 10 years:** *Fail (minor).* EPS has been positive and generally upward, but not strictly increasing every single year. TSMC had two slight EPS drops: 2019 EPS fell about 1-2% from 2018 , and 2023 EPS fell \~19% from 2022 (due to the cyclical downturn) . Otherwise, every other year saw EPS growth, often strong double-digits. But Buffett's criterion of "no down year in 10 years" is not met because of those two hiccups. The overall trend is strongly up (EPS \~NT$9.97 in 2014 to NT$45.25 in 2024 ), but the **earning stream isn't perfectly smooth**. This likely wouldn't completely eliminate TSMC from consideration (Buffett can tolerate cyclical dips for an exceptional business), but it does show that TSMC's earnings, while resilient, are subject to industry cycles.

-   **Book Value per Share 10-year CAGR ≥ 10% and no year of decline:** *Pass.* TSMC's book value per share (equity per share) compounded well above 10% annually over the last decade. For instance, book value per ADR grew from around $8 in 2014 to about $30 in 2024 (in TWD, NT$82.9 to NT$163.7 from 2015 to 2024) - a CAGR \~13-15%. There were **no years of book value contraction**; shareholder equity grew every year, even in the down-profit years (since net income was still positive and dividend payout was not 100%). This indicates consistent value creation. Buffett often uses growth in book value as a proxy for intrinsic value growth - TSMC would score well here. (Note: in capital-intensive businesses, book can grow from retained earnings that fund capex; in TSMC's case, those retained earnings have yielded high returns, validating the growth in equity).

-   **Owner's Earnings positive every year, and Owner's Earnings/Market Cap ≥ 10%:** *Partial.* "Owner's earnings" (Buffett's term) is essentially free cash flow available to owners. As discussed, TSMC's owner earnings (which we can approximate by FCF or maybe net income + depreciation - capex) have been positive each year - so that part is a *Pass*. However, **Owner's Earnings yield** (OE/Market Cap) is not ≥ 10% in most cases. Given TSMC's market cap has grown huge, the FCF yield is relatively low. For instance, at the 2022 low price, FCF yield spiked to \~4-5%, but that's still below 10%. At the current \~$1.2T NT market cap (Oct 2025), even 2024's high FCF (\~NT$870B, US$28B) is only \~3.3% of market cap. Historically the **FCF/Market Cap** ratio for TSMC was in the low single digits (2-5%) . So by that stringent measure, TSMC fails - the stock has usually been priced such that owner earnings yield is far under 10%. (Buffett ideally likes to buy when OE yield &gt; 10%, i.e. P/FCF &lt; 10; TSMC's P/FCF has been in the 20-50 range often ).

**4. Balance Sheet Safety:**

-   **Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:** *Pass.* TSMC has very low leverage. Its Debt/Equity ratio in recent years: 0.20 in 2020, rising slightly to 0.29 in 2023 , and \~0.25 in 2024 . All are well below 0.5. Even historically, TSMC carried little debt; it often had **net cash**. As of 2024, debt was around $27B vs equity $141B (D/E \~0.19). This conservative balance sheet easily meets Buffett's criterion.

-   **Interest Coverage (EBIT/Interest ≥ 3×) & Interest Exp/Op Income ≤ 15%:** *Pass.* TSMC's interest expense is negligible relative to its operating profits. In 2024, interest expense was about NT$10.5B , while operating income was NT$1.323 *trillion* - interest was &lt;0.8% of op income (coverage &gt; 125×!). Even as it issues some bonds for expansion, interest costs remain very low (TSMC carries high credit ratings AA-/Aa3 , so cheap interest rates). For all years, **Interest/EBIT** has been far under 15% - e.g. 2022 it was \~0.9%, 2023 \~1% . So TSMC's earnings could drop 90%+ and still cover interest comfortably. In short, *no debt worries at all*.

-   **Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:** *Pass.* TSMC's current ratio has been strong. At end of 2023 it was 2.33, and 2024 \~2.36 . It dipped to \~1.7 in 2020 (as current liabilities grew with capex payables) , but even that is above 1.5. Typically, TSMC holds large cash reserves, giving it a current ratio around 2 or higher. So liquidity is excellent - more than enough current assets to cover short-term liabilities, fulfilling Buffett's safety criterion.

**5. Profitability & Efficiency:**

-   **Gross Margin ≳ 40%:** *Pass.* TSMC's gross margins have always been comfortably above 40%. In fact, over the last decade GM ranged roughly 45%-59% . Even in its worst year (2019), GM was \~46% . Recent years are \~53-59%. This exceeds Buffett's benchmark (which indicates strong pricing power and cost advantage).

-   **Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:** *Pass.* TSMC's net margin has consistently been &gt;20%. It was \~30-38% most years , and peaked around 40-45% in 2022-2024. Even in "down" year 2019, net margin \~32%. So a clear pass - TSMC is one of the most profitable large manufacturers in the world on a net margin basis, indicating a durable moat.

-   **Return on Equity (ROE) ≥ 15% (10-yr avg):** *Pass.* TSMC's 10-year average ROE is around 25-30%. It has *never* been as low as 15% in the last decade . For example, ROE was \~26-30% in the mid-2010s, dipped to \~24% in 2017-2018, then jumped to \~39% in 2022 . In 2023 it came down to \~26.7% . But all above 15%. The average from 2014-2023 was \~27% - nearly double Buffett's threshold. This reflects outstanding capital efficiency.

-   **Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) ≥ 12% (10-yr avg):** *Pass.* TSMC's ROIC (or ROTC) has also been healthy. Precise ROIC depends on definitions, but stockanalysis showed ROIC (which they define similarly to ROC) around 16-21% in recent high years and \~14% in lower years . The 10-yr average ROIC is roughly \~17-18%, exceeding 12%. TSMC has managed to maintain high returns on the massive capital it reinvests, a key Buffett hallmark. Not many companies can double capex and still keep ROIC &gt;15%. TSMC did, thanks to limited competition and strong demand for leading-edge chips.

-   **Return on Assets (ROA) ≥ 6%:** *Pass.* ROA for TSMC has been well above 6% throughout the decade. It was \~13-16% during 2020-2022 , and even in slower times stayed &gt;10%. In 2023 it was \~11% . Back in mid-2010s, ROA was a bit lower (\~8-9%), but still above 6%. So it comfortably meets this criterion. High ROA, given the asset-heavy nature, again underscores TSMC's efficient asset utilization and profit generation.

-   **Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:** *Fail.* This is one area where TSMC falls short, due to its business model. Depreciation as a share of gross profit is relatively high - meaning a lot of gross profit goes into replacing equipment. In 2022, depreciation ($14.2B) was about 31% of gross profit ($45B). In 2023, that ratio worsened to \~45% (as gross profit shrank, depreciation rose) . Historically it's been nowhere near ≤10%; more like 25-40%. Buffett's standard here comes from preferring businesses with low capital reinvestment needs. TSMC is the opposite - it must continually reinvest a large chunk of its gross earnings into new fabs. This doesn't mean TSMC isn't a great business (its returns prove it is), but it does mean it's **capital-intensive**. Buffett generally favors businesses that convert a large portion of gross profit into free cash (like software or consumer goods). TSMC's heavy depreciation indicates a significant portion of current profits are essentially "spoken for" to maintain and upgrade capacity. So on this metric, TSMC fails - depreciation typically runs at 15-20% of revenue, which is far above Buffett's ideal (&lt;10% of gross profit).

**Buffett Criteria Summary:** TSMC passes many qualitative and quantitative Buffett tests - **high margins, high returns on equity/capital, consistent growth in value, strong balance sheet, and ample free cash flow**. Where it falls short is mainly on the **valuation side** (it's usually expensive relative to book or earnings, except during rare dips) and the **capital intensity** issue (low depreciation-to-profit ratio fails, as TSMC must reinvest heavily). Buffett himself recognized TSMC's exceptional management and competitive position ("best-managed and most important chip company"), but he sold early partly due to **valuation and risk concerns** . If we strictly apply the checklist:

-   **Financial strength & profitability:** clearly a **check✅** (TSMC's numbers are well above the thresholds for margins, ROE, etc., and debt is very low).

-   **Consistent earnings growth:** mostly **check✅** (only a couple minor hiccups in a decade - overall a stable upward trend, especially impressive given industry cyclicality).

-   **Effective use of retained earnings:** **check✅** (very high returns on reinvested capital, fueling future earnings).

-   **Valuation reasonable:** **no, ❌** (TSMC seldom trades at the deep value multiples Buffett prefers; today it's quite high-priced, which would likely keep Buffett away absent a big discount).

-   **Capital intensity/depreciation:** **no, ❌** (requires heavy reinvestment - Buffett might demand a larger margin of safety to compensate for this).

In conclusion, TSMC as a business is **wonderfully high-quality**, but as an investment, a Buffett-style investor would wait for a much more attractive price. In late 2022 when the stock dropped into the \~$60s (P/E \~10-12), it briefly *did* meet many valuation tests - which is when Berkshire bought . At current elevated prices, however, it fails the valuation discipline, meaning it's not a clear "Buffett buy" at this time despite its outstanding business fundamentals.

## **DCF Valuation Analysis**

To estimate TSMC's intrinsic value, we conduct a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** analysis under three scenarios: **Conservative**, **Nominal (base case)**, and **Optimistic**. We'll project future Free Cash Flows using historical growth rates as guides, then discount to present value. All figures in USD.

**Free Cash Flow (FCF) Basis:** We use TSMC's 2024 free cash flow as a starting point. In 2024, FCF was roughly **US$28 billion** (NT$870B) . This jump was partly due to an earnings surge and slightly moderated capex. Over the past 10 years, TSMC's FCF growth has been volatile - big jumps in some years and dips in others - reflecting its capex cycles. We examined the **past decade's FCF growth**:

-   *Lowest annual growth:* In 2023, FCF fell **-44.7%** (a large decline as capex peaked while profits dipped) . Excluding negatives, the slowest positive growth year was around +6.6% (trailing twelve months ending Q3 2025 vs 2024) . Thus, a conservative growth might be low single digits or zero to be safe.

-   *Average growth:* Rather than arithmetic average (which is skewed by outliers like +198% in 2024), we consider a rough CAGR. From 2014 to 2024, FCF grew from \~US$5-6B (est.) to $28B - roughly \~18% CAGR. However, part of that is catching up from low base years. A more normalized growth rate considering future scale might be in the low-teens. So we'll use \~**15%** as a "nominal" average growth estimate (TSMC's own revenue CAGR target is \~20% , but FCF can grow a bit slower if capex remains high).

-   *Highest annual growth:* 2024 saw +198% YoY FCF (coming off a weak 2023) , and 2019 had +104% (rebounding from a 2018 dip). These are unsustainably high. A better optimistic gauge might be TSMC's best multi-year run: e.g. from 2019 to 2022, FCF grew \~60% annually (0.36→0.53→0.29→0.87 NT$ trillions). But that included a trough. For a forward optimistic, we might assume FCF could grow \~25% annually for a few years while AI demand booms, then taper.

Given these, our scenarios use **FCF growth rates** as follows:

-   **Conservative Case:** **5% annual FCF growth**. This assumes very subdued expansion - perhaps accounting for potential downturns or higher capex eating into cash flow. 5% is near the lowest positive growth seen and below long-term semiconductor industry growth. It's essentially assuming TSMC's earnings plateau (in real terms) and only grow at inflation-like pace.

-   **Nominal Base Case:** **15% annual FCF growth**. This reflects the average of past performance and aligns with TSMC's plans (revenue \~20% CAGR, but FCF slightly lower due to ongoing capex). A 15% FCF growth for several years is ambitious for a company already at $90B revenue, but TSMC's AI and 3nm/2nm ramp could plausibly drive double-digit growth for a while. We'll use 15% for \~10 years as a baseline.

-   **Optimistic Case:** **25% annual FCF growth** (initially). This represents a scenario of extraordinary demand (e.g. AI/EV super-cycle) where TSMC's FCF grows at an extremely rapid clip (nearly doubling every 3 years). This was achieved in some intervals historically (e.g. FCF \~tripled from 2019 to 2024, which is \~25% CAGR). However, sustaining 25% for a full decade would make TSMC enormous (FCF \~10x larger). More realistically, we might apply \~25% for a next 5-year high-growth phase, then moderate. But for simplicity, we'll model \~25% for 10 years as an optimistic stretch, recognizing this likely overestimates the later years.

**DCF Calculations:** We'll assume a **10-year projection** plus a terminal value at year 10. We use a **discount rate of 10%**(approximate weighted cost of capital / Buffett's hurdle rate). For terminal value, assume a modest **2% perpetual growth**beyond year 10 (conservative given world GDP growth; essentially reflective of perpetual inflation-level growth).

-   **Conservative DCF (5% FCF growth):
    > **Starting FCF = $28.0B (Year 0). We grow this at 5% for 10 years. By Year 10, FCF = \~$45.6B. The sum of discounted FCFs for years 1-10 = **$208 billion**. Terminal value in Year 10 = FCF\_10 \* (1+g) / (disc - g) = $45.6B \*1.02 / (0.10-0.02) ≈ $582B. Discount that back = $582B / (1.10^10) ≈ **$224B**.
