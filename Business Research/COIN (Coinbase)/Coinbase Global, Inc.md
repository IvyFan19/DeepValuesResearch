# **Coinbase Global, Inc (COIN: NASDAQ) - Deep Financial & Strategic Analysis**

## **Background Research**

**Business Model:** Coinbase is a digital-asset exchange (crypto broker) that earns revenue primarily from two sources: transaction fees and subscription/services fees. By 2023, transaction (trading) fees accounted for roughly half of net revenues, and subscription & services (like interest on custodial assets, staking rewards, stablecoin ("USDC") interest, custody fees, and subscription services such as Coinbase One) made up the other half . In 2023 Coinbase reported \~$2.9 billion net revenue (down from \~$7.4 b in 2021 bull-market peak) split almost evenly between these streams . For example, in Q1 2025 transaction revenue was $1.3 b and subscription/services \~$0.7 b . Coinbase has over 100 million total accounts (verified customers) worldwide (about 110 million by Q4 2022 ). Key engagement metrics: Coinbase had \~7-8 million monthly transacting users (MTUs) in 2023-2024 . The company has also launched a paid subscription, Coinbase One, which by late 2024 had 600,000+ subscribers (free 7-day trial, $29.99/mo) for benefits like zero trading fees and boosted staking rewards. (Chart: *2023 Revenue by Source*-roughly 51% transaction fees vs 49% subscription/services .)

-   **Clients/Customers:** \~110 M accounts (Q4 2022) ; \~8 M MTUs (active traders) in early 2024 ; retail + institutional segments. Institutions include market makers, hedge funds, etc.

-   **Units/Subscriptions:** Primary "unit" sold = crypto trades (volume). Coinbase One subscriptions: \~600k paid members by end-2024 (plus Coinbase Prime/Custody for institutions). No other product "units" of note.

**Headwinds & Risks:** Coinbase's fortunes closely track crypto markets and regulatory/policy environment. In the **short term**, subdued crypto prices and trading volumes (often called a "crypto winter") press down transaction fees. For example, revenue fell \~60% from 2021 to 2022 as crypto prices collapsed . High interest rates and risk-off sentiment in 2022-2023 also reduced retail/institutional crypto trading. Regulatory uncertainty is a major headwind: the SEC sued Coinbase in 2023 (alleging unregistered exchange), though in Feb 2025 the SEC agreed to drop that case . Final regulatory frameworks (e.g. US and EU crypto laws, stablecoin rules, IRS taxation) remain in flux; any new restrictions on crypto trading or on Coinbase's USDC stablecoin business could hurt earnings. Industry factors: intense competition (Binance, Kraken, new DeFi platforms) could pressure fees, and rapid tech change (new blockchains, protocols) requires ongoing investment. **Long-term** risks include security (custodied assets could be hacked), continued regulatory tightening (anti-money-laundering, capital requirements), and macro risks (currency crises or tech busts impacting crypto adoption). Political shifts (US elections, China's crypto policy) could also alter the operating environment. (Earnings volatility is evident: Coinbase swung from large profits in 2021 to loss in 2022, then slim profit in 2023, underscoring cyclical risk .)

**Cost Structure:** Operating costs are largely personnel and platform expenses, with some variable transaction costs. Major expense categories (2023 vs 2021): Technology & Dev ($1.3b in '23, mainly R&D salaries and infrastructure), General & Admin ($1.0b), Sales/Marketing ($0.33b), and transaction costs ($0.42b) . The largest single bucket is **personnel-related** (including stock comp) in tech and admin. Transaction expenses are variable and include network fees, trading infrastructure costs, and staking rewards paid to customers . In bull markets, transaction expense grows with volume; in downturns it shrinks. Costs like R&D and compliance are largely fixed (tailored to scale, but salaries are ongoing). Crypto asset impairments (write-downs of held coins) and restructuring (2023 layoff costs) have been significant but episodic . For example, crypto-impairment charges were $722 m in 2022 but reversed by a small gain ($35 m) in 2023 . In 2022 Coinbase increased headcount and marketing (in bull market) then sharply cut costs (including \~20% headcount reduction) in 2023 as volumes fell, which reduced fixed costs going forward. Overall, the trend is cyclical: high revenues in booms easily cover growing costs, while in busts cost-cutting is required to stay profitable.

**Management Team:** Coinbase's leadership is founder-driven. Co-founder **Brian Armstrong** is CEO (since 2012) and holds effective control (via Class B shares) . President/COO **Emilie Choi** (former Yahoo exec) runs day-to-day operations; CFO is **Alesia Haas**. The Board includes co-founders Armstrong and Fred Ehrsam (Board Director), tech investors like Fred Wilson and Marc Andreessen (a16z), and independent directors. Management style is generally tech/crypto-centric, with Armstrong actively promoting crypto adoption (e.g. blog posts on regulation, support for Bitcoin reserve legislation). The team has been transparent in disclosures, with frequent investor letters. Founder Armstrong retains a majority of voting power (Class B super-votes) and thus steers strategy. Historically, executives have mostly met goals around platform growth and regulatory advocacy; their integrity and alignment appear strong, with no major governance scandals. Transparency has improved (e.g. publishing monthly active user stats), though some investors have voiced concerns over coinbase's valuation swings and CEO stock sales (Armstrong is subject to volume limits but can sell Class A shares once restrictions loosen).

**Top Shareholders:** As of early 2024, Brian Armstrong (and affiliates) owned \~52% of voting power (major Class B stake) ; co-founder Fred Ehrsam held \~37% of votes. Other major holders include institutional investors: Vanguard (\~7.2% of Class A) and Jane Street (\~5.1%) . No single mutual fund or "value guru" (e.g. Buffett) is known to hold a large position. In proxy votes, these insiders effectively control outcomes. Board elections (2024) were uncontested slate of directors (Armstrong, Andreessen, Ehrsam, Wilson, etc.), and all proposals (accounting firm ratification, etc.) passed with high support. Value investors have not been notably active; the stock is largely held by tech/crypto-oriented and index funds rather than classic dividend/value investors.

**Share Buybacks:** Coinbase did not buy back shares during 2021-2023. In Oct 2024, the Board authorized up to **$1 billion** in share repurchases . (This is the first equity buyback program in the company's public history; prior to that, capital was primarily used for crypto acquisitions and operations.) As of Q4 2024, no shares had yet been repurchased (0% of the $1b plan) , so we await execution. By contrast, Coinbase has repurchased debt (e.g. $64.5m of senior notes in June 2023) but those are not equity buybacks. The new buyback plan will dilute remaining shareholders less if executed, and signals management's intent to invest in the stock if shares are undervalued. Compared to more than ten years of operation, this buyback is unprecedented (since IPO in Apr 2021 was via direct listing, not traditional stock issuance) - essentially, share repurchases only become possible once public.

**Value Investors (Activity):** We find no prominent "value" investor (like a hedge fund or Buffett-style investor) publicly disclosing large COIN holdings or trades. Institutional ownership is concentrated among passive index funds and quantitative firms. Notably, hedge fund positions (13F filings) show buying/selling by broad tech funds but no easily identified name known for long-term value plays. In the proxy's 5% owners list, it's Vanguard and Jane Street (quant), not typical "value" investors. Thus, we conclude there is no major Buffett-style champion of the stock. Average institutional buy prices were mostly around the 2022 lows (when funds saw high 10s-$30 range) and late 2023 (around $80), but we lack evidence of famous value managers taking sizable new stakes. Current insider holdings (Armstrong, other executives) still represent roughly 9-10% of total shares each (including vested options) .

**Intrinsic Value (Buffett Method):** Applying a conservative cash-flow approach yields a modest intrinsic estimate. Taking 2023 free cash flow \~ $0.86 b (net cash from ops $0.923b minus capex), and assuming 0-2% annual growth in real cash flows plus a 10-15% discount rate, the present value of cash flows over 10+ years comes out around $9-10 b (roughly $40/share on \~250M shares). With a 30-50% margin of safety (Buffett's recommendation), a "buy" range could be on the order of $20-30/share. This implies a potential return of 5-10% annually from current prices ($206) back to fair value over the decade, modest by high-growth standards. (Exact numbers depend heavily on assumptions, but roughly: at 12% discount and 2% growth, PV ≈$9.4 b ; with 1% growth/10% discount, ≈$10.5 b.) The current price far exceeds these conservative estimates, suggesting a significant margin-of-safety discount would be needed to make Coinbase look like a "deep value" buy. In reverse, the current P/E (market price vs near-zero trailing EPS) is extremely high (\~470x GAAP EPS for 2023) - even on normalized profits, the multiples imply lofty expectations.

**Latest Quarter (Q1 2025):** Coinbase's Q1 2025 results showed modest growth: **$2.0 b** total revenue, **$66 m** GAAP net income . Transaction revenue was $1.3 b (down \~19% from Q4) and subscription/services \~$0.7 b (up \~9% Q/Q) . Trading volumes remain below 2021 peaks, but Coinbase noted expanding derivatives trading and continued growth in USDC stablecoin balances. Adjusted EBITDA was robust ($930m) , reflecting tight cost control. Key metric: Monthly transacting users were roughly 8.0 m in Q1 (up from 7.0 m in 2023) . Coinbase highlighted strategic progress (new product launches and the pending $2.9 b acquisition of crypto derivatives exchange Deribit) , and noted that the dismissal of the SEC lawsuit (Feb 2025) is a major positive development . Overall Q1 results showed stable but still-low revenue relative to 2021, slight profitability, and continued emphasis on investing for long-term growth.

## **Financial Research**

**Historical Financial Metrics (2021-2024):** Coinbase's metrics have been volatile, reflecting crypto cycles:

-   **Revenue:** $7.35 b (2021), $3.15 b (2022, -57%), $2.93 b (2023, -7%), \~ $8.0 b (annualized Q1'25).

-   **Gross Profit:** (Not explicitly disclosed, but net revenue lines given).

-   **Operating Income:** $3.08 b (41.8% of rev) in 2021; loss of $2.71 b (-86%) in 2022; loss of $0.162 b (-5.5%) in 2023 .

-   **Net Income:** +$3.624 b (49.3% margin) in 2021; -$2.625 b (-83.4%) in 2022; +$0.095 b (3.2%) in 2023 .

-   **EPS:** $17.47 (basic) in 2021; -$11.81 in 2022; $0.40 in 2023 .

-   **FCF (Operating Cash):** +$4.04 b in 2021; -$1.585 b in 2022; +$0.923 b in 2023 .

-   **Balance Sheet:** Total assets jumped from \~$80 b in 2021 to $90 b in 2022 and $207 b in 2023 (mostly due to custodial crypto/assets, not company "earnings" assets) . Stockholders' equity was \~$5.5 b (2022) and $6.28 b (2023) .

-   **Debt:** Long-term debt \~$3.4 b (2022) and $3.0 b (2023) (debt/equity \~62% in 2022, \~48% in 2023) . Debt/assets is very low (\~4% in 2022, \~1.5% in 2023) since most "liabilities" are custodial balances. No dividends have ever been paid (Dividends/share = $0).

-   **Valuation Ratios:** P/E swings from \~6x in 2021 (at IPO prices) to negative in 2022 to &gt;400x in 2023. P/B was very high in 2021 (stock was richly priced) then fell in 2022 (price hit multi-year lows). ROE was extremely high in 2021 (due to profit), hugely negative in 2022, and near zero in 2023. ROIC is not very meaningful (Coinbase holds large crypto custodial balances that earn interest, etc.).

-   **Growth Rates:** Revenue growth: -57% (2022), -7% (2023); Net income growth swung -$ (2022 loss vs 2021 gain). FCF growth similarly inverted.

**Metric Interpretation:** The charts (not shown) would illustrate a steep drop in revenues and profits during the 2022 crypto bear market, followed by stabilization in 2023. 2021 was an outlier boom (coin prices up 60-70%), so metrics like profit margin (49%) and ROE (&gt;&gt;30%) were inflated. In 2022, Coinbase lost money as transaction revenue collapsed, demonstrating low earnings resilience. By 2023 and early 2024, cost cuts and a better revenue mix (more stablecoin and staking income) brought Coinbase back to slight profitability. Balance-sheet strength (huge custodial assets) is not directly profitable to Coinbase, but equity remains solid. Valuation multiples have ranged wildly (P/E swung from single digits to enormous as earnings turned near-zero). Current P/B is low (stock price is only \~8-10× book value due to depressed price), but since book value mostly reflects un-earnings assets, this metric is less meaningful. In summary, Coinbase's financial trends track crypto market cycles: excellent metrics in bull run, very weak in downturn, and gradual recovery as new services (staking, stablecoin interest, derivatives) diversify revenue.

**Trend Charts (line graphs):** Over 2021-2024, *Market Price* plummeted from its 2021 peak (\~$250) to \~$35 in Q4 2022, then recovered to \~$174 by end-2023. *P/E* and *P/B* ratios have been extremely volatile (P/E was low in 2021, undefined in 2022 negative, skyrocketed by 2023 as earnings returned to breakeven; P/B crashed in 2022 and rose as share price recovered). *EPS* fell from +$17 to -$11 to +$0.37 over the three years . *Free Cash Flow* (operating cash) similarly dropped into negative in 2022 before rebounding in 2023 . Liquidity/leverage: *Current Ratio* has stayed \~1.0, *Debt/Equity* fell from \~0.65 (2022) to \~0.48 (2023) as earnings rebuilt equity. *Net Income Margin* went from +49% (2021) to -83% (2022) to +3% (2023) . (Graphs would show a sharp V-shape in many metrics - a collapse in 2022 and partial recovery thereafter.)

**Conservative DCF:** Using a simple Discounted Cash Flow with low growth: assume 2024 FCF \~ $0.86-1.0 b (similar to 2023), growing at 1-2% for 10 years, discounted at 10-12%, with terminal growth ≤3%. Our rough DCF yields a present value on the order of $9-10 b for the firm, or roughly $40/share (2023 basis). Applying a 30-50% margin of safety (as Buffett suggests) reduces that to $20-30/share. For example, at 12% discount, 2% growth, PV ≈$9.4 b ($38/share). At 10% discount, 1% growth, PV ≈$10.5 b ($42/share). These imply expected equity returns of 5-10%/yr back to that intrinsic range. By contrast, at current price ($206), the implied expected return (assuming long-term normalization) is negative. This analysis highlights that COIN trades well above any conservative intrinsic value estimate (even pre-Safety Margin), reflecting very optimistic expectations built into the price.
