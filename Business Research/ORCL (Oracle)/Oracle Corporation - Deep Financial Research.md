# **Oracle Corporation - Deep Financial Research**

## **Background Research**

### **Business Model and Main Sources of Income**

Oracle Corporation is one of the world's largest enterprise software and IT infrastructure providers, offering a broad portfolio of database software, business applications (ERP, CRM, etc.), and cloud computing services. Oracle's business model has transitioned in recent years from traditional on-premises software licensing to **cloud-based services**. The company operates in three primary segments:

-   **Cloud Services and License Support** - Recurring revenue from Oracle's cloud SaaS/PaaS/IaaS offerings and ongoing software support/update contracts for on-premise licenses. This is Oracle's largest revenue source (detailed below). Customers pay subscription fees for cloud services or annual support fees for licensed software.

-   **Cloud License and On-Premise License** - One-time fees from sales of new software licenses (for on-premise use or customer-managed cloud) and cloud license deployments. These are upfront revenues recognized when Oracle sells new software licenses to customers (many of whom then also purchase support).

-   **Hardware** - Sales of Oracle's engineered systems, servers, storage appliances, and related hardware support. Oracle's hardware products (like Exadata and Oracle servers) generate revenue both from initial hardware sales and from support contracts.

-   **Services** - Revenue from consulting, implementation, and advanced customer support services to help clients use Oracle products effectively. This includes professional services and training.

**Revenue Breakdown:** Oracle's income is dominated by its cloud services and license support segment. In fiscal year 2024, Oracle's total revenue was about **$53.0 billion**, of which roughly **74%** came from cloud services and license support subscriptions. The remaining revenue was split among new licenses (\~10%), services (\~10%), and hardware (\~6%). The chart below illustrates Oracle's FY2024 revenue mix:

*Oracle FY2024 revenue by segment (total $52.96B). Cloud Services & License Support represents the bulk of Oracle's revenue.*

As shown, recurring **cloud services & license support** revenue was about $39.4B in FY2024 (74.3% of total). **Cloud license & on-premise license** sales contributed roughly $5.1B (\~9.6%). **Services** revenue was $5.4B (\~10.2%), and **Hardware** revenue was $3.1B (\~5.8%). This revenue mix underscores Oracle's dependence on recurring cloud and support contracts, which provide a high-margin, predictable income stream (Oracle's cloud/support gross margins exceed 75%).

Oracle's business model is increasingly cloud-centric: the company not only sells cloud software applications (SaaS) like Fusion ERP and NetSuite, but also rents out cloud infrastructure capacity (OCI - Oracle Cloud Infrastructure) and platform services. These cloud services are delivered from Oracle's data centers and also via partnerships to run Oracle Cloud inside other clouds (e.g. Microsoft Azure). Oracle still monetizes on-premises customers through support contracts and occasional license sales, but growth is driven by cloud adoption. The **subscription-based cloud model** provides Oracle with recurring revenue and higher lifetime client value, albeit often at the expense of lower upfront fees compared to legacy license sales.

**Customers and Units Sold:** Oracle serves hundreds of thousands of customers worldwide - businesses of all sizes, governments, and educational institutions. Exact customer counts aren't regularly disclosed, but Oracle's reach is broad across industries (notably financial services, retail, manufacturing, and the public sector). For instance, Oracle's cloud ERP has thousands of customers, and NetSuite (targeted at mid-market firms) had 32,000+ customers as of a few years ago. The company often highlights that all 100 of the Fortune 100 use Oracle in some capacity.

In terms of "units" sold: Oracle doesn't sell physical units for software, but for hardware, it sells engineered systems (e.g. Exadata database machines) - likely a few thousand such systems annually (exact figures are not public). For cloud, Oracle measures adoption in cloud consumption (for IaaS) and number of SaaS users. For example, Oracle's Fusion Cloud ERP had over 10,000 customers as of 2023, and Oracle's cloud database services are used by thousands of organizations (recently expanded via partnerships with Microsoft and Google). While specific counts are proprietary, Oracle's cloud user base and customer count have been growing, evidenced by a 44% jump in Remaining Performance Obligations to $98B by FY2024 (a proxy for contracted future cloud and support revenue).

### **Short-Term and Long-Term Headwinds & Risks**

Oracle faces several headwinds in both the near term and long term:

-   **Short-Term / Temporary Factors:**

    -   *Macroeconomic conditions:* Broad economic slowdowns or IT spending delays can temporarily soften Oracle's revenue. For example, in times of recession or tight IT budgets, companies may defer software upgrades or cloud migrations, impacting Oracle's short-term sales growth. Foreign exchange fluctuations are another short-term factor - a strong U.S. dollar can reduce reported revenue growth in USD (Oracle noted 2024 revenue was up 6% in USD **and constant currency**, implying FX headwinds).

    -   *Political & Regulatory:* Geopolitical events (trade restrictions, sanctions) or regulatory delays can postpone government or international deals. For instance, government procurement slowdowns or uncertainty around U.S.-China trade relations could temporarily impact Oracle's hardware exports or cloud region expansion in certain countries. These are typically transient and resolve over time.

    -   *Cloud Capacity & Data Center Build-Out:* In the near term, Oracle's cloud growth could be **constrained by data center capacity**. Management noted that in FY2024 some cloud demand outpaced available OCI infrastructure, and they are racing to build new data centers. This is a *good* problem (demand &gt; supply), but in the short run, if OCI cannot provision capacity fast enough, revenue might be deferred. Oracle expects this to ease as new capacity comes online in FY2025.

    -   *Integration of Acquisitions:* Oracle's recent acquisitions (e.g. Cerner in 2022 for healthcare IT) present short-term execution risk. Integrating acquired companies can temporarily distract management and incur restructuring costs. Notably, Oracle's FY2022 net income was reduced by acquisition-related expenses from the Cerner deal. Over a few quarters, these integration issues usually stabilize.

-   **Long-Term / Structural Factors:**

    -   *Cloud Competition:* Oracle's biggest long-term challenge is competing against top cloud providers (Amazon AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud) in infrastructure and against SaaS specialists (like Salesforce, Workday) in applications. Oracle was a late entrant to cloud and must fight for market share. The risk is that despite recent gains, Oracle could permanently lose database or application workloads to competitors' cloud platforms. For example, AWS and Azure offer database services that threaten Oracle's core database franchise. Intense competition could pressure Oracle's growth and margins in the long run if not managed.

    -   *Technology Transitions:* The IT industry evolves rapidly (e.g. rise of open-source databases, AI-driven automation). Oracle faces the risk of technological disruption. **Open-source databases** (MySQL, PostgreSQL - though Oracle owns MySQL) and cloud-native databases have eroded the dominance of Oracle's flagship Database in some scenarios. Additionally, the shift to **AI and machine learning workloads**- Oracle is adapting by offering AI cloud services, but failure to lead in new tech paradigms could be a long-term headwind.

    -   *Permanent On-Premises Decline:* Oracle still derives significant revenue from legacy on-premise license support (\~$19.6B in FY2024). This stream could permanently decline as customers migrate to cloud alternatives (some not Oracle's). Oracle needs to successfully transition these customers to Oracle Cloud to avoid a long-term attrition of its installed base. A related risk is **customer consolidation and saturation** - many large enterprises already run Oracle, so growth must come from expanding cloud services usage or taking share from competitors.

    -   *Economic & Political Regime Changes:* While macro effects can be short-term, there are potential lasting effects from broad changes like deglobalization or major regulatory shifts. For instance, increased data sovereignty laws (requiring data to be stored locally) could raise costs for Oracle's global cloud operations long-term. Similarly, if U.S.-China relations worsen significantly, Oracle's business in China (which historically contributed a few percent of revenue) could be permanently constrained.

    -   *Leadership and Cultural Factors:* Larry Ellison (Oracle's co-founder, CTO and former CEO) remains a driving force in Oracle's strategy. At 79 years old, Ellison's eventual transition could pose long-term uncertainties. Oracle's culture and vision have been heavily influenced by Ellison - any future leadership change is a longer-term consideration. However, CEO Safra Catz has been with Oracle \~24 years and provides continuity.

In summary, Oracle's short-term risks relate to economic cycles and execution (cloud capacity, acquisition integration) which could *temporarily* slow results for a few quarters. Its long-term headwinds center on *structural industry shifts and competitive dynamics* that could *permanently* impact the business if Oracle fails to adapt (cloud competition, technological disruption, declining legacy business). So far, Oracle has shown resilience by pivoting to cloud and leveraging its entrenched enterprise customer base, but these strategic challenges will persist.

### **Cost Structure: Fixed vs. Variable Costs**

Oracle's cost structure combines high fixed costs (for product development and sales) with variable costs (especially to deliver cloud services). Key cost components include:

-   **Research & Development (R&D):** R&D is a largely fixed cost. Oracle spent $8.9 billion on R&D in FY2024 (about 17% of revenue). This includes software development, engineering salaries, and product enhancements. R&D doesn't fluctuate directly with sales in the short term - Oracle maintains steady investment to improve its database, applications, and cloud platform. Over time, R&D has grown (it was $7.2B in FY2022, $8.6B in FY2023) as Oracle invests in cloud and emerging tech. In economic downturns Oracle might slow hiring but generally **R&D is fixed** and critical for long-term competitiveness.

-   **Sales and Marketing (S&M):** These include salesforce salaries, commissions (variable portion), and marketing programs. In FY2024, S&M expense was $8.27B. A chunk of S&M is fixed (base salaries, marketing campaigns) but there's a **variable component** - e.g. sales commissions and bonuses scale with bookings. Oracle noted S&M expense actually *decreased* slightly in 2024 (from $8.83B to $8.27B) partly due to lower headcount and sales-related expenses. In tougher economic conditions, Oracle can trim variable sales compensation or discretionary marketing, making S&M semi-variable. However, cutting too deeply could hurt future sales, so Oracle balances cost control with continued customer engagement.

-   **Cloud Infrastructure & Support Costs (Cost of Cloud Services):** Operating Oracle's cloud (data center facilities, servers, network, energy, and cloud support staff) is a **variable cost** that scales with usage and capacity. In FY2024, cloud services and license support expense was $9.43B. As cloud revenues grow, Oracle must spend more on infrastructure and personnel to serve customers - but there are economies of scale (gross margin \~76% in this segment). This cost is partly fixed (maintaining data centers ready for peak loads) and partly variable (e.g. electricity, third-party cloud hosting, usage-based components). In downturns, if cloud usage growth slows, these costs would also grow more slowly. Oracle's challenge is to **optimize cloud capacity** - unused capacity is a fixed cost drag, whereas fully utilized infrastructure improves margins.

-   **Hardware Manufacturing & Support Costs:** These vary with hardware sales volume. Oracle's hardware cost of goods was $0.891B in FY2024 (on $3.07B hardware revenue). This includes component costs and assembly (Oracle outsources hardware manufacturing). It's mostly variable per unit, though Oracle has some fixed overhead in managing the hardware business. As hardware revenue has been declining, Oracle has reduced these costs (FY2024 hardware COGS fell 14% YoY) to maintain margins. In a given quarter, if hardware sales drop, Oracle's material costs drop too, but not perfectly - there are fixed support and supply chain expenses in this segment.

-   **General & Administrative (G&A):** G&A was $1.55B in FY2024 - this covers corporate overhead, legal, finance. It behaves as fixed cost (relatively stable year to year). Oracle did show a slight decrease in G&A from $1.58B to $1.55B in 2024, indicating some cost discipline or one-time savings. G&A might be scaled back modestly in lean times (e.g. fewer contractors, travel), but core G&A is needed to run the business and thus largely fixed.

-   **Capital Expenditures (CapEx):** While not an operating expense, it's worth noting Oracle's **CapEx** has risen due to cloud - building data centers is capital-intensive (servers, facilities). CapEx peaked around $4 billion in FY2022 as Oracle invested in cloud capacity and acquired Cerner data centers. CapEx is a fixed investment that doesn't vary short-term with revenue, but Oracle can adjust future CapEx plans depending on expected demand (e.g. accelerate or delay data center construction). CapEx affects **cost per unit** in cloud (depreciation per server). Oracle's depreciation expense flows into cost of cloud services over hardware lifetimes, so if CapEx is underutilized, cost per unit of cloud rises. Oracle's strategy is to grow cloud revenue to absorb these fixed infrastructure costs.

**Cost Per Unit and Trends:** Oracle's **cost per revenue dollar** has generally improved in stable periods and worsened temporarily during transitions. For instance, as cloud revenue grows, gross margin improves (spreading fixed data center costs). Oracle's cloud gross margin expanded as OCI scale increased and legacy support (very high margin) remains large. However, in FY2022 Oracle's **net margin** dropped due to integration costs of Cerner (a lower-margin business initially) - essentially a temporary hit. In economic downturns, Oracle's margins may compress slightly if revenue growth stalls while fixed costs like R&D and cloud infrastructure continue. Oracle tends to prioritize sustaining R&D and cloud capacity for long-term competitiveness, even if it means short-term margin pressure. Conversely, in boom times or high growth periods, fixed costs are leveraged and margins expand. Historically, Oracle has very high operating margins (often \~40% non-GAAP). In FY2024, GAAP operating margin was \~29% (lower due to Cerner amortization and stock comp), but excluding those it was \~44%. This underscores Oracle's high fixed-cost, high-margin model: once expenses are covered, additional sales heavily contribute to profit. Over the past decade, Oracle's operating expenses grew much slower than revenue, reflecting efficiency and cost discipline. For example, sales and marketing in 2024 was slightly lower than 2019 even though revenues were higher - Oracle trimmed sales costs as much of its revenue comes from renewals (lower cost than new sales). During economic stress (like 2020's pandemic), Oracle managed to keep R&D and S&M roughly flat, protecting profitability, and in fact operating income still grew. In summary, **fixed costs** (R&D, much of S&M, cloud infrastructure depreciation) are significant and require Oracle to maintain a large revenue base; **variable costs** (sales commissions, cloud energy usage, hardware components) provide some flexibility to scale down if demand falls. Oracle's largest expenses - R&D and cloud ops - tend to persist across economic cycles, reflecting a commitment to innovation and service delivery even in downturns.

### **Management Team and Governance**

**Current Executive Leadership:** Oracle is led by **Safra A. Catz (CEO)** and **Lawrence J. "Larry" Ellison (CTO and Chairman)**. Safra Catz has been with Oracle since 1999 and became CEO in 2014 (initially co-CEO, sole CEO since 2019). She is known for her financial acumen and has overseen Oracle's profitability and acquisition integration for years. Larry Ellison co-founded Oracle in 1977 and served as CEO until 2014; he remains deeply involved as Chief Technology Officer (driving product strategy) and as Chairman of the Board. Ellison (age 79) is the visionary behind Oracle's database and cloud push, and his continued role provides technological direction and continuity. Other key executives include: **Edward Screven (Chief Corporate Architect)**, who guides Oracle's technology roadmap, **Juan Loaiza (EVP, Oracle Database Technologies)**, and **Clay Magouyrk (EVP, Oracle Cloud Infrastructure)** - all long-tenured leaders responsible for Oracle's core product areas. Oracle's executive team is notably stable; many have decades at the company, which has helped maintain strategic consistency.

**Board of Directors:** Oracle's Board is chaired by Larry Ellison and includes 14 other directors, a mix of industry veterans and independent members. Notable board members are **Jeffrey O. Henley (Vice Chairman)** - Oracle's former CFO, on the board since 1991; **Michael Boskin** (Stanford economist, on board since 1994); **Bruce Chizen** (former Adobe CEO); **Leon Panetta** (former U.S. Defense Secretary and CIA Director); and several others with diverse backgrounds in technology, finance, and government (Naomi Seligman, George Conrades, etc.). The board skews older and has faced some criticism in the past for long tenures. However, it provides experience and oversight. Importantly, **Safra Catz** is also on the board, and **Mark Hurd** was a director until his passing in 2019 (the AdvisoryCloud reference is outdated). Oracle's board is generally considered management-friendly (Ellison's sizable ownership influences this). They have approved Oracle's aggressive share buybacks and acquisition strategy historically. The board's compensation committee has drawn shareholder ire in the past for large executive pay packages (Ellison's security costs, stock option grants to CEOs), which ties into governance concerns discussed below.

**Historical Leadership and Culture:** Oracle's leadership under Ellison has been famously competitive and bold. The company grew through a combination of technological innovation and aggressive acquisition of competitors. A **historical executive of note** is **Bob Miner** (Oracle co-founder, lead architect of Oracle's database; he passed away in 1994) - while not on the current team, his engineering ethos set early technical direction. Another is **Jeffrey O. Henley**, Oracle's CFO from 1991-2004, who helped shape Oracle's financial strategy and remains as Vice Chairman. In the 2000s, Oracle's second-in-command was **Charles Phillips (President)** and then **Mark Hurd** (former HP CEO) who joined as co-CEO in 2014. Hurd was known for operational excellence and sales rigor; his tenure (2014-2019) alongside Catz was marked by a smooth transition as Ellison stepped back from day-to-day management. After Hurd's death, Safra Catz has solely led as CEO. The continuity of having long-time insiders (Catz, Ellison) at the helm has meant Oracle's strategy (focus on database dominance, aggressive sales, cloud shift) has been consistently executed. Oracle's culture has often been described as **highly results-driven** and sometimes combative in the marketplace (reflecting Ellison's personality). While that culture successfully built an enterprise software giant, it has had downsides (e.g. some customer dissatisfaction with aggressive sales tactics in the past). Recently, Oracle's culture has been evolving to be more cloud-customer-centric, though it remains very much performance-focused.

**Management Credibility and Track Record:** Oracle's management has a mixed but generally positive track record with investors:

-   *Following Through on Promises:* In the past, Oracle sometimes over-promised on "cloud" growth when transitioning its reporting. Notably in 2018, Oracle stopped separately disclosing cloud revenues and folded them into broader categories, which some analysts viewed as a lack of transparency when cloud growth appeared to slow. This raised questions about management credibility at that time. However, in recent years Safra Catz tends to give **conservative guidance** and often meets or beats it. For example, Oracle projected mid-single digit revenue growth for FY2024 and delivered 6% (constant currency). On major strategic shifts (like building a Gen2 Cloud or acquiring Cerner to enter healthcare IT), Oracle has executed steadily - cloud revenue is now accelerating (up 50%+ in IaaS for Q4 FY2025), and the Cerner unit (Oracle Health) is being integrated with an eye toward long-term growth despite short-term margin impact. Overall, management has delivered on turning Oracle into a cloud contender, albeit later than some rivals.

-   *Transparency and Problem Disclosure:* Oracle's management provides substantial detail on financials but can be opaque on certain metrics (like cloud segment profitability). They generally do not "hide" problems outright, but in the 2010s Oracle was criticized for not breaking out cloud vs on-prem revenue which masked the slow start of Oracle's cloud business. More recently, Oracle has been upfront about challenges - e.g. Safra Catz acknowledged that legacy license sales were declining and cloud was cannibalizing some of that, which is a transition Oracle must navigate. On earnings calls, Catz is known for being direct and factual, though often tight-lipped beyond guidance and prepared remarks. She tends not to sugarcoat results - for instance, if currency hurt results or an acquisition trimmed margins, she'll state it. However, Oracle's communication style is generally less candid than some peers (they prefer prepared scripts to spontaneous Q&A depth). The management doesn't often volunteer negative news, but required disclosures (like a revenue miss or large loss contracts) are made. Importantly, **no major scandals**regarding financial reporting have occurred since Oracle's early history (in the 1990s Oracle had a one-time accounting issue with revenue recognition, long resolved). Investors' trust in Oracle management is decent, though some have been skeptical of Oracle's ability to innovate in cloud - something Ellison and Catz have worked to prove through results.

-   *Analyst Engagement:* On earnings calls, Safra Catz and Larry Ellison both speak. Catz handles financials; Ellison often interjects with product commentary. Ellison can be feisty - in past calls he has openly criticized competitors or dismisses certain analyst concerns. For example, when asked about cloud competition, Ellison has been known to confidently claim Oracle will surpass rivals in database cloud, sometimes with bold statements. Safra Catz, by contrast, answers analysts' questions in a measured, if somewhat guarded, manner. If an analyst asks something she views as off-base, she often reiterates Oracle's official stance rather than provide new detail. Analysts have noted that Oracle's calls are less "open" than some peers - management sticks to key points. Nonetheless, they do address major questions (such as Cerner growth rates, cloud capex needs, etc.) albeit often without extensive elaboration.

**Integrity and Shareholder Alignment:** There have been occasional concerns about Oracle's governance - particularly **executive compensation**. Larry Ellison famously took $1 salary for years but received huge stock option grants and Oracle covers his personal security costs (over $1.5M/year). Safra Catz's pay has also been very high (including large multi-year stock options). In 2019, Oracle's "say on pay" vote barely passed with \~53% approval, meaning nearly half of shareholders objected to the pay packages. Proxy advisors ISS and Glass Lewis have flagged Oracle for potential governance issues like lack of an independent board chair and outsized executive awards. Some institutional investors have questioned management's alignment given these pay issues. However, it's also true that **Ellison owns \~40%** of Oracle, an enormous personal stake worth tens of billions, which arguably aligns his interests with all shareholders (Oracle's share price performance directly impacts Ellison's net worth significantly). Safra Catz, in contrast, owns only \~0.03% (about 1.1 million shares as of 2025, after selling stock over years). Her wealth is still largely tied to Oracle's success through stock grants, but she has periodically sold shares (including a \~$200M sale in June 2025). Those sales follow pre-set plans and often involve exercising options, but heavy insider selling can cause investor unease. Notably, Ellison himself has occasionally sold small portions of stock, usually to fund personal ventures (e.g. his purchase of Hawaiian island Lanai), but he retains the vast majority. There haven't been allegations of wrongdoing by Oracle's management in recent memory; the questions are more about **shareholder friendliness** (e.g. returning cash, avoiding entrenchment).

In terms of **longevity**, Safra Catz has been CEO for 9 years (in Oracle's C-suite for over two decades) and is well-regarded for operational and financial execution. Larry Ellison's relentless focus on winning (sometimes at the expense of warm customer relations) defined Oracle's culture, but he's mellowed somewhat in public presentations, now emphasizing Oracle's technology strengths. Management's credibility improved recently as Oracle's cloud momentum picked up, validating their strategy. There were skeptics who doubted Oracle's cloud pivot; the strong FY2023-FY2025 cloud numbers have answered some of those doubts. As a result, Oracle's stock hit all-time highs in 2023-2025, reflecting renewed investor confidence in management's strategic execution.

**Value Alignment (Skin in the Game):** Oracle's top leadership has significant equity interests. **Larry Ellison** owns roughly 1.145 billion Oracle shares (≈40% of shares outstanding) - an extraordinarily high insider ownership for a $300B+ company. This stake, worth over $100 billion, means Ellison's wealth is heavily tied to Oracle's stock performance, arguably aligning his interests with shareholders. He benefits far more from Oracle's market cap growth than from any salary (his annual salary is trivial, though he does get stock options and perks). **Safra Catz**, while not an owner on Ellison's scale, has accumulated shares over years - but she tends to exercise options and sell stock regularly. As of 2025 she directly owned \~1.12 million shares (worth \~$250M at current prices) after a recent sale. She also holds additional vested and unvested options. Her net worth thus does depend on Oracle's share price, and Oracle has often structured CEO compensation with large performance stock units (PSUs) that only pay if stock targets are met. For example, in 2018 Oracle granted Catz and Hurd multi-year performance stock options requiring Oracle's stock to outperform the market by certain thresholds - showing an attempt at alignment (though some shareholders still opposed the scale of the grant). Other directors and executives own comparatively small stakes (outside Ellison).

A point to note is Ellison's stake could theoretically create a conflict: as such a large shareholder, he can heavily influence votes (which can deter activist investors and certain governance changes). But from a value perspective, Ellison has an interest in Oracle's long-term success and has often preached that he manages Oracle for the long run, not short-term stock pops.

**Insider Buying/Selling in Declines:** Insiders at Oracle (aside from Ellison) generally **sell on strength** rather than buy on dips. We have not seen Safra Catz or other top execs make open-market purchases during stock drops. Instead, they tend to exercise options and sell as part of diversification or pre-arranged plans. For example, in early 2022 when Oracle stock dipped after the Cerner acquisition announcement, we did not see public filings of Safra or others buying stock; rather, Safra continued selling some shares that year as per her trading plan. Ellison also has not really *bought* Oracle stock on the open market in recent memory - but he also hasn't sold large portions (only small periodic sales). Essentially, Ellison's huge existing stake serves as his "skin in the game," whereas other execs are not known for opportunistic buying during short-term declines. This might raise a slight concern about confidence, but it's also common for executives with large option grants to monetize some portion.

**Capital Allocation and Shareholder Meetings:** Oracle's management has shown strong shareholder-return tendencies via **massive share buybacks** and regular (and increasing) dividends. Over the last decade, Oracle repurchased an astonishing amount of stock - its share count dropped from \~4.6 billion in 2014 to \~2.8 billion by 2024. These buybacks (over $70 billion worth in 10 years) returned capital to shareholders and boosted EPS, aligning with value investors' interests. Safra Catz, as a former investment banker, is very focused on efficient capital use; she once quipped that Oracle's goal was to **"shrink the float"** aggressively when the stock was undervalued. Indeed, Oracle repurchased shares heavily especially in the 2017-2020 period when the stock traded at lower multiples (e.g. \~$40-50). This indicates management believed the stock was a good investment then - an alignment with long-term shareholders. In recent years, buybacks slowed (only \~$0.9B total in FY2024, mainly to offset stock compensation) as Oracle used cash for Cerner and deleveraging. But Oracle announced a new buyback authorization of $10B in 2022 after the acquisition, and by Q4 2025 the board authorized a further $20B buyback, signaling continued commitment to returning cash. The **dividend** was initiated in 2009 and has grown consistently (from $0.05/quarter in 2009 to now $0.50/quarter in 2025). This steady dividend growth (14% CAGR over 10 years) shows alignment with shareholders seeking income. The latest raise in June 2023 (from $0.32 to $0.40) and again in June 2025 (to $0.50) demonstrates management/shareholder alignment in sharing profits.

In shareholder meetings and votes, Ellison's influence dominates (he controls \~40% voting power). This means shareholder proposals not supported by Ellison/board typically fail. For example, proposals for an independent board chair or enhanced pay equity reporting have been regularly voted down - likely because Ellison (and allied insiders) vote against them. On the advisory "Say on Pay," as mentioned, 2019's vote was low. The board responded by slightly modifying compensation structure, and by 2021 say-on-pay support rose to 78%. Top institutional shareholders (Vanguard \~5.7%, BlackRock \~5.2%) generally vote with management on most issues except extreme cases. In Oracle's case, in 2019 both Vanguard and BlackRock *did* vote against the say-on-pay (per reports of low support) - essentially sending a message on pay practices. Oracle's management took note but did not drastically cut pay; they framed high pay as necessary to retain top talent. This episode shows some tension but ultimately management's large ownership can override external dissent.

**Overall Management Assessment:** Oracle's management team is highly experienced and deeply invested (especially Ellison) in the company's success. They have navigated Oracle through major industry transitions (mainframe to client-server, to internet, to cloud) and maintained Oracle's profitability. There have been concerns about governance (e.g. board independence, executive pay), but no evidence of malfeasance. Shareholders have benefited from Oracle's substantial buybacks and dividends, though some might prefer more conservative pay. The presence of Ellison as both a major owner and CTO ensures continuity of vision (database and applications focus, integrated tech stack strategy). Safra Catz provides financial discipline - Oracle has consistently high margins and cash flows under her watch. One could argue management was late to cloud, but once convinced, they redirected the company aggressively (even accepting short-term margin hits to build cloud capacity and acquire cloud capabilities like NetSuite and Cerner). Their recent execution suggests they are meeting the challenges, as seen in accelerating cloud revenues and upbeat FY2025 guidance (expecting double-digit growth). Going forward, investors will watch how Oracle's management capitalizes on new trends (AI) and continues integrating acquisitions to sustain growth. The significant insider ownership (Ellison) and long tenures indicate management's incentives are largely aligned with long-term value creation, albeit with the caveat of Ellison's outsized control possibly stifling certain governance changes that other shareholders might favor.

### **Top Shareholders and Shareholder Votes**

Oracle's ownership is concentrated. **Larry Ellison** is by far the largest shareholder with roughly 1.145 billion shares (about **40.8%** of the company). This stake gives Ellison effective veto power on any shareholder resolutions and board elections. The next largest holders are the big index fund firms: **Vanguard Group** owns \~146.5 million shares (\~5.2%), **BlackRock** about 160.9 million (5.7%), and **State Street** around 3% (these figures can fluctuate slightly each quarter). Other notable holders include **Berkshire Hathaway** *briefly* in 2018 (Buffett bought $2B stake, \~1% of Oracle, then sold within a quarter) - as of now Berkshire has no Oracle position. **Institutional ownership** overall is high (over 50% of shares, excluding Ellison), meaning most non-Ellison shares are held by mutual funds, ETFs, pensions, etc. These institutions typically vote in line with proxy advisor recommendations on governance issues.

At Oracle's shareholder meetings, all board-nominated directors are routinely elected (Ellison's votes ensure that). **Shareholder proposals** occasionally appear - in recent years, proposals have included: (1) a request for an independent board chair (separate Chair/CEO - essentially asking Ellison to step down as Chair), (2) a pay equity report (asking Oracle to report on gender pay gap), and (3) a proposal to lower the threshold for shareholders to call special meetings or act by written consent. These kinds of proposals have generally **not passed** at Oracle. For example, proposals to require an independent chairman have been defeated - likely because Ellison's 40% vote plus the board's recommendations against it sway the outcome. The **top institutional holders** (Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street) often support certain governance reforms across their portfolios - but even if all of them supported a proposal, Ellison's stake can outvote them. In 2020, a shareholder proposal for a gender pay gap report at Oracle received only \~32% support (despite such proposals sometimes getting higher support elsewhere), indicating that large holders did not universally back it or Ellison's block decided it.

**Voting Tendencies of Top Shareholders:**

-   *Ellison:* Votes with management (himself) on all issues. Essentially, his stance = management stance. He has voted down proposals that would diminish his control (like independent chair).

-   *Vanguard & BlackRock:* These firms have published general proxy voting guidelines. Both typically favor combined CEO/Chair roles only if a strong lead independent director is present (Oracle does have a lead independent director, Dr. Michael Boskin). They sometimes vote for an independent chair in cases of governance concern - it's not public exactly how they voted at Oracle, but the repeated failure of that proposal suggests they likely sided with management or abstained. On executive pay (say-on-pay), in 2019 Vanguard and BlackRock *did*vote against Oracle's pay package (as evidenced by only \~53% approval, meaning many institutional votes against). Since then, Oracle's say-on-pay approval improved (78% in 2024's meeting), implying the top shareholders became more comfortable (perhaps after Oracle engaged with them and tweaked some pay practices). On shareholder rights (written consent, special meetings), large institutions often support reasonable measures but also defer to board's reasoning. Oracle's board has argued its existing provisions are adequate, and such proposals haven't passed (in 2024, written consent proposals at many firms got \~30-40% support on average, likely similar at Oracle). So Vanguard/BlackRock may have split or been swayed by management arguments in Oracle's case.

-   *Other Large Holders:* **State Street** tends to vote in line with governance best practices but is also management-friendly. **T. Rowe Price, Fidelity, etc.** (active managers who hold Oracle) have varied stances but usually focus on financial performance. Given Oracle's stock did well in recent years, active managers were likely supportive of management in votes (since no activist campaign or value destruction scenario is occurring).

**Key Topics and Stands:** The top shareholders' main "votes of significance" in Oracle's recent history were on executive compensation. **Say-on-Pay votes:** In 2017 and 2018, Oracle's say-on-pay got unusually low support (for example, 2017 had only \~54% support, 2018 about the same). This reflected institutions being unhappy with huge stock option grants to Ellison, Catz, and Hurd without performance conditions. In 2019, facing a likely defeat, Oracle added performance conditions to a new mega-grant for Catz/Hurd and did extensive outreach; even so, only 53% approved. The opposition came from big institutional investors (Vanguard, BlackRock, CalPERS, etc.) who publicly criticized Oracle's pay practice (CalPERS withheld support for many Oracle directors too that year). By 2021 and 2022, say-on-pay support was up around 85-95%, meaning most top investors were satisfied after Oracle made some changes (e.g. Oracle did not give Ellison new stock options in those years and reduced some perks). Thus, top shareholders effectively pressured Oracle's board to moderate pay, and the board responded enough to placate them (though Oracle still pays top execs richly).

Another significant topic is Oracle's **acquisitions and stock issuance**. The Cerner acquisition in 2022 was Oracle's largest ever ($28B). It was financed mostly by debt rather than stock, so no shareholder vote was needed (only target shareholders voted). But top shareholders generally supported Oracle's M&A strategy historically, as it drove growth. The only concern was if Oracle were to issue a lot of stock (dilution) for deals - which they usually haven't (they prefer using cash/debt). Indeed, after Cerner, Oracle's share count *increased slightly* from 2.766B to 2.823B shares (issuing some equity to a trust for Cerner holders), but that \~2% dilution was minor relative to long-term buybacks. Shareholders seem content as long as Oracle's acquisitions eventually bolster earnings.

In annual meetings, routine proposals (election of directors, ratification of auditors, etc.) easily pass. The **board of directors elections**: Vanguard, BlackRock, etc., have policies to vote against certain directors if, say, they attend &lt;75% of meetings or if there are persistent governance failings. In Oracle's case, despite concerns, no director election has been lost or even close. So effectively, **top shareholders often engage behind the scenes with Oracle** (through investor relations and governance meetings) rather than mounting public proxy fights that they'd likely lose. By 2025, relations seem stable: Oracle's stock is up strongly, the dividend is increased, and governance issues are not front-page because performance is good. Vanguard and BlackRock's main focus lately has been on ESG issues (like climate disclosure, diversity). In summary, Oracle's top shareholders (outside Ellison) exert influence mainly through advisory votes and engagement, rather than through decisive votes in meetings (since Ellison's block is controlling). They have pushed for better alignment on pay and transparency, and Oracle's management did make partial concessions. All top institutional holders appear generally supportive of Oracle's strategy - no activist investor campaigns exist, which implies satisfaction with the direction and returns. ### **Share Buyback Program (Current vs 10-Year History)**

Oracle has been a prolific repurchaser of its shares. Over the past 10 years, Oracle spent on the order of **$100 billion** on share buybacks, reducing its shares outstanding by nearly 45%. This is one of the largest buyback programs among tech companies. The **strategy** has been to use Oracle's prodigious free cash flow and borrowed funds (when interest rates were low) to retire shares, boosting EPS and returning cash to shareholders.

**Historical Buybacks (2013-2022):** In the early 2010s, Oracle's share count was around 4.5-5.1 billion. Oracle initiated large repurchases around 2013-2014. For example, in FY2014 Oracle bought back \~$10 billion in stock (reducing the share count from \~4.6B to 4.4B). The pace accelerated: during FY2017-FY2019, Oracle sometimes repurchased $10B+ per year - notably in FY2018 Oracle did an aggressive buyback funded partly by new debt issuance (taking advantage of the 2017 tax reform that allowed repatriating overseas cash cheaply). By May 2019, shares outstanding were down to \~3.73B, a dramatic decline. Oracle continued buybacks in 2019 (e.g. a large \~$12B accelerated buyback in Q2 2019) such that by May 2021, share count was \~3.02B. This is a \~35% drop in shares in just 7 years - significantly enhancing each remaining share's claim on earnings.

Oracle's buybacks slowed after 2019 for a couple reasons: (a) the stock price rose substantially (reducing the attractiveness of buybacks vs other uses), and (b) Oracle took on debt to finance buybacks and had to manage its balance sheet. In the decade prior, Oracle's net debt was low, but by 2020 after big buybacks, Oracle had net debt of $20B. Still, in FY2020-FY2021 Oracle continued moderate repurchases ($15B across those two years).

**Current Buyback Plan:** Oracle paused large repurchases in FY2022 due to the Cerner acquisition. In June 2022, Oracle issued $15B in new debt for the Cerner deal and understandably curtailed buybacks to focus on debt repayment. As a result, in FY2023 and FY2024, Oracle's buybacks were minimal - primarily offsetting employee stock issuance. For instance, in FY2024 Oracle repurchased only about **$0.4B** net (they bought back \~$1.1B but also issued \~$0.7B to employees). The share count actually ticked *up* slightly in FY2023 due to shares issued for the Cerner acquisition and stock compensation, ending a long streak of decline.

However, as of FY2025, Oracle signaled a return to buybacks. In December 2022 (FY2023), Oracle's Board authorized a new **$10 billion** repurchase program. By March 2023, Oracle used some of it, and in June 2023 Oracle still had \~$8B authorization left. The board in June 2023 then added **another $20B** authorization, bringing available buyback firepower to about $28B. This coincided with Oracle's stock reaching \~all-time highs (above $120 in mid-2023). Interestingly, Oracle did not rush to execute that authorization immediately - likely because they are balancing debt reduction and cash needs. Through the first half of FY2025, Oracle's 10-Q shows only modest buyback activity (e.g. \~$150M per quarter). This indicates management is being patient - possibly saving the authorization to use opportunistically if the stock weakens or once leverage is reduced further.

**Comparison to Former 10 Years:** In the **former decade (2013-2022)**, Oracle's average annual buyback yield was around 5-6%, meaning it repurchased about 5-6% of its shares each year on average - exceptionally high. Some years it was much higher (e.g. \~9% in 2018). In contrast, the **current pace (2023-2024)** has been negligible (&lt;&lt;1% of shares). Essentially, Oracle took a breather from buybacks due to the Cerner acquisition. Now with the new authorizations, we can expect the pace to pick up again. If Oracle uses the $28B authorization fully over say 2-3 years, that could repurchase \~8-10% of shares at current market prices - a significant tailwind to EPS. Safra Catz has stated that once debt is paid down to target levels, any surplus cash will go to buybacks. Oracle's operating cash flow is \~$20B/year, dividend uses \~$6B, CapEx \~$2-3B; that leaves potentially &gt;$10B/year for either debt paydown or buybacks. As interest rates on their new debt are in the \~3.8-4.3% range (from 2022 bond issues), paying those down versus buying stock which has an earnings yield of \~4% (P/E \~25) is roughly breakeven financially - so they can do a mix.

**Impact on Shares Outstanding:** Over 2014-2019, shares fell by \~15 million per quarter on average. In 2020-2022, it slowed to \~5 million per quarter, and in 2023 it was flat-to-up due to Cerner stock issuance. In 2024, share count was about 2.823B. If Oracle resumes buybacks aggressively, we might see share count drop under 2.5B in a few years. This magnifies per-share metrics and shows management's continued shareholder-friendly stance.

It's worth noting that Ellison benefits enormously from buybacks - his ownership % rose from \~25% in 2010 to \~40% now largely because Oracle bought out other shareholders while he held his stake. Some governance critics say Oracle's buybacks consolidated Ellison's control. But all shareholders who remained saw their stake grow proportionally. Many value investors have applauded Oracle's buybacks, especially when the stock was undervalued in the $40s and $50s a few years ago. Now that the stock is \~$100+, one could question if buybacks are as accretive. However, Oracle's management clearly still sees them as a good use of capital given the large new authorization - implying they believe Oracle's growth prospects make the stock a worthy investment of cash (or simply they have excess cash with no better alternative uses).

In summary, Oracle's current buyback plan is to **resume significant repurchases** after a hiatus, facilitated by strong cash flows and moderated capex. The former 10-year period saw one of the tech industry's most aggressive buyback campaigns, shrinking the float dramatically. Moving forward, Oracle is likely to continue repurchasing shares, albeit perhaps not quite at the frenetic pace of the late 2010s (unless the stock price dips and they seize the opportunity). The combination of dividends and buybacks should remain a core component of Oracle's capital allocation, underscoring management's confidence in Oracle's long-term business (they're effectively betting on themselves by buying stock) and commitment to returning value to shareholders.

### **Intrinsic Value Analysis (Warren Buffett's Method)**

To estimate Oracle's **intrinsic value** in a Warren Buffett-style framework, we perform a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) focusing on Owner Earnings (free cash flow) projections. Buffett's approach emphasizes using conservative assumptions: a **discount rate of 10-15%**, a **margin of safety of 30-50%**, and a **terminal growth rate below 3%** (below long-term GDP). We'll derive an intrinsic value per share and compare it to the market price, then estimate the implied expected return at the current price.

**Inputs for DCF:** We use Oracle's current financials as a base. In FY2024, Oracle's free cash flow (FCF) was about **$11.8 billion**. (Owner earnings would be similar to FCF since Oracle's maintenance capex is roughly equal to depreciation over time; Oracle's FY2024 operating cash flow was $18.7B and capex \~$6.9B, yielding FCF \~$11.8B.) Shares outstanding are \~2.82 billion, so FCF per share is about $4.18. Oracle also paid out $1.60 in dividends, leaving \~$2.58 per share in retained cash flow.

**Scenario 1: Conservative (Lowest growth in last 10 years)
**Oracle's lowest annual revenue growth in the past decade was around -3% (FY2016), but that was an anomalous decline. Excluding negative outliers, the lowest positive growth was \~2-3%. To be conservative, we can assume **0% revenue growth for a few years** (flat revenue) and only nominal growth thereafter. However, Oracle's margins have been rising with cloud mix, so even flat revenue could yield some FCF growth. But let's be very cautious: **assume FCF grows just 2% annually** for the next 5 years (below inflation), then 1.5% terminal growth (below GDP). This essentially models a stagnation scenario.

-   Starting FCF = $11.8B (Year 0).

-   Years 1-5: grow at 2% (so Year 5 FCF \~ $13.0B).

-   Terminal growth = 1.5%.

-   Discount rate = 10% (Buffett's minimum hurdle for stable large companies).

Calculating DCF:
Present Value of 5-year cash flows ≈ $11.8B \* \[1 - (1.02)^5 / (1.10)^5\] / (10% - 2%) (this formula is approximate for growing annuity). Let's do it stepwise:
Year 1 FCF $12.0B, PV at 10% = $10.9B;
Year 2 FCF $12.3B, PV^2 = $10.2B;
Year 3 $12.5B PV = $9.5B;
Year 4 $12.8B PV = $8.9B;
Year 5 $13.0B PV = $8.3B.
Sum of PV 5-year FCF ≈ $47.8B.

Terminal value in Year 5 = Year6 FCF ($13.2B) / (Discount 10% - Terminal 1.5%) = $13.2B / 8.5% ≈ $155.3B. PV of terminal = $155.3B / (1.10)^5 ≈ $96.5B.

Total intrinsic enterprise value ≈ $47.8B + $96.5B = **$144.3B**. Adjust for Oracle's net debt (\~$53B debt - $10B cash = $43B net debt as of 2024) gives equity value $101.3B. Divide by 2.82B shares = **$35.90 per share**. This would be an extremely pessimistic valuation. It's well below the current market price ($105), implying the market is not pricing such stagnation.

However, $35.9 is likely too low because Oracle's actual growth prospects are better than zero. Note this scenario essentially values Oracle as if it never really grows and barely keeps pace with inflation. Buffett's margin of safety is often applied to such a conservative scenario; here the current stock price (\~$105) is almost 3x this, indicating no margin of safety if one believed this stagnation scenario. But Oracle is actually growing faster, so we need a more reasonable scenario.

**Scenario 2: Average Growth (Past 10-year average)
**Over the last decade, Oracle's revenue CAGR was \~3-4%, but earnings and FCF grew a bit faster (due to buybacks and margin expansion). Let's assume Oracle can grow FCF at, say, **6% annually for next 5 years**, then taper to 2% terminal growth. 6% is closer to Oracle's recent organic growth (FY2023 was 17% due to Cerner, FY2024 6%; management expects double-digit in FY2025 with AI/cloud boost, but we'll moderate to 6% average).

-   Year 1 FCF \~ $12.5B, Year 5 FCF \~ $15.8B (growing 6% compound).

-   Terminal from Year6 (grow at 2%): Terminal value in Year5 = $15.8B \* (1.02) / (10% - 2%) = $16.1B / 8% = $201.25B, PV back 5 years = $201.25 / (1.10)^5 ≈ $125B.

-   PV of first 5-year FCF: Year1 $12.5B (PV $11.4B), Year2 $13.3B ($11.0B), Year3 $14.1B ($10.6B), Year4 $14.9B ($10.2B), Year5 $15.8B ($9.8B). Sum ≈ $53.0B.

Enterprise value ≈ $53B + $125B = **$178B**. Minus net debt $43B gives equity \~$135B. Per share = **$47.9**. That's still under half of the current price. The conservative 10% discount rate is weighing heavily. If Oracle truly can grow 6% for many years, one might argue a 10% required return is a bit high for such a stable, cash-rich business (some might use 8% for the discount, given low interest rates recently - but Buffett tends to stick to \~10% as a hurdle).

Even this scenario shows intrinsic value (\~$48) well below market \~$105. Does that mean Oracle is overvalued? Not necessarily - it may mean the market is pricing in higher growth or using a lower discount rate. Let's analyze expected return implied by current price:

**Current Market-Implied Expectations:** With the stock \~$105, let's see what discount rate would equate DCF to \~$105 using Scenario 2 growth. If we set margin of safety to 0% (i.e. accept current price as "fair"), what discount rate yields $105? We adjust the discount rate downward until the DCF equals \~$105.

At 8% discount rate, for example:

-   Terminal (in Year5) with 2% growth would be $15.8B \*1.02 / (0.08-0.02) = $16.1 / 6% = $268B, PV back5 at 8%: $182B.

-   PV of years1-5 at 8%: Year1 $12.5B/1.08 = $11.6B, Y2 $13.3/1.08^2=$11.4B, Y3 $14.1/1.08^3=$11.1B, Y4 $14.9/1.08^4=$10.9B, Y5 $15.8/1.08^5=$10.7B, sum $55.7B.

-   EV ≈ $55.7 + $182 = $237.7B, equity minus debt $237.7-43 = $194.7B, per share \~$69.

At 6% discount (which is probably too low to consider as a required return), EV becomes enormous:

-   Terminal at 6%: $16.1B/(0.06-0.02)= $16.1B/4%=$402.5B; PV back5 at 6% = $300B.

-   PV of 5yr flows at 6% ≈ $60B.

-   EV ≈ $360B, equity \~$317B, per share \~$112.

So at \~6% required return, the stock looks slightly undervalued (intrinsic \~$112 vs $105 price). At \~7% discount, we'd get intrinsic near current price.

Thus, the **implied expected return** for an investor buying at $105 assuming the scenario (6% growth, 2% terminal) is roughly in the 6.5-7.5% range. Indeed, solving for discount rate that gives \~$105 value yields \~**7%** expected return. This suggests the market is pricing Oracle like a quasi-bond with growth - investors may be willing to accept \~7% annual returns given Oracle's cash generation and current low interest rate environment.

Buffett's method, however, would demand at least 10% return plus a margin of safety (say valuing at 15% discount rate and then buying at 30% below that intrinsic value). Under those stringent criteria, Oracle would not qualify as a "buy" today - it's too popular and richly priced now. Back when Oracle was $45 and largely unloved (\~2017), it likely met those thresholds (which is why Berkshire bought then). But after doubling, the margin of safety is gone.

To further illustrate, **Margin of Safety** analysis: If our intrinsic value at 10% discount with 0% growth scenario was $36 and with moderate growth $48, even the optimistic scenario gave \~$70 at an 8% discount. Buffett often wants a 30-50% margin of safety - current price is way above those conservative values, meaning no margin of safety exists at current price under typical Buffett yardsticks.

However, one might argue we were too conservative in assumptions. Oracle's own guidance implies potentially higher growth (they forecast FY2026 revenue to be 16%+ cc growth, with cloud growth 40%+). If Oracle manages say 10% FCF growth for 5 years then 3% terminal, at 10% discount the intrinsic might rise significantly (quick calc: Year5 FCF \~$19B, terminal \~$272B PV \~$169B, plus PV of flows \~$57B, EV \~$226B, equity \~$183B, per share \~$65). Still below $105. Essentially, **to justify $105 using a 10% discount, Oracle would need something closer to 15% annual FCF growth for a decade - a very tall order for a company of its size.** The fact that the stock trades at $105 indicates investors are likely using a lower required return (7-8%) due to Oracle's stability or expecting sustained double-digit growth which they believe can last a long time (given the AI/cloud tailwinds Ellison touts).

**Conclusion on Intrinsic Value:** Using Buffett's traditional 10% discount rate and adding a margin of safety (30%), Oracle's **intrinsic value** would come out far below the current market price. For instance, even a somewhat optimistic DCF might yield \~$60 intrinsic, and a 30% margin of safety target buy price would be \~$42 - which is about where Oracle traded in 2016. In other words, from a pure value perspective, Oracle was a bargain back then, but not now after its rally.

Buffett often emphasizes quality of the business and growth prospects, so he might accept a lower yield for a stronger franchise. Oracle is a strong franchise, but perhaps he felt he didn't fully understand the cloud competitive dynamics, hence he sold. If one were to apply a slightly lower discount (reflecting lower interest rates and Oracle's strong moat), say 8%, intrinsic might be in the $70 range - still below market price (no margin of safety).

Thus, by **Buffett's method, Oracle's stock at \~$105 appears above the range a strict value investor would pay**. The expected return at $105 (with moderate assumptions) is around 7% annually, which may not meet Buffett's 10% hurdle.

For current Oracle shareholders, the **expected return** can be derived by finding the discount rate that sets DCF = $105. This appears to be around **7%** as mentioned. That 7% would be the approximate **equity IRR** from owning at this price, given moderate growth assumptions. If one required 10%, one would want to pay much less for the same cash flows.

It's important to note, however, that Oracle's future could surprise on the upside (e.g. if AI workloads drive much faster growth, or if margins expand more than assumed). If Oracle can grow FCF 10%+ for a decade (entering a new renaissance with cloud/AI), the intrinsic value under Buffett's method would be much higher - potentially justifying the current price or more. For instance, plug 10% growth for 10 yrs, 3% term, 10% discount: intrinsic per share might approach $100 (just roughly). So the market might be betting Oracle is at an inflection point where growth accelerates (thus compressing the payback period).

**Buffett's likely view:** Buffett prefers simple, predictable businesses. Oracle has some predictability (huge recurring support revenue), but also tech risk. Buffett did not hold on, implying he didn't see a wide margin of safety. Using his margin of safety principle, one could say *if* one demanded a 50% margin, they'd want to buy Oracle at \~$30 (which was feasible a decade ago but not now). Those who did are sitting on large gains.

In summary, **intrinsic value (DCF) estimates** for Oracle vary widely with assumptions. Under conservative Buffett-like assumptions (10%+ discount, low growth), intrinsic value is far below market price. The current price seems to be baking in either a lower required return or higher growth. If one strictly follows Buffett's method, Oracle would be considered **overvalued at $105** and not offering a margin of safety. Conversely, the implied **expected return at the current price** is around **7% per year** (in line with a bond-like equity). Investors may accept that given Oracle's competitive strengths and the lower yields available elsewhere, but it is lower than Buffett's typical 10% hurdle.

### **Company Assessment Based on Metrics**

However, the recent acceleration in revenue (+17% in FY23, +11% in latest quarter) combined with management's guided 40% cloud growth next year suggests Oracle's growth profile is markedly improving. If achieved, Oracle would transform from a slow-growth to a moderate/high-growth company, warranting a higher valuation multiple (which we've seen the market assign). Oracle's backlog (RPO $138B) indicates multi-year demand pipeline, reinforcing confidence in near-term growth.

-   **Cash Flow and Shareholder Returns:** Oracle is a cash cow. Even in leaner years, FCF was above $8B; it's now trending $15B+ for FY25. Oracle's conversion of earnings to free cash is excellent over time (e.g. in FY2024 net $10.5B, FCF $11.8B - slightly higher because of non-cash amortization and good working capital management). This indicates high earnings quality. Oracle has deployed cash extremely shareholder-friendly: $100B buybacks and dividends. This has significantly reduced share count and boosted per-share metrics. It also signaled management's confidence (they wouldn't heavily leverage and buy shares if they thought Oracle's business was deteriorating). The downside is elevated debt which introduces some risk (especially if interest rates rise or if new acquisitions are needed - Oracle's debt capacity is somewhat constrained now). But Oracle's step to slow buybacks post-Cerner and prioritize debt reduction is a prudent pivot to maintain financial strength. The new dividend hike to $0.50/qtr further underlines Oracle's abundant cash and commitment to return it to investors.

-   **Balance Sheet Risk:** Oracle's negative/low equity and high debt ratios might alarm a casual observer, but given Oracle's stable recurring revenue (over 70% of rev is recurring cloud/license support) and hefty cash generation, the leverage is manageable. Interest coverage \~4-5x is adequate. Oracle's plan to pay down \~$7B debt per year (which they did in FY2025) will, within a few years, bring debt to a more comfortable &lt;3x EBITDA and reduce interest burden. Essentially, Oracle intentionally optimized its capital structure for cheap debt usage; as rates have risen, they're easing back on that leverage. Thus the risk of financial distress is minimal barring an unexpected collapse in demand (which seems unlikely given enterprise IT's stickiness and Oracle's unique position in databases). In worst-case scenario, Oracle could cut buybacks or dividend to preserve cash - an option they clearly have (they are discretionary outflows).

-   **Valuation & Market Expectations:** Oracle's current valuation ratios (P/E \~30x non-GAAP EPS) show the market is pricing Oracle as a growth stock rather than a value stock now. That multiple is higher than peers like SAP (around 25x) or IBM (15x), reflecting expectation of superior growth. Considering Oracle's guided cloud growth and AI upside, the valuation can be justified if Oracle achieves sustained double-digit earnings growth. If growth falls back to low single-digits after a spurt, then the stock could be overpriced. But given backlog and product momentum, a period of above-average growth looks likely. Also, the market likely appreciates Oracle's increasing portion of subscription revenue (which merits higher multiples for reliability). With 77% of revenue now recurring (cloud + support), Oracle's earnings are more predictable than a decade ago (when license sales were lumpy). That lower risk plus growth could warrant a premium.

-   **Competitive Moat:** The metrics indirectly confirm Oracle's moat - consistently high margins and ROIC mean Oracle can fend off competitors and maintain pricing (customers rarely rip out Oracle databases; they pay the support). However, flat revenue for many years indicated some moat erosion or at least difficulty expanding (cloud up, but on-prem license down, etc.). The new growth spurt hints Oracle found new ways to leverage its strengths (e.g. synergy of database + infrastructure, integrated cloud apps). If those strategies hold, Oracle's moat will extend into the cloud era. The large backlog shows customers are committing to Oracle long term (including new cloud customers in ERP, infrastructure deals). That is a bullish sign regarding Oracle's competitive positioning.

-   **Management Efficacy:** The financial history depicts management adept at managing cycles. They didn't panic in low-growth years - instead, they doubled down on R&D ($8-9B/year consistently) and executed large buybacks at low prices (2015-2018). That capital allocation benefited shareholders massively when growth returned. The downside was increased debt, but as discussed, they are handling that. Management's forecasts (e.g. promising acceleration and delivering in FY2023-24) have been largely on target, which builds credibility. The big upcoming test is hitting that &gt;40% cloud growth in FY2026 - if Safra Catz delivers that, the market will likely reward Oracle further.

**Overall View:** Oracle today looks financially strong and on an upswing. It's a mature company that has reignited growth via strategic moves, all while maintaining enviable profitability. The trade-offs are a levered balance sheet and a stock price that already reflects some optimism. But the metrics show a clear improvement trajectory: revenue climbing, margins recovering, cash flows at record levels. Oracle appears to be *in the best financial shape it's been in years*, aside from the high debt (which is being whittled down). If I had to summarize: Oracle has transitioned from a **cash-rich, low-growth value stock** to a **cash-rich, moderate-growth compounder**.

Investors should watch how much of the backlog translates to revenue (execution risk in cloud deployment), whether margins continue to improve (cloud scale vs cloud price competition), and that debt reduction stays a priority. As of now, the trends are favorable: net margins expanding, FCF margin recovering, and debt metrics improving quarter by quarter.

Given these metrics, I feel **positively** about Oracle's fundamentals. The company's entrenched products ensure resilience (we saw even in tough times like FY2020, revenue only dipped 1%). Now with new growth drivers, Oracle is poised for a period of potentially significant earnings expansion, which could further improve valuation ratios (bringing P/E down if E grows). If those growth plans falter, the high valuation could be a risk. But the numbers provide confidence that Oracle's reinvention is succeeding.
