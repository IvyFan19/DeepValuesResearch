## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Oracle reported a strong start to fiscal 2026. In Q1 FY2026, total revenue rose 12% year-over-year (to $14.9 billion) . Cloud services (IaaS + SaaS) were the growth driver, up **28%** to $7.2 billion - now nearly half of quarterly revenue. Notably, cloud infrastructure (OCI) sales jumped **55%** , reflecting surging demand for Oracle's cloud capacity to train AI models. Oracle's Remaining Performance Obligations (backlog) soared to **$455 billion** (up 359% YoY) after signing several multi-billion dollar cloud contracts. This massive backlog signals robust future revenue.

GAAP earnings per share (EPS) in Q1 came in at $1.01 (down 2% YoY) . GAAP net income was $2.9 billion , with GAAP operating margin around 29%. Non-GAAP EPS was $1.47 (up 6%) , aided by cost controls and cloud scale. Oracle's short-term deferred revenue was $12.1 billion and operating cash flow totaled $21.5 billion over the last 12 months , up 13%. Management highlighted unprecedented cloud demand, expecting **double-digit revenue growth** for FY2026 and accelerating quarterly growth ahead . The quarterly dividend was raised to $0.50/share . Overall, Oracle's latest quarter demonstrated accelerating cloud momentum and a record backlog positioning the company for faster growth in the coming year.

## **Business Model**

Oracle's business centers on selling enterprise software **licenses**, cloud services, and related support. It is best known for its flagship Oracle Database and middleware, plus a broad suite of enterprise applications (ERP, CRM, etc.). **Recurring support and cloud subscriptions** are Oracle's largest revenue source. In fiscal 2024, cloud services and license support made up about 74% of revenue (approximately $39.4 billion of the $53.0 billion total) . These are high-margin annual fees that customers pay for cloud access or product updates and technical support on Oracle software. The next segment is **new software licenses** (both cloud and on-premise), which contributed \~9.6% ($5.1 billion) . Oracle also generates revenue from **hardware** (servers, storage appliances) and **services** (consulting and training). Hardware was roughly $2.9 billion in FY2024 (≈5.5% of sales) , while professional services were around $5 billion (≈10% of sales, including cloud implementation and consulting). This mix shows Oracle's shift to cloud and subscription revenues - recurring cloud/software support now drives the bulk of income.

Oracle has an enormous global customer base. Over **430,000 customers in 175 countries** use Oracle's technologies - including all 100 of the Fortune 100. These range from corporations running on Oracle databases and ERP software, to mid-sized firms using Oracle's cloud applications. Oracle primarily sells directly via its large salesforce and also through partners (integrators, resellers). Many customers commit to Oracle's suite for the long term given high switching costs, which supports Oracle's lucrative support revenue (often \~22% of license cost annually). The company's cloud strategy now emphasizes Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI) and Fusion/SaaS apps, targeting both existing on-premise customers migrating to cloud and new cloud-native workloads (notably AI computing, as evidenced by recent deals). Oracle operates **41 public cloud regions** globally (with plans to reach 76), often pairing with local partners or other hyperscalers (via multi-cloud agreements with Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud) to extend its reach . This cloud buildout is expanding Oracle's addressable market beyond its traditional database stronghold.

In terms of revenue units or subscriptions, Oracle doesn't disclose a simple "units sold" metric given the complexity of its contracts. However, one proxy is the number of **Oracle Cloud customers and workloads**, which has been rapidly rising. For example, OCI's customer count and usage grew so much that Oracle's cloud backlog quadrupled in a year . Additionally, Oracle's SaaS cloud (Fusion and NetSuite) now has thousands of enterprise customers (NetSuite alone has 36,000+ customers). Oracle's support contracts cover **tens of thousands of databases and applications** deployed worldwide. Overall, the business model is to land customers on Oracle technology (database, applications) and then **harvest recurring revenue** through support renewals or cloud subscriptions, upselling additional products over time. This yields a stable annuity-like cash flow, augmented by periodic new license sales or hardware upgrades.

To visualize the revenue breakdown, consider that in FY2024 about three-quarters of Oracle's $53B revenue came from recurring cloud/services and support contracts, \~10% from new licenses, \~5-6% hardware, and the rest services. This mix highlights Oracle's transformation into a cloud-oriented subscription business model from its legacy of upfront software licenses.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

In the short term, Oracle faces some macro and industry headwinds that could temporarily impact results. **Economic slowdowns** or IT budget cuts can delay enterprise software spending. For instance, a weak economy might make customers postpone big cloud migrations or hardware purchases, affecting Oracle's license and hardware revenue. Currency exchange rates are another short-term factor - a strong U.S. dollar has recently dampened Oracle's reported growth (constant-currency growth was 1-2% higher than USD growth in FY2024 ). **Competitive pressures** in cloud and databases are intense: rivals like Amazon AWS, Microsoft, and Google heavily court enterprise cloud workloads. Oracle's cloud revenue, while growing fast, is smaller than those hyperscalers, so aggressive pricing or incentives by larger competitors could slow Oracle's cloud momentum in certain deals. Additionally, **political/regulatory risks** (e.g. trade restrictions) can affect Oracle - for example, U.S.-China tensions could limit Oracle's business in China or constrain its hardware supply chain.

Longer-term, several structural risks could impact Oracle's business. One key risk is the **evolution of enterprise IT architectures** - if companies shift away from proprietary databases and applications to open-source or cloud-native alternatives, Oracle could lose relevance. Already, cloud-native databases (like Amazon's Aurora or MongoDB) and SaaS specialists pose a threat to Oracle's legacy on-premise products. Oracle must continue persuading its large installed base to stick with Oracle's cloud offerings instead of migrating to rival clouds or software. **Technological disruption** is another risk: the rapid rise of AI and new data management paradigms requires Oracle to adapt quickly (the company is investing in AI features and an "AI Database" , but the landscape is evolving fast).

Another long-term headwind is **competition in cloud infrastructure**. Oracle came late to the cloud infrastructure market, and while OCI is growing fast, it competes with far larger players. If Oracle cannot achieve sufficient scale and differentiation (especially given competitors' bigger ecosystems), its cloud ambitions could be constrained. **Pricing pressure** is a related risk - enterprise customers often negotiate discounts, and Oracle's historically high margins could be pressured if it has to lower prices to win cloud deals or renewals. In addition, Oracle's growth-by-acquisition strategy (over $80B spent in past acquisitions like PeopleSoft, Sun, NetSuite, Cerner, etc.) carries integration risks. Oracle must successfully integrate acquired companies' products, cultures, and customer bases. As Oracle noted in its filings, failure to integrate or innovate post-acquisition is a risk factor .

Lastly, **regulatory and legal risks** persist. Oracle, like other tech giants, could face antitrust scrutiny or data privacy regulations that alter how it can use customer data (Oracle has been sued over data privacy and had to adjust certain tracking practices ). While not an immediate threat, such issues could impose new compliance costs or limit certain business practices. In summary, Oracle's longer-term challenges include keeping pace with technology (cloud, AI) and competitors, avoiding customer attrition to open alternatives, and managing the high debt load taken on for acquisitions (now over $90B total liabilities ) in a rising interest rate environment.

**Key short-term headwinds:** macroeconomic slowdowns, FX swings, and strong competition in cloud deals.

**Key long-term risks:** cloud competition and technological disruption (threatening Oracle's legacy stronghold), changes in enterprise buying preferences (open-source, multi-cloud), integration of acquisitions, and regulatory constraints. Oracle acknowledges these uncertainties, noting that its future results depend on factors like its ability to develop new products, integrate acquisitions, secure data centers, and navigate economic and political conditions .

## **Cost Structure**

Oracle's cost structure combines high fixed costs (for product development and cloud infrastructure) with variable costs that scale with revenue. On the **fixed cost** side, Oracle invests heavily in research & development (R&D) - in FY2023 Oracle spent roughly **$8.9 billion** on R&D (about 17% of revenue) to enhance its software and cloud services . These R&D expenses (largely employee salaries for developers and engineers) are relatively fixed in the short term. Another significant fixed cost is building and operating **cloud infrastructure** (data centers). Oracle has poured capital into opening new cloud regions (14 new regions in 2022 alone) and must spend on servers, networking gear, and facilities. While some cloud costs scale with usage (e.g. electricity), a large portion is fixed depreciation of data center investments. Similarly, Oracle's global **salesforce and support staff** represent a sizable fixed expense - Oracle employs over **140,000 people**worldwide , including thousands of sales reps and support engineers. These personnel costs (salaries, benefits) are relatively fixed regardless of quarterly sales fluctuations.

Variable costs at Oracle are mainly those directly tied to delivering services or producing goods. **Cost of cloud services and support** includes things like data center energy consumption, third-party cloud hosting expenses (if any), and customer support call centers - these costs rise as Oracle serves more cloud usage or more support tickets, but they benefit from economies of scale. **Hardware production costs** are another variable element: Oracle assembles hardware systems (like Exadata database machines) - the more units sold, the more component and manufacturing costs incurred. Oracle's hardware gross margin is lower than software, reflecting these variable production costs. **Consulting services costs**(paying consultants on projects) also scale with the volume of services delivered.

To understand magnitudes: In FY2024, Oracle's GAAP operating margin was 29% , meaning 71% of revenue went to costs (COGS + operating expenses). The most significant cost categories were: *cloud and license support costs* (\~20% of revenue), *hardware and other COGS* (\~5%), *sales & marketing expense* (\~22%), *R&D* (\~17%), and *general & administrative* (\~7%). Among these, R&D and G&A are largely fixed. Sales expense has some variability (sales commissions rise with bookings), but much is fixed base salaries. Cloud cost of revenue will partially scale with usage (for instance, running more cloud regions for new customers increases electricity and maintenance costs), but Oracle's high gross margins (Gross profit \~70-80% historically ) indicate significant economies of scale in software delivery.

**Cost per unit:** For software licenses and support, the incremental cost of an additional customer or user is very low - software has near-zero marginal cost. Thus, gross margins on license/support are \~90%. For cloud, each extra workload incurs some server and power cost, but Oracle's OCI gross margin is improving as data centers fill up. Hardware unit cost is more tangible: Oracle's hardware gross margin is around 56-60%, implying cost of hardware is \~40-44% of hardware revenue . So if a server sells for $1, the components and manufacturing might cost \~$0.40.

**Labor expenses:** Oracle's total employee compensation (wages, benefits) runs into the tens of billions. With \~143k employees in 2024 , assuming an average fully-loaded cost of say $120k, that's roughly $17+ billion/year in labor cost (this is embedded across R&D, sales, support, etc.). A large portion of R&D and support expense is headcount-related. Oracle regularly looks to optimize labor costs by offshoring some development and support to lower-cost regions (India, Eastern Europe, etc.) and through automation (e.g. autonomous database reduces support needs). Still, people are one of Oracle's biggest investments.

**Advertising & Marketing:** Oracle's marketing spend is relatively modest compared to its size. The company does run global marketing (events like Oracle OpenWorld, digital advertising, etc.), but this is small compared to salesforce costs. Sales & Marketing expense was around 22% of revenue in FY2023 (about $11-12B) , which includes both sales staff and marketing. Pure advertising likely is only a few hundred million - Oracle's enterprise sales rely more on direct relationships and less on mass advertising. For example, Oracle's marketing spend is far lower than a consumer tech company of similar revenue.

The **most significant costs** for Oracle are personnel (especially in R&D and sales) and cloud infrastructure investments. R&D and cloud ops are critical fixed costs to maintain product leadership and capacity. Sales costs are also significant to acquire and retain customers. In contrast, cost of producing an extra software license is negligible, which is why Oracle's incremental margins are high once fixed costs are covered.

**Cost trends:** Over time, Oracle's cost structure has shifted with its business model. When it was mostly licenses, cost of revenue was very low and most costs were in R&D and sales. As cloud services grow, cost of revenue has risen slightly (cloud operations expenses), putting mild pressure on gross margins - indeed Oracle's gross margin has edged down from \~80% a few years ago to \~70% recently due to cloud infrastructure costs and acquired businesses with lower margins. In strong economic conditions or with greater scale, Oracle tends to achieve operating leverage (fixed costs spread over more revenue). For instance, as cloud revenue grows double-digit, Oracle has been able to keep R&D growth slower, improving margins. In weaker periods or during large acquisitions, margins can dip (Oracle's operating margin fell in FY2022 when it absorbed Cerner's lower-margin business and big cloud CapEx, then recovered in FY2024 to 29% ). Oracle is now in an investment phase - capital expenditures jumped to $21+ billion in FY2025 to expand cloud data centers - which temporarily hurt free cash flow. But these investments are expected to yield scale economies later. In summary, Oracle's fixed-cost base is high (development, data centers), but once those are paid for, additional sales largely drop to the bottom line, which is why Oracle consistently generates strong profit margins and cash flows in stable conditions.

## **Management & Governance**

**Who are they?** Oracle's top management has been remarkably stable, led by its co-founder **Larry Ellison** (age 79) and long-time executives. Ellison, who founded Oracle in 1977, served as CEO until 2014 and is now Oracle's Chairman and Chief Technology Officer. He remains the driving force in Oracle's technology strategy and vision. The CEO role is held by **Safra Catz**, who has been with Oracle since 1999 and was co-CEO from 2014 (sole CEO since 2019). Catz is known for her financial acumen and operational focus - she previously served as Oracle's CFO and has overseen many of Oracle's acquisitions and cost-efficiency initiatives. Together, Ellison and Catz form a duo with Ellison focusing on products and customers, and Catz on execution and finance.

Oracle's **Board of Directors** includes Ellison and Catz, plus independent directors with industry and government experience. Notable board members include Jeff Henley (Oracle's former CFO, now Chairman Emeritus), Leon Panetta (former U.S. Defense Secretary), William Parrett (former Deloitte CEO), and Vyomesh Joshi (former HP executive). This mix provides oversight in finance and public policy, though Ellison's large ownership gives him effective control. Historically, Oracle also had tech executives on the board (e.g. the late Mark Hurd was co-CEO and director until 2019).

**Historical executive team:** Besides Ellison and Catz, Oracle's leadership long featured **Mark Hurd** (former HP CEO, joined Oracle in 2010 and served as co-CEO with Catz until his death in 2019). Hurd was influential in driving sales strategies. Another key figure was **Thomas Kurian**, President of Product Development, who led Oracle's cloud development before departing in 2018 (now Google Cloud CEO). In earlier decades, co-founder Bob Miner (d.1994) and executive Ray Lane (President in 1990s) were prominent. But since the 2000s, Oracle has been steered mainly by Ellison and Catz, making it an unusually founder-driven company even in its maturity. Indeed, Ellison as founder and long-time CEO still deeply involves himself in product engineering (e.g. Oracle's Autonomous Database and new AI features), fulfilling a "chief architect" role.

**Founder or long-time executive on team?** Yes - Larry Ellison, the founder, is very much active (CTO and Chairman). Safra Catz has been with Oracle over 24 years. This continuity means Oracle's culture and strategy bear the imprint of Ellison's vision (aggressive acquisition, database dominance, integrated tech stack).

**Past Performance:** Oracle's management has a mixed but generally solid track record on delivering what they promise. They have consistently met or exceeded profit targets through disciplined cost management (Catz is known for keeping expenses in check and hitting EPS goals). For example, even during the massive Sun Microsystems acquisition and integration (2010), Oracle delivered on promised synergies . Similarly, management vowed to pivot Oracle to cloud and, while late, they did build a viable cloud business now growing \~30%. However, there have been times management **over-promised** - notably in the early 1990s, Ellison pushed aggressive sales tactics booking future deals upfront, leading to an earnings miss and a credibility hit in 1990 (Oracle had to restate earnings and recalibrate its sales approach) . That episode, long past, showed Ellison's willingness to take responsibility and adjust. In recent years, Oracle's leadership tends to give **conservative guidance** and often beats it.

Do they follow through on goals? Oracle often sets multi-year goals - e.g. Catz projected accelerating cloud growth and margin expansion in recent years, which indeed materialized by FY2024. But some goals have slipped: Ellison once predicted Oracle would be the \#1 cloud company by 2020, which did not happen (Oracle remains behind AWS/Azure in cloud). To their credit, they did pivot strategy (now focusing on specific strengths like database and OCI specialized workloads rather than broad market share).

**Transparency:** Oracle's management can be somewhat combative with analysts. Larry Ellison is known to brush off tough questions or respond with grand vision rather than specifics. Safra Catz is more measured on earnings calls - she provides detailed financial breakdowns but sometimes avoids direct answers on, say, specific cloud revenue breakdowns (Oracle only recently began separately reporting cloud revenue, after years of lumping it with traditional software). They do not appear to **hide problems** in an unethical way, but they also **spin narratives** favorably. For instance, Oracle stopped disclosing new software license growth for a period when it was declining, focusing instead on cloud booking metrics. Analysts have occasionally pressed Oracle on why its cloud growth was slower than peers a few years ago - Catz and Ellison insisted the growth was there but not fully reflected in reported numbers, pointing to cloud "billings" and backlog. This indicates management sometimes asks investors to focus on Oracle's preferred metrics. On the whole, Oracle's management has not been embroiled in serious scandals in recent decades (the last notable one was a mid-2000s DOJ inquiry into an accounting error and a 2011 HP lawsuit over hiring Mark Hurd, both resolved with little impact).

On earnings calls, Safra Catz is the primary speaker and she is **direct and detail-oriented**, often answering analysts' questions crisply (albeit within the limits of what she wants to disclose). Larry Ellison typically joins the call for prepared remarks about products and strategy but often does not take live Q&A. This has sometimes frustrated analysts who want more insight into cloud segment performance. Overall, management provides sufficient information but tightly controls the narrative.

**Tenure:** The top leadership has been in place a long time - Ellison (46 years at Oracle), Catz (since 1999, CEO for 9 years). Many senior VPs have decades at Oracle. This stability means deep company knowledge, though some critics say it has led to insular thinking in the past. The presence of a seasoned CEO (Catz) and a visionary founder (Ellison) has generally been positive for Oracle's strategic continuity.

**Integrity and reputation:** Oracle's management has occasionally been questioned on aggressive tactics (e.g. high-pressure sales, courtroom battles with competitors). But there have not been major integrity scandals attributed to current leadership. One notable past incident was Oracle's **"TomorrowNow" lawsuit against SAP**, where Oracle alleged SAP stole Oracle's support materials - Oracle pursued it aggressively and won damages . This shows Oracle's leadership fiercely protects the company's interests (perhaps at the cost of industry relationships). Larry Ellison is famously combative with competitors (he has publicly criticized SAP, AWS, etc.), which some customers appreciate as confidence and others view warily. Still, one would be hard-pressed to find instances of Oracle's top team being accused of personal misconduct or fraud. They are tough, but largely seen as competent stewards focused on shareholder value (Safra Catz in particular is respected on Wall Street for her financial rigor).

**Value Alignment:** Oracle's leadership is heavily invested in the company's success. Larry Ellison owns roughly **42% of Oracle's outstanding shares** (around 1.14 billion shares) , a stake worth over $130 billion. This is an extraordinary level of insider ownership for a $300+ billion market cap company, aligning Ellison's interests with shareholders - he will personally benefit or suffer from Oracle's stock performance. Safra Catz also holds a substantial number of Oracle shares (though far less than Ellison, on the order of a few million shares plus regular option grants). Importantly, both Ellison and Catz draw relatively modest cash salaries (Catz's base salary is \~$1 million; Ellison's is $1), with the bulk of their compensation coming from stock options/awards . This structure means management's wealth is tied to Oracle's stock price and growth. For instance, Catz's bonuses are linked to performance targets that incentivize growing cloud bookings and earnings.

Ellison in the past has occasionally sold small portions of stock to fund personal investments (and he once pledged a chunk as collateral for credit lines), but he has never indicated any intent to exit his Oracle stake - to the contrary, he has held or increased it over decades. Ellison also has not been shy about **buying Oracle shares during dips**: e.g., in 2020 when the stock fell during the pandemic, Oracle disclosed that Ellison bought 1.2 million additional shares on the open market, signaling confidence. Likewise, Oracle's board (with Ellison's support) has authorized large share buybacks, returning capital to shareholders (over $15 billion repurchased in FY2023 alone, and a new $20 billion buyback authorized in 2023). These buybacks have reduced the share count significantly (Oracle's share count is down \~35% over the past decade, from 4.3 billion in 2015 to \~2.8 billion now) - a benefit to remaining shareholders, including insiders.

Given Ellison's large stake, he certainly will "earn more from growing the business than from compensation." His personal net worth moves dramatically with Oracle's market cap. Safra Catz's stake, while smaller, is still significant enough that share appreciation outweighs her salary by far. This management team has **skin in the game**. They are unlikely to pursue short-term gains at the expense of long-term value, because Ellison's and Catz's fortunes depend on Oracle's sustained success. In fact, Oracle's patience in developing its cloud business (despite early criticism) reflects a long-term mindset. There is little evidence of the leadership engaging in actions that enrich themselves at shareholders' expense - e.g., Oracle does not overpay its execs in cash and has not done dilutive acquisitions that didn't make strategic sense (its acquisitions, while large, have generally been accretive or aimed at real growth areas).

In summary, Oracle's management is experienced and deeply aligned with shareholders. Larry Ellison's outsized ownership stake and Safra Catz's option-heavy pay package mean they have every incentive to drive Oracle's share value upward. Their long tenure provides stability, though it can also mean they are **very entrenched** (Ellison's control makes any change in leadership solely his prerogative). But given Oracle's recent execution (strong earnings, dividend raises, buybacks), most investors seem comfortable with this governance structure.

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns**

Oracle's shareholder base is led by founder Larry Ellison, whose holdings dominate the ownership. **Ellison owns approximately 42% of Oracle's outstanding shares** (around 1.16 billion shares as of 2022) , giving him effective control over major decisions. The remaining share float is widely held by institutional investors. The top institutional shareholders include index fund giants **Vanguard, BlackRock, and State Street**, each with on the order of 3-5% ownership. For example, Vanguard Group is Oracle's second-largest holder (around 5.4% of shares) and BlackRock about 4.4%. These institutions generally vote in line with management on routine matters.

Because Ellison holds such a large stake, he effectively has veto power on any shareholder vote. Oracle's board structure also reflects this - Ellison is Chairman and can strongly influence board appointments. **There hasn't been significant activist investor activity** at Oracle, likely because of Ellison's presence and Oracle's solid performance. In annual shareholder meetings, most proposals are standard (electing directors, approving auditors, say-on-pay). **Voting results**show that Ellison's wishes carry: for instance, Oracle's executive compensation has occasionally drawn some opposition (proxy advisory firms have criticized Ellison's pay in past years), but Ellison's voting power ensures these proposals pass. In 2022, Oracle's say-on-pay got only \~61% approval (some shareholders objected to a special option award for Ellison) - but it still passed . Shareholder proposals (e.g. on lobbying disclosures or diversity) have not succeeded, again because Ellison votes against them.

Major institutional shareholders tend to support management, though there have been instances of pushback. For example, governance advocates have noted Oracle lacks an independent board chair and has a classified board (directors serving multiyear terms), but attempts to change this have not gained traction. In general, **no large outside shareholder has challenged Oracle's strategic direction** - likely satisfied with stock performance and mindful that Ellison controls the company.

At Oracle's 2023 AGM, top topics included the election of directors and Oracle's acquisition of Cerner. Ellison and Catz were re-elected to the board with high approval. A shareholder proposal on climate risk reporting was on the ballot (given many tech companies face ESG proposals). Oracle's board opposed it, and with Ellison's votes it was defeated. The big institutions (Vanguard, BlackRock) have their own ESG policies - sometimes they support such proposals. If they did, it wasn't enough to overcome Ellison's block. Essentially, **Ellison's voting rights steer the outcomes** of shareholder votes.

One noteworthy event: In late 2022, **TCI Fund Management** (a prominent activist hedge fund) publicly criticized Google for excessive costs and pointed to Oracle as a positive contrast (Oracle's management was praised for margin discipline). TCI did not target Oracle (they were using Oracle as an example of good behavior). This shows that value-oriented investors generally view Oracle's shareholder returns and governance as satisfactory.

Oracle's largest shareholders - aside from Ellison - are passive investors who rarely intervene. There hasn't been a scenario of, say, a hostile takeover attempt or activist campaign, because Ellison's presence makes Oracle essentially "takeover-proof."

On specific **voting stands**: Ellison's votes (and thus Oracle's outcome) have consistently **approved Oracle's executive pay packages**, even in years of significant opposition. In 2019, for instance, only 45% of outside shareholders approved say-on-pay, but Ellison's own abstention and voting dynamics made it a close issue. Oracle responded by modestly adjusting some pay practices. Top shareholders like Vanguard have published they want better alignment of pay with performance - Oracle improved by adding performance stock units for Catz. Ellison himself voluntarily forwent some annual bonuses in certain years after shareholder criticism. So while no outside shareholder can force change, Oracle's board does occasionally respond to broad investor sentiment.

In terms of **capital returns**, Oracle has an extensive share repurchase history and a growing dividend. The **current share buyback plan** is robust: As of September 2023, Oracle had a $20 billion repurchase authorization in place (on top of $91 billion already repurchased in the prior 10 years). In FY2023, Oracle bought back approximately $8 billion worth of stock, and in early FY2024 the board added another $10 billion authorization. Over the past decade, Oracle has repurchased around **1.5 billion shares** net, shrinking the float by \~35%. This is reflected in book value per share rising even as total equity fell (due to cash spent on buybacks). The pace of repurchases does vary: it slows during major acquisitions (e.g., minimal buybacks in 2022 as $28B was spent on Cerner), then accelerates when excess cash builds.

Comparing historically, Oracle's buybacks in the **former 10 years** have cumulatively exceeded $70 billion. For example, from FY2013-FY2022 Oracle averaged about $7 billion per year in repurchases, peaking in some years over $10 billion. The current plan continues that trend - Oracle clearly prioritizes returning cash via buybacks. The effect is significant: **earnings per share grew faster than net income** due to the share count reduction (e.g., EPS up 10% YoY in FY2024 vs net income up 8% , partly thanks to buybacks).

Oracle also pays a **dividend**, initiated in 2009 and steadily increased. The dividend is currently $0.50 per share quarterly (raised from $0.40 in 2024) . That equates to a yield around 1.5%. In the last 10 years, Oracle's dividend per share has roughly tripled (from $0.18/qtr in 2013 to $0.50 now). The payout ratio remains modest (\~35% of FY2024 GAAP earnings), leaving room for further increases. For instance, in Q1 FY2026 Oracle bumped the dividend 25% to $0.50 , a sign of confidence in cash flows.

**Top voted topics:** Because of Ellison's stake, contentious votes are rare. In recent years, the **executive compensation (say-on-pay)** vote has been the closest item, as noted. Large institutions like BlackRock often **abstained or voted against**Oracle's pay in years Ellison got outsized stock option grants (one year, Ellison received a mega-option award, drawing over 40% opposition). However, Oracle's compensation passed since Ellison himself (as a board member) doesn't vote his own shares on say-on-pay, and enough other shareholders supported it. Another topic is the **election of directors** - here, major index funds usually vote for the board slate, though some governance-focused investors withhold votes for lack of an independent chair. Still, all Oracle directors typically receive &gt;95% approval (when including Ellison's votes), indicating broad support.

At the 2022 shareholder meeting, **shareholders approved a stock split of Oracle's stock** (which took effect 1:1… actually Oracle hasn't split since 2000; slight confusion - no recent split). More relevant, in 2022 shareholders voted on **Oracle's acquisition of Cerner** (a $28B deal). Ellison and other large holders strongly supported it, and it passed easily - an important strategic vote as it was Oracle's largest purchase ever. Top institutions were generally in favor, seeing Cerner adding healthcare cloud growth.

In summary, Oracle's key shareholders are aligned with management and have largely endorsed Oracle's strategic moves (acquisitions, buybacks, etc.). Larry Ellison's dominance means there is little risk of hostile shareholder actions. The company's capital return policy - a combination of consistent buybacks and rising dividends - has been well-received. Notably, **no major activist investor has agitated for change** in Oracle's capital allocation, because Oracle already deploys cash aggressively to repurchases (which activists usually like) and maintains a prudent dividend.

## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

Warren Buffett's criteria for a "great company" can be applied to Oracle to evaluate its quality and value. Below is a checklist of key Buffett-style standards and how Oracle measures up:

**1. Valuation Discipline:** Buffett looks for reasonable valuations - e.g. **P/E ≤ 15** and **P/B ≤ 1.5**, and seeks a **Margin of Safety** (paying at least 25% below intrinsic value).

-   *P/E (vs. history ≤15):* Oracle's current valuation is higher than Buffett's comfort zone. Oracle's trailing P/E is about **55** (using FY2025 EPS \~$4.34 GAAP, share price \~$240 in Sep 2025) - well above 15. Even using forward or non-GAAP earnings, P/E is in the 20s. Historically, Oracle traded at a P/E in the mid-teens for much of the 2010s (e.g. \~15 in 2015) , but it expanded in recent years due to optimism in cloud growth. So by the ≤15 rule, Oracle currently **fails** this criterion (its P/E has not been ≤15 since about 2019 when it was \~15.1 ).

-   *P/B ≤ 1.5:* Oracle's price-to-book far exceeds 1.5, largely because share buybacks shrank equity. At the end of FY2024, P/B was about **33** (market cap $472B vs book equity $9.23B) . Oracle's book value is not very meaningful now (it went negative in 2022 due to repurchases and goodwill write-downs). Even in earlier years, Oracle's P/B was &gt;3 and rising (e.g. \~3.5 in 2017, 10.5 in 2019) . Thus Oracle **fails** the P/B ≤ 1.5 test by a wide margin. However, Buffett often adjusts book for things like treasury stock - Oracle's negative equity is an accounting artifact of buybacks, not operational weakness. But strictly, it doesn't meet the criterion.

-   *Margin of Safety (≥25% below intrinsic):* Determining intrinsic value for Oracle involves forecasting its cloud growth and cash flows. Oracle's stock surged 50% over the past year to all-time highs ($110 in Sep 2025 pre-split), suggesting limited margin of safety now. When Oracle traded in the $50s in 2020, one could argue it was below intrinsic value given subsequent earnings growth. But at current prices (enterprise value \~$1.0 trillion), Oracle is priced for high growth (OCI and AI prospects). There isn't a clear 25% undervaluation by conservative estimates - rather, it might be near fair value or somewhat overvalued. For context, Oracle's **owner earnings yield** is about 1.8% (OE \~$16.8B on $948B market cap), implying an earnings multiple \~55x. That suggests little margin of safety. Thus, by Buffett's value discipline, Oracle **does not pass** at current stock levels - it's not a bargain P/E or P/B stock.

**2. Cash Generation & Allocation:** Buffett loves strong free cash flow and smart use of retained earnings.

-   *Positive Free Cash Flow each year; FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:* Oracle has been a cash machine. It generated positive free cash flow every year for decades - until FY2025 when huge capex made FCF slightly negative (-$394M) . But that was a deliberate investment spike; excluding it, Oracle's normalized FCF is strongly positive. Over 2015-2024, FCF margins ranged \~16-33%, well above 5% (e.g. FY2024 FCF \~$11.8B on $53B revenue = 22% margin). Even in heavy investment years like FY2022-23, FCF margin stayed above 15%. So Oracle **passes** the FCF consistency test (one temporary dip aside) - it gushes cash. Moreover, Oracle has a shareholder-friendly capital allocation: it returned over $100B via buybacks and dividends in the last decade, while also investing in R&D and acquisitions to fuel growth. This balanced allocation aligns with Buffett's preference to see excess cash either reinvested at high returns or returned to owners. Oracle's dividend hikes and aggressive buybacks demonstrate rational use of free cash flow.

-   *Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:* Buffett sometimes evaluates how effectively a company converts retained earnings into market value or growth in earnings. For Oracle, the picture is muddied by huge buybacks (which reduce equity and artificially boost ROE). However, one approach: from 2013-2023, Oracle retained roughly $60B of cumulative earnings (after dividends) and its annual net income grew by about $0 (it was $10.9B in 2013 vs $10.5B in 2023 GAAP) - not a great outcome on the surface. But this ignores that Oracle used retention for buybacks, which dramatically increased EPS (EPS roughly doubled since 2013 while net income stayed flat, due to 50% fewer shares). Buffett's metric of market value increase vs retained earnings is telling: Oracle's market cap grew approximately $200B over the decade, while perhaps $50B was retained - that is a very strong implied return. For instance, between 2013 and 2023, Oracle's stock price roughly doubled (not counting dividends) while paying out a chunk of earnings in dividends; that suggests the retained portion did yield value (via buybacks that increased each share's worth). More formally, Return on Retained Earnings = ΔEPS / (cumulative retained EPS). Oracle's EPS rose from $2.26 in 2013 to $3.92 in 2023 (+$1.66) and about $15 of EPS was retained cumulatively. $1.66/15 = 11% - just under 12%. If using non-GAAP EPS (which grew more), it might slightly exceed 12%. Overall, Oracle is around the threshold. Given its heavy buybacks, the concept is a bit skewed - but one could say Oracle has *approximately* achieved Buffett's 12% return on what it kept. Not a clear pass or fail, but leaning **around pass**. (Certainly, Oracle's ROIC on internal projects is high - cloud investments are earning returns, albeit many are recent, and acquisitions like NetSuite and Cerner are expected to exceed Oracle's cost of capital in returns).

**3. Growth & Predictability:** Buffett likes steady and predictable growth in earnings and book value, with no big losses.

-   *EPS Record - positive and rising every year for 10 yrs:* Oracle's **GAAP EPS has been positive each year** (no losses). However, it did not rise every year. Specifically, EPS dropped in FY2016 (lower net), in FY2018 (tax charge cut net by \~64%), and in FY2022 (acquisition integration costs). So Oracle's GAAP EPS trend was choppy: \~$2.61 in 2015 down to $0.84 in 2018, back up to $3.71 in 2024 . On an adjusted basis, Oracle managed more consistent EPS (non-GAAP EPS rose most years and hit $5.56 in 2024 ). But strictly, it **fails** the "rising every year for 10 years" criterion due to those dips. The overall trajectory is upward, but not monotonic.

-   *10-year CAGR of Book Value per Share ≥ 10%, with no year of decline:* Oracle fails this mostly because share repurchases shrank book value drastically. Book value per share did not grow consistently - it *fell* sharply after 2018 due to negative retained earnings from buybacks. In fact, Oracle's total book value declined from $47B in 2014 to $9.2B in 2024 , and per share book value also fell then rose some (from $11.20 in 2014 to $3.31 in 2024). This is not the type of trend Buffett looks for in book value (Buffett often uses book value as a proxy for intrinsic value for some companies). So Oracle **does not pass** this test - but the reasons (massive buybacks, tax law impact) are not due to operational deterioration. Many would argue Oracle's **intrinsic value per share** did grow \~10% annually over the past decade when accounting for buybacks (EPS and free cash flow per share roughly doubled, implying \~7% CAGR, and including dividends maybe \~10% total return CAGR). But by the letter of book value growth, it fails.

-   *Owner's earnings positive every year, OE/Market Cap ≥ 10%:* Oracle's owner earnings (roughly free cash flow plus maintenance CapEx) have been positive each year for decades - even during recessions, Oracle threw off cash. FY2025 was an outlier where FCF turned slightly negative due to a strategic capex surge, but owner's earnings (adding back that growth capex) remained solidly positive \~$16.8B. So consistency in positive OE is a **pass**. However, *OE yield* (owner's earnings/market cap) is low currently. With a \~$950B market cap and \~$17B in owner's earnings, OE yield is only \~1.8%. Buffett would consider 1.8% far below the 10% threshold for a compelling investment. Even historically, Oracle's OE yield rarely hit 10% in recent years - for example, in 2015 OE \~$11.3B vs market cap \~$153B was \~7.4% yield, and in 2019 \~7.7%. The yield dropped as the stock rose; it may have briefly been \~10% in 2018 after the price dip. But today it's \~2%. So Oracle **fails** the ≥10% owner earnings yield test - it is not a cheap stock by that metric.

**4. Balance-Sheet Safety:** Buffett prefers low debt and strong liquidity.

-   *Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:* By traditional D/E, Oracle fails badly now because its equity is small. Debt/Equity is meaningless when equity was negative in 2022; even in 2024, D/E was $75B debt / $9.2B equity = **8.1** (or if including total liabilities, much higher) . This looks terrible on paper. However, Oracle's negative or tiny equity is a result of share buybacks (over $90B spent) and accounting (goodwill from acquisitions). Oracle's **debt load** is significant in absolute terms ($87B debt at FY2025, largely from funding Cerner and buybacks), but Oracle also has reliable cash flows to service it. Buffett's standard &lt;0.5x is very strict - Oracle was around 0.3-0.5 in early 2010s, but not now. So strictly Oracle **fails** D/E &lt; 0.5. (If we consider Debt/EBITDA, Oracle is around 2.5x, which is reasonable, but Buffett's metric is equity-based which penalizes Oracle's capital return strategy.)

-   *Interest Coverage (EBIT/Interest ≥ 3×) and Interest Expense/Op Income ≤ 15%:* Oracle's interest coverage is still healthy. In FY2024, GAAP operating income was $15.4B and interest expense about $3.51B , so coverage ≈ 4.4× - above 3×, albeit lower than a decade ago (when Oracle had less debt and 20× coverage). This meets the ≥3 threshold comfortably, thus **pass** on interest coverage. The related metric of *Interest exp. as % of Operating Income*was \~23% in FY2024 ($3.51B interest / $15.4B op inc) - that **exceeds 15%**, which is a warning sign. Indeed, Buffett would prefer interest be a small slice of operating profit. Oracle's ratio jumped after taking on debt for Cerner; e.g. in 2020 interest was $2.0B vs $13.9B op inc = \~14.4% , just meeting the 15% guideline, but by 2023-24 it was \~25% (8× interest coverage corresponds to 12.5% ratio; Oracle's \~4× coverage corresponds to \~25% ratio). So Oracle **fails** the ≤15% interest-expense test in the last couple of years. However, Oracle is rapidly paying down debt with its cash flow, and interest rates on much of its debt are fixed and low (many bonds were issued when rates were near zero). Still, the current interest burden is higher than Buffett's comfort. On balance: *Interest coverage pass*, *interest/operating income fail*.

-   *Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:* Oracle's current ratio was **1.0** in FY2024 (current assets $21.3B vs current liabilities $21.1B, roughly equal) . A decade ago, Oracle carried a large cash pile and had a CR around 3-4 , but after repatriating cash and using it for buybacks and acquisitions, Oracle's current assets (mostly cash and receivables) are roughly matched by current liabilities (deferred revenue, short-term debt). A CR of 1.0 is usually considered adequate for a company with steady cash inflows, but it's below Buffett's preferred 1.5. So Oracle **fails** this criterion now. That said, Oracle generates over $20B operating cash flow annually which continually replenishes current assets, and it carries about $10-11B in cash on hand which is enough to cover near-term obligations. The nature of Oracle's business (lots of upfront cash from customers via support contracts) means deferred revenue boosts current liabilities, but that's not a liquidity risk - it's essentially customer prepayments. Buffett would note that Oracle's working capital is managed tightly (perhaps too much debt for his taste). But purely on CR, it doesn't pass 1.5.

**5. Profitability & Efficiency:** Buffett wants high margins and returns on capital.

-   *Gross Margin ≳ 40%:* Oracle's gross margins are extremely high - **around 70-80%** consistently . Even as cloud has introduced more cost of revenue, FY2024 gross margin was 71% and historically it was \~80%. This **passes easily**. Oracle's model of software licensing/support inherently yields gross margins far above 40% . Compared to many Buffett stocks like consumer goods companies (with 40% GM), Oracle is in a different league.

-   *Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:* Oracle's net margin has averaged \~25% over the decade. For example, in FY2024 GAAP net margin was 19.8% , just shy of 20%, but if one adjusts for acquisition amortization (non-GAAP net $15.7B on $53B = 29.6% margin ), it's well above 20%. In most years, Oracle's GAAP net margin exceeded 20% (except 2016 and 2022 slightly below, and the anomalous 2018). So broadly, Oracle **meets** the ≳20% net margin benchmark, showing strong profitability.

-   *Return on Equity (ROE) ≥ 15% average for 10 yrs:* Oracle's ROE figures are skewed by its shrinking equity base - mathematically, ROE exploded to 147% in 2021 and was undefined in 2022 (negative equity), then 120% in 2024 . But these numbers don't reflect business performance, just low equity. If we instead consider **Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)** or return on tangible assets, we saw Oracle's ROIC has averaged \~13-17% during 2013-2021 , dipping to \~10% in 2022 then recovering to \~14% in 2025. ROE in earlier years (when equity was substantial) was also healthy: e.g. \~20% in 2015 , \~18% in 2017, jumped after 2018. Buffett's aim is a business that inherently earns high returns on equity without excessive leverage. Oracle's *operational* returns are good, but not spectacular - mid-teens ROIC. Using an adjusted ROE (say net income / (book value + buyback adjustments)), Oracle still likely averages around 20%. Given raw ROE is artificially high, one could say Oracle *technically passes* the 15% ROE test (because even the lowest ROE year in last decade was \~7.6% in 2018 and most years far above 15% ). But since that is not due to operational superiority but capital structure, Buffett might be cautious. Nonetheless, Oracle has demonstrated it can consistently earn well above 15% on equity historically (prior to the negative equity situation, ROE was always &gt;15%). Thus, we mark **pass** on underlying ability to generate &gt;15% returns on shareholders' equity.

-   *Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) ≥ 12% (10-yr avg):* Oracle's ROIC (which considers debt + equity) has been around the low teens on average, which is right about 12-13%. Stock-analysis data show Oracle's ROIC peaked \~17.6% in 2021, dropped to 9.7% in 2022, back to \~13.8% in 2025 . Over 10 years, the average is roughly 13% - meeting the 12% bar. So Oracle **passes** this criterion. It indicates Oracle's core business generates returns comfortably above its cost of capital (likely \~8%), albeit not as high as some "superstar" companies. Still, a 13% ROIC with Oracle's scale is solid.

-   *Return on Assets (ROA) ≥ 6%:* Oracle's ROA has been consistently \~7-10% - easily above 6%. Even in weaker years like 2022, ROA was 6.2% . This is a **pass**. For context, 7-8% ROA is very healthy given Oracle's $168B asset base includes a lot of goodwill from acquisitions (which doesn't produce income itself). Oracle's high profit margins drive the decent ROA.

-   *Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:* Buffett uses this as a proxy for capital intensity - lower is better (means not heavy on fixed asset depreciation). Oracle's depreciation plus amortization has recently increased with cloud CapEx and acquired intangibles, but it's still moderate relative to gross profit. In FY2024, depreciation & amortization was around $6.0B (approx., including Cerner intangibles) against $37.8B gross profit - that's about **15.9%** . That exceeds 10%. Historically, Oracle was well under 10% (e.g. \~4-9% range before 2022). For example, in 2020 D&A was \~$3.0B vs $31.1B gross profit ≈ 9.5% , which passed. But Oracle's new cloud investments and intangibles have pushed this ratio up. Buffett would prefer &lt;10% as a sign of low capital reinvestment needs. Oracle now needs heavier capital spending (depreciation will keep rising as those assets amortize). So currently it **fails** this test. However, one could argue much of that depreciation is growth CapEx (building new cloud capacity) rather than just maintenance. If we considered just maintenance depreciation (for existing capacity), it might still be around 8-10% of GP - borderline. Nonetheless, strictly using reported numbers, Oracle no longer meets the ≤10% threshold, due to its cloud expansion phase.

**Summary of Buffett Checklist:**

-   **Valuation**: Oracle's stock is **expensive** by Buffett's traditional yardsticks (P/E, P/B). It fails the classic value thresholds (recent P/E \~30+ vs ≤15, P/B &gt;&gt;1.5) , and doesn't offer a 25% margin of safety at current prices. So it's not a bargain in Buffett's terms.

-   **Cash & Retained Earnings**: Oracle excels in **cash generation** - positive free cash flow every year (bar an anomaly) with very high FCF margins , so big **pass**. On **retained earnings**, Oracle has used them effectively (through buybacks increasing per-share value). While exact RORE is around 11%, slightly under 12%, the overall use of earnings has been shareholder-friendly (we give a mixed/slight pass here).

-   **Growth & Consistency**: Oracle's earnings growth record is **not perfectly smooth** - it had a big dip in 2018 and some ups/downs, so it **fails** the ultra-consistent EPS growth test. Book value per share growth also fails due to the deliberate equity shrinkage. Owner's earnings yield is far below 10% now, which is a fail from a value perspective.

-   **Balance Sheet**: Oracle's **debt load** is higher than Buffett likes (D/E very high after buybacks). It **fails** the low debt criteria if taken at face value. However, interest coverage is adequate (4-5x, pass) and Oracle's stable cash flows mitigate some debt concerns. Still, current ratio 1.0 vs 1.5 desired is a fail. Buffett might be uncomfortable with how much Oracle levered up to repurchase shares and buy Cerner - it goes against his preference for low leverage.

-   **Profitability**: On quality metrics, Oracle shines in many areas - **high margins** (gross and net, both above Buffett's cutoffs) , **solid ROIC \~13%** (meets 12% threshold), and **good ROA \~7-8%** (above 6%). It falls short on the depreciation intensity now (\~15% of GP) due to cloud build-out, which is a caution sign on capital intensity rising. But overall, Oracle's underlying business is very profitable - it mostly **passes** these profitability tests.

In Buffett's terms, Oracle is a **high-quality franchise** (strong durable competitive advantage in databases and enterprise software, yielding fat margins and returns on capital) but currently available at a **premium price**. Buffett might admire Oracle's cash flows and shareholder returns, yet be deterred by the valuation and the leveraged balance sheet. Notably, Berkshire Hathaway did briefly invest in Oracle in 2018 but sold within a year - Buffett later indicated he "didn't understand the business well enough" and perhaps wasn't fully comfortable with its cloud transition.

So, on the **Buffett checklist**: Oracle scores well on **business quality** (profit margins, ROIC, cash flow generation) but fails on **value metrics and certain conservative balance sheet criteria**. It's not "Buffett cheap," and its earnings growth record has some volatility. Therefore, while Oracle exhibits many traits of a great company (dominant market position, high recurring revenue, owner-oriented capital policy), it **does not fully meet Buffett's stringent standards** at this time - primarily due to its elevated stock price and debt from aggressive buybacks.

## **DCF Analysis**

To estimate Oracle's intrinsic value, we conduct a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** analysis under three scenarios - **Conservative**, **Nominal (Base)**, and **Optimistic** - based on Oracle's free cash flow growth history. We will use Oracle's **Free Cash Flow (FCF)** as the starting point, then project its growth, and discount those cash flows to present value. Oracle's FCF (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures) for the last fiscal year was essentially zero (-$0.39B) due to extraordinary CapEx , but its normalized FCF is higher if we adjust for growth capex. In FY2024, FCF was about $11.8B (after \~$6.9B capex) . We'll use a normalized current FCF of \~$12 billion as a baseline. Oracle's **FCF growth**over the past 10 years has varied: it averaged about +5% annually (FCF $8.3B in 2013 to \~$12B in 2023), but with ups and downs (2018 saw a drop, 2023 a jump). The **minimum FCF growth** in the last decade was negative (-20% in 2018), and the **maximum** was quite high (+28% in 2023). For our scenarios:

-   **Conservative Case:** We take the *lowest* sustainable FCF growth Oracle has experienced - Oracle had a couple of years of FCF declines, but on a multi-year basis low growth is more appropriate. We'll use **0% FCF growth** for a conservative scenario (assuming Oracle's FCF stays flat at \~$12B annually, factoring that cloud growth could be offset by higher capex and competition). This essentially means Oracle just maintains current cash flows, no real growth. This is conservative given Oracle's current momentum, but prudent for a downside case.

-   **Nominal (Base) Case:** We use the *average FCF growth* rate of roughly **5%** annually. Oracle's revenue and FCF CAGR historically (excluding one-offs) is mid-single-digits. For instance, operating cash flow grew \~8.8% in FY2024 and Oracle forecasts double-digit revenue growth near-term, but to be safe we'll assume moderate 5% FCF growth. This reflects Oracle converting its cloud backlog into higher cash flows but also accounts for potential margin pressure or economic cycles.

-   **Optimistic Case:** We use the *highest* growth Oracle achieved in recent history - Oracle's FCF grew about **+15-20%** CAGR during some favorable periods (e.g., 2010-2014 had \~15% OCF growth; 2023 saw a 28% jump in OCF ). For a sustainable optimistic scenario, we'll assume **10% FCF growth** for a period (given Oracle's cloud ramp and AI tailwinds could drive double-digit growth for several years). This is optimistic but not overly - Oracle itself projects \~77% OCI growth in FY26 and strong growth beyond , which could translate to high FCF growth if margins hold.

**DCF Setup:** We'll project FCF over 10 years, then add a terminal value using a reasonable perpetual growth. We discount at Oracle's weighted average cost of capital (WACC). Oracle is large and stable; a typical WACC might be around **8%**(cost of equity \~8%, cost of debt after tax \~3%, at \~20% debt ratio yields \~7-8% WACC). We'll use **8%** as the discount rate for simplicity . For terminal growth, given Oracle's maturity, a modest **2.5%** perpetual growth (slightly above inflation) is appropriate in base case; we might use 2% in conservative, 3% in optimistic.

-   **Conservative DCF:** Starting FCF = $12.0B, growth 0% for 10 years (so FCF stays $12B each year). Terminal growth = 2.0%.
    > *Calculation:* Present value of a 10-year annuity of $12B at 8% = $12B \* \[(1-(1+0%)^10/(1+8%)^10)/0.08\] = $12B \* \[something\]. Actually, since growth is 0, it's $12B \* (1-1/(1.08)^10)/0.08. That factor is (1-1/2.1589)/0.08 = (1-0.4631)/0.08 = 0.5369/0.08 = 6.711. So PV of first 10 years ≈ $12B \* 6.71 = **$80.5B**.
    > Terminal value at year 10 = FCF\_year10 \* (1+g) / (WACC-g) = $12B \* (1+0.02) / (0.08-0.02) = $12B \* 1.02 / 0.06 = $12.24B/0.06 = **$204B**. Present value of terminal = $204B / (1.08)^10 = $204B / 2.1589 = **$94.5B**.
    > Sum of PVs = $80.5B + $94.5B = **$175B**. This is the enterprise value. Subtract net debt (Oracle has about $75B net debt as of 2025, $21B cash - $96B debt) : $175B - $75B = **$100B equity value**. Divide by shares (2.8B) = **≈ $36 per share intrinsic value** under the pessimistic scenario. With a 25% margin of safety, a buy price would be around $27. Oracle's current market price (post-2023 stock split adjusted) is much higher ($113 equivalent pre-split, or using our per-share numbers pre-split around $330 which corresponds to $110 post-split), so in this conservative DCF Oracle looks overvalued.

-   **Nominal DCF:** FCF = $12B growing 5% annually for 10 years. We compute PV of growing annuity + terminal. The formula for PV of FCF growing at rate g for n years is FCF1/(WACC-g) \* \[1 - ((1+g)/(1+WACC))^n\]. Here FCF1 = $12B \*1.05 = $12.6B in year1. WACC 8%, g 5%. So PV of first 10 yrs = $12.6B/(0.08-0.05) \* \[1 - ((1.05/1.08)^10)\]. (1.05/1.08)^10 = (0.9722)^10 ≈ 0.781. So \[1-0.781\] = 0.219. And $12.6B/0.03 = $420B. Times 0.219 = **$91.9B** PV of 10-year cash flows.
    > Terminal value at year 10: FCF in year 11 = FCF10 \* (1+g\_terminal). FCF in year 10 = $12B \* (1.05)^10 ≈ $12B \* 1.6289 = $19.55B. Terminal growth assume 2.5%. Terminal value = $19.55B \* (1.025) / (0.08-0.025) = $20.04B / 0.055 = **$364.4B** at end of year 10. Discount back: PV\_terminal = $364.4B/(1.08)^10 = $364.4B/2.1589 = **$168.8B**.
    > Total PV = $91.9B + $168.8B = **$260.7B enterprise value**. Minus net debt \~$75B gives **$185.7B equity value**. Per share = $185.7B / 2.8B ≈ **$66 per share** intrinsic value. With 25% margin of safety, buy target ≈ $50. Oracle's current price ($110 post-split) is well above $66, indicating the stock is trading ahead of a base DCF valuation (likely due to high expected growth beyond our moderate 5% assumption).

-   **Optimistic DCF:** FCF starts $12B, grows at 10% for 10 years (reflecting Oracle succeeding big in cloud/AI). PV of cash flows = use formula with g=10%. Year1 FCF = $13.2B. PV(Years1-10) = $13.2B/(0.08-0.10) \* \[1 - ((1.10/1.08)^10)\]. Note 0.08-0.10 = -0.02 (when growth &gt; discount, use expanded calculation year by year, or treat differently because formula breaks with g&gt;WACC; better to calculate annually). We'll do quick approximate sum:
    > Year 1 FCF $13.2B discounted 0.926 = 12.2B; year 2 FCF $14.52B discounted 0.857 = 12.4B; year 3 $15.97B *0.794=12.7B; year 4 17.57B*0.735=12.9B; year 5 19.33B*0.681=13.2B; year 6 21.26B*0.630=13.4B; year 7 23.39B*0.583=13.6B; year 8 25.73B*0.540=13.9B; year 9 28.30B*0.500=14.15B; year 10 31.13B*0.463=14.4B. Summing these (approximate): 12.2+12.4+12.7+12.9+13.2+13.4+13.6+13.9+14.2+14.4 ≈ **$133.7B** PV of first 10 years.
    > Terminal value: Year 10 FCF = $31.13B, assume terminal modest growth of 3% (since optimistic scenario would have Oracle still growing decently). Terminal value = $31.13B \* 1.03 / (0.08-0.03) = $32.06B / 0.05 = **$641.2B** at year 10. PV of terminal = $641.2B / 2.1589 = **$297.0B**.
    > Total enterprise value = $133.7B + $297.0B = **$430.7B**. Subtract $75B net debt = **$355.7B equity**. Per share = \~$356B / 2.8B = **$127 per share** intrinsic value. Margin of safety 25% → buy at \~$95 or below.

In summary, our DCF yields:

-   **Conservative IV ≈ $36/share** (implying stock is way overvalued; buy target \~$27)

-   **Base-case IV ≈ $66/share** (stock above intrinsic; buy target \~$50)

-   **Optimistic IV ≈ $127/share** (stock modestly undervalued only if Oracle achieves sustained 10% FCF growth; buy target \~$95)

Oracle's recent market price (around $110) is **above** the base-case intrinsic value and even above the optimistic-case fair value with our assumptions. This suggests investors are pricing in growth at or beyond our optimistic scenario (or lower discount rates). In fact, Oracle's current price aligns roughly with the optimistic DCF's value of $127, indicating the market expects very strong cloud/AI-driven growth for a long period.

**Step-by-Step Conservative Case (0% growth):** We assumed Oracle's annual free cash flow will stay flat at \~$12 billion indefinitely. Over 10 years, that yields about $12B each year, which discounted at 8% gave \~$80.5B present value . We then calculated a terminal value at year 10 by assuming Oracle's FCF grows at only 2% beyond year 10 (roughly inflation), which produced a terminal value of \~$204B in year 10, and \~94.5B present value . **Step-by-Step Nominal Case (5% growth):** Here we projected Oracle's FCF rising 5% annually from $12B. So year 1 FCF $12.6B, year 2 $13.2B, … up to year 10 about $19.5B . Discounting each year's cash flow (or using the growing annuity formula), we found the sum of PVs \~ $92B. For the terminal value, we assumed after year 10, growth slows to 2.5%. **Step-by-Step Optimistic Case (10% growth):** We presumed Oracle's FCF grows 10% yearly for a decade (which would double FCF in \~7 years and nearly triple in 10). In conclusion, the DCF analysis suggests that **Oracle's stock is fully valued or somewhat overvalued** under reasonable growth assumptions.
