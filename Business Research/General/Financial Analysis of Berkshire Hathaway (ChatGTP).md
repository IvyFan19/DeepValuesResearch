## **Background Research**

### **Business Model and Income Sources**

Berkshire Hathaway Inc. is a diversified holding company owning dozens of subsidiaries across insurance, freight rail (BNSF Railway), energy (Berkshire Hathaway Energy), manufacturing, services, and retailing ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=Berkshire%20Hathaway%20Inc,the%20company%27s%20reported%20financial%20performance)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=Manufacturing)). Its income streams are correspondingly broad. The largest source of revenue is insurance (GEICO and several primary/reinsurance units), which in recent quarters contributed about 26% of revenue and nearly half of pre-tax earnings ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=,includes%20investment%20income)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=Revenue%20grew%20by%2018.9,of%20earnings)). Other major segments include manufacturing (\~21% of revenue, \~25% of profit), the **BNSF** railroad (\~6% of revenue, \~13% of profit), **Berkshire Hathaway Energy** (utilities, \~8% of revenue) and service/retail operations (\~11% of revenue) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=Berkshire%27s%20manufacturing%20businesses%20fall%20into,building%20products%2C%20and%20consumer%20products)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=Service%20and%20Retailing)). In 2023, Berkshire also consolidated Pilot Travel Centers (\~14% of revenue) after increasing its ownership to 100% ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=A%20controlling%20interest%20in%20PTC,of%20EBT)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=A%20controlling%20interest%20in%20PTC,of%20EBT)). The company also holds a **vast equity investment portfolio** (notably a \~Apple stake and others) which produces investment income and unrealized gains/losses that affect reported net earnings ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=company%20whose%20subsidiaries%20engage%20in,the%20company%27s%20reported%20financial%20performance)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=Investment%20and%20Derivative%20Gains%20and,Losses)).

([<u>BERKSHIRE HATHAWAY INC</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/feb2225.pdf#:~:text=Earnings%20of%20Berkshire%20Hathaway%20Inc,96%2C223%20Net%20earnings%20includes)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=Revenue%20grew%20by%2018.9,of%20earnings)) *Figure: Berkshire's operating earnings by segment (Full-Year 2024 vs 2023). Insurance underwriting and investments provide the largest share of profit, followed by the railroad (BNSF), energy, and the diverse manufacturing/service/retail businesses ([<u>BERKSHIRE HATHAWAY INC</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/feb2225.pdf#:~:text=Investment%20gains%2Flosses,36)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=Revenue%20grew%20by%2018.9,of%20earnings)).*

**Customers and Units:** Berkshire's subsidiaries collectively serve tens of millions of customers. For example, GEICO is one of the largest auto insurers in the U.S., with **around 16 million policies in force** as of 2023 ([<u>GEICO ends 2023 with a profit</u>](https://coverager.com/geico-ends-2023-with-a-profit/#:~:text=Auto%20policies,policies%20according%20to%20our%20estimates)). BNSF Railway serves **thousands of shippers** and transported over **9 million carloads** in 2023 ([<u>BNSF Railway's carloads 2014-2023 - Statista</u>](https://www.statista.com/statistics/547952/carloads-of-bnsf-railway/#:~:text=BNSF%20Railway%27s%20carloads%202014,carloads%20in%20the%20previous%20year)), moving \~40% of U.S. long-distance freight by volume. Berkshire Hathaway Energy's regulated utilities provide power and gas to **approximately 5.3 million retail customers** across numerous states ([<u>printmgr file</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2024ar/2024ar.pdf#:~:text=regulated%20energy%20interests%20are%20comprised,and%20investments%2C%20and%20a%20liquefied)). The retail businesses (e.g. See's Candies, Duracell, Nebraska Furniture Mart) and service companies (e.g. NetJets fractional aviation) add millions more end-users. In sum, Berkshire's conglomerate structure yields a highly diversified revenue base rather than a single subscription or unit-sales metric.

### **Short-Term vs Long-Term Headwinds and Risks**

**Short-term (temporary) headwinds** include macroeconomic and market fluctuations that impact Berkshire's cyclical businesses and investment portfolio. In the near term, **high inflation and rising interest rates** can pressure certain operations - for example, higher claims severity in insurance and higher financing costs for utility and railroad capital expenditures ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway faces headwinds as shareholders look to its future \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/warren-buffetts-berkshire-hathaway-faces-headwinds-shareholders-look-its-future-2021-05-03/#:~:text=With%20unprecedented%20government%20stimulus%20and,rising%20consumer%20prices%2C%20Smead%20said)). An economic slowdown or recession would temporarily soften volumes at BNSF (freight shipments fell in 2023 amid weaker demand) and retail sales. Geopolitical events or supply chain disruptions could also transiently affect manufacturing subsidiaries. Market volatility is a notable short-term factor: due to an accounting rule change in 2018, Berkshire must report unrealized gains/losses on equity holdings in earnings, causing "wild and capricious swings" in GAAP profits ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=However%2C%20including%20losses%20of%20%2429,4%20billion)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=While%20the%20company%20generated%20solid,large%20decline%20in%20Apple%20stock)) - as seen in 2022 when a stock market decline produced a $-22.8B net loss, and in 2023 when a rebound drove net $97B profit ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway Financial Performance from 1995 to 2024</u>](https://www.scienceagri.com/2023/08/warren-buffett-and-berkshire-hathaway.html#:~:text=In%20the%20performance%20aspects%20of,22%20billion%20USD)). These swings, however, are mostly paper fluctuations rather than operating performance changes.

**Long-term (structural) risks and challenges:** A key long-run concern is **management succession**. Warren Buffett (94) and Charlie Munger (died in 2023) have led Berkshire for decades; their eventual departures raise uncertainty about whether successors (vice-chairs Greg Abel and Ajit Jain) can maintain the same investing acumen and decentralized culture. That said, Buffett has designated Greg Abel to succeed him as CEO and slowly shifted more responsibilities to Abel and Jain since 2018 ([<u>Jain Successor Is Recurring Berkshire Board Topic; Leadership ...</u>](https://www.carriermanagement.com/news/2024/05/06/261793.htm#:~:text=Jain%20Successor%20Is%20Recurring%20Berkshire,crowd%20assembled%20in%20Omaha%2C%20Neb)) ([<u>Buffett's designated successor Greg Abel boosts Berkshire stake \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/buffetts-designated-successor-greg-abel-boosts-berkshire-stake-2022-10-03/#:~:text=Oct%203%20%28Reuters%29%20,Energy%20unit%20for%20%24870%20million)). Another structural risk is **size and growth constraints** - Berkshire's massive capital base (\~$950B assets) makes it harder to grow at high rates or find acquisitions "big enough to move the needle." Buffett himself has noted it's "gotten harder" to compound at past rates ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway faces headwinds as shareholders look to its future \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/warren-buffetts-berkshire-hathaway-faces-headwinds-shareholders-look-its-future-2021-05-03/#:~:text=Investors%20have%20long%20been%20happy,induced%20slump)). In insurance, **climate change** poses a long-term risk by increasing catastrophe losses and uncertainty in pricing property coverage. Berkshire's heavy insurance exposure (GEICO, Gen Re, etc.) means more frequent severe weather events could erode underwriting profits over time. Similarly, BNSF faces secular risk from shifts in energy and transportation: for instance, the decline of coal shipments (historically \~20% of BNSF's volume) and competition from trucking (e.g. if autonomous trucks improve economics) are long-term challenges. **Regulatory and political** changes could also impact Berkshire's businesses - e.g. new insurance regulations, railroad safety or pricing rules, or tax law changes (the 2017 US tax cut significantly boosted Berkshire's net income that year ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway Financial Performance from 1995 to 2024</u>](https://www.scienceagri.com/2023/08/warren-buffett-and-berkshire-hathaway.html#:~:text=2017))). Another permanent factor is **market risk in the equity portfolio**: Berkshire's \~$350B stock portfolio (with big holdings like Apple, Bank of America) means the company's book value and comprehensive income are tied to market fortunes of those firms ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=company%20whose%20subsidiaries%20engage%20in,the%20company%27s%20reported%20financial%20performance)) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=While%20the%20company%20generated%20solid,and%20energy%20segment%20this%20quarter)). A major, prolonged market downturn would weigh on Berkshire's intrinsic value. Overall, while Berkshire's diversification provides resilience, its size and certain sector exposures (insurance, utilities, rail) mean it is not immune to broad economic or industry headwinds.

### **Cost Structure: Fixed vs Variable Costs**

Given its conglomerate nature, Berkshire's cost structure varies by segment. **Insurance operations** have a large variable cost component in claims payouts - for example, GEICO's biggest expense is losses and loss-adjustment expenses (which rise with accident frequency/severity). GEICO also historically spent heavily on marketing (over $1 billion annually), though it cut advertising 16.5% in 2023 (to **$838 million**, a 14-year low) to improve profitability ( [<u>Geico shows 'spectacular' profitability in 2024 but lackluster growth \| S&P Global</u>](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/geico-shows-spectacular-profitability-in-2024-but-lackluster-growth#:~:text=largely%20reflect%20the%20company%27s%20reacceleration,ratio%20for%20Progressive%27s%20direct%20auto) ). Underwriting costs like commissions fluctuate with premium volume. **Railroad (BNSF)** has high fixed costs in its network - owning 32,500 miles of track, locomotives, yards, etc. - which require constant maintenance. BNSF's major variable costs are fuel and crew wages: diesel fuel alone was \~80% of BNSF's greenhouse emissions and a significant operating expense ([<u>printmgr file</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2024ar/2024ar.pdf#:~:text=Consumption%20of%20diesel%20fuel%20by,electric%20and)) ([<u>printmgr file</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2024ar/2024ar.pdf#:~:text=Competition%20The%20business%20environment%20in,The%20potential%20expansion%20of%20longer)). In 2023, volume declines helped reduce BNSF's operating expenses by \~2.5%, offsetting some revenue drop ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway's Q4 Earnings and Revenues Rise Year Over Year \| Nasdaq</u>](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/berkshire-hathaways-q4-earnings-and-revenues-rise-year-over-year#:~:text=Full)). **Utilities (BHE)** similarly have heavy fixed asset depreciation and interest costs, while fuel and purchased power are variable. **Manufacturing and retail** units (like Precision Castparts, Duracell, Dairy Queen, etc.) incur variable costs for raw materials (steel, lumber, etc.), labor, and distribution that move with sales, as well as fixed overhead for factories and facilities.

Across Berkshire, **labor expense** is significant - the conglomerate employs about *383,000 people* (2022 figure) - but decentralized. Some units have unionized workforces with relatively fixed wage schedules (e.g. BNSF's union labor agreements led to a $290 million charge in 2024 for a new contract ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway's Q4 Earnings and Revenues Rise Year Over Year \| Nasdaq</u>](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/berkshire-hathaways-q4-earnings-and-revenues-rise-year-over-year#:~:text=Costs%20and%20expenses%20decreased%201.7,and%20by%20increased%20litigation%20accruals))), while others have more flexible staffing. **Advertising and marketing** spend is notable in a few subsidiaries (GEICO, for one, is famous for its ad campaigns), but many Berkshire businesses (utilities, industrial manufacturers) spend minimally on advertising. Berkshire's largest single cost item in 2024 was likely insurance losses and loss-adjustment expenses (tens of billions), followed by cost of goods sold in manufacturing, then compensation.

**Most significant costs:** In insurance, claim payouts (and claim reserves) and underwriting expenses drive the combined ratio; in 2023 GEICO dramatically improved its loss ratio to 81% (from 93% in 2022) by reducing accidents and prior-year claim estimates ([<u>GEICO ends 2023 with a profit</u>](https://coverager.com/geico-ends-2023-with-a-profit/#:~:text=GEICO%E2%80%99s%202023%20earnings%C2%A0reflected%20higher%20average,a%20reduction%20in%20advertising%20costs)) ([<u>GEICO ends 2023 with a profit</u>](https://coverager.com/geico-ends-2023-with-a-profit/#:~:text=Underwriting%20expenses%20declined%20%24752%20million,expenses%20and%20improved%20operating%20leverage)). In BNSF, fuel and compensation accounted for the bulk of its \~$17B operating costs. BHE's major costs include fuel for generation and maintenance of power infrastructure. Raw material inputs are critical for manufacturers like **Clayton Homes** (which uses lumber and materials to build \~51,000 homes annually) ([<u>printmgr file</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2024ar/2024ar.pdf#:~:text=Clayton%20Homes%2C%20Inc,services%20and%20competes%20on%20price)) ([<u>printmgr file</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2024ar/2024ar.pdf#:~:text=Access%20to%20key%20housing%20inputs%2C,by%20low%20supplies%20of%20pre)) or **Marmon** and **Shaw** (which rely on steel, resins, etc.). These input costs can be volatile with commodity markets. For instance, Shaw Industries (flooring) noted resin and fiber prices can swing and are factored into pricing ([<u>printmgr file</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2024ar/2024ar.pdf#:~:text=Clayton%20Homes%2C%20Inc,services%20and%20competes%20on%20price)) ([<u>printmgr file</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2024ar/2024ar.pdf#:~:text=Access%20to%20key%20housing%20inputs%2C,by%20low%20supplies%20of%20pre)).

**Cost trends:** Berkshire's decentralized model lets each business manage its expenses according to economic conditions. During downturns, variable costs typically fall (e.g. lower fuel use when BNSF hauls less freight, or GEICO's claims dropping when drivers travel less). Some businesses have demonstrated flexible cost management: GEICO aggressively cut $752 million in underwriting expenses in 2023 (by reducing ad spend and headcount \~20%) to restore profitability ([<u>GEICO ends 2023 with a profit</u>](https://coverager.com/geico-ends-2023-with-a-profit/#:~:text=Underwriting%20expenses%20declined%20%24752%20million,expenses%20and%20improved%20operating%20leverage)) ([<u>GEICO ends 2023 with a profit</u>](https://coverager.com/geico-ends-2023-with-a-profit/#:~:text=share%20of%2013.8,market%20share%20in%202021)). However, fixed costs provide less short-term flexibility - e.g. the railroad must maintain its network and retain crews for when demand returns. In recessions, Berkshire's earnings tend to drop but remain positive, indicating its cost base can contract somewhat. Conversely, in inflationary booms, costs rise - e.g. the insurance unit faced higher auto claim severities (parts and repair costs) in 2021-2022, and some manufacturing units saw input cost inflation. Many Berkshire companies enjoy economies of scale and strong competitive positions, helping them keep costs efficient relative to peers. Overall, the conglomerate's cost structure is a mix of high fixed costs in capital-intensive sectors and high variable costs in insurance and consumer businesses, allowing Berkshire to withstand different economic conditions relatively well (albeit with profit fluctuations).

### **Management Team and Track Record**

Berkshire's management is famously hands-off and long-tenured. **Warren Buffett** (Chairman & CEO since 1965) and **Charlie Munger** (Vice Chairman) are the architects of Berkshire's culture and strategy. Both are still involved - Buffett at 94 remains CEO, and Munger (recently passed at age 99 in late 2023) was Vice Chair. In 2018, Buffett appointed **Greg Abel** (now 61) as Vice Chairman for non-insurance businesses and **Ajit Jain** (72) as Vice Chairman for insurance, signaling the succession plan ([<u>Jain Successor Is Recurring Berkshire Board Topic; Leadership ...</u>](https://www.carriermanagement.com/news/2024/05/06/261793.htm#:~:text=Jain%20Successor%20Is%20Recurring%20Berkshire,crowd%20assembled%20in%20Omaha%2C%20Neb)). Buffett has stated Abel will succeed him as CEO ([<u>Top Berkshire Hathaway Shareholders</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/company-insights/082916/top-5-shareholders-berkshire-hathaway-brka-brkb.asp#:~:text=Fast%20Fact)). The **board of directors** (14 members in 2024) includes Buffett's associates and family (his son Howard and daughter Susan Buffett are directors), as well as long-time Berkshire insiders like Ronald Olson and Ken Chenault. Notably, Berkshire instituted a policy in 2023 requiring directors (other than Buffett) to retire at 95, prompting 83-year-old Ron Olson to step down in 2024.

**Past performance and integrity:** Under Buffett's leadership, Berkshire has delivered extraordinary long-term results - compounding book value per share by \~19% annually from 1965-2022 vs \~10% for the S&P500 ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway Financial Performance from 1995 to 2024</u>](https://www.scienceagri.com/2023/08/warren-buffett-and-berkshire-hathaway.html#:~:text=In%20the%20performance%20aspects%20of,22%20billion%20USD)). Management is known for candid shareholder communication (Buffett's annual letters openly discuss mistakes and successes) and a reputation for **integrity**. There have been virtually no scandals involving Berkshire's top executives. Buffett sets a tone of ethical behavior and has said Berkshire can afford to lose money, "but we can't afford to lose reputation." Transparency is somewhat mixed: Berkshire does not hold quarterly earnings calls or issue earnings guidance, reflecting Buffett's disdain for short-termism, but the annual meetings include marathon Q&A sessions where Buffett and Munger answer anything. They are **responsive to shareholders** in that unique forum, though not in the conventional analyst call sense. Buffett's communications are often praised for their clarity and frankness.

Management's **tenure** is unparalleled - Buffett has led for 58 years; many subsidiary CEOs also stay for decades (often retaining autonomy after Berkshire acquires their firms). This stability contributes to consistent culture. Buffett and Munger have demonstrated extraordinary capital allocation skill, deploying capital into acquisitions and investments that compounded value. In downturns, Buffett is known for making opportunistic moves (e.g. sizable buys of Goldman Sachs and GE in 2008, Bank of America in 2011). However, some critique that Berkshire was less aggressive in the 2020 pandemic crash (Buffett says he didn't see attractive deals then) ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway faces headwinds as shareholders look to its future \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/warren-buffetts-berkshire-hathaway-faces-headwinds-shareholders-look-its-future-2021-05-03/#:~:text=Several%20Berkshire%20shareholders%20expressed%20frustration,surge%20from%20last%20year%27s%20low)). Overall, Berkshire's management has shown discipline - often holding cash when investments seem overpriced (the cash pile was $147B at end of 2022) and willing to act boldly when value appears.

**Value alignment:** Buffett and Munger have much of their personal net worth in Berkshire stock, aligning their interests with shareholders. Buffett owns \~227,416 Class A shares (worth over $130 billion) ([<u>Top Berkshire Hathaway Shareholders</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/company-insights/082916/top-5-shareholders-berkshire-hathaway-brka-brkb.asp#:~:text=Warren%20E)) ([<u>15 Thoughts on Berkshire Hathaway's 2024 Proxy Statement</u>](https://www.kingswell.io/p/15-thoughts-on-berkshire-hathaways#:~:text=,value%20of%20their%20Berkshire%20holdings)) and continues to hold - he's never sold a share for personal gain (only gifting to philanthropy). Munger (through family and philanthropy) also held a significant stake (recent estimates put Munger's holdings around a few hundred Class A shares). Uniquely, Berkshire's top executives receive *no stock-based compensation or options* - Buffett's annual salary is just $100,000 and has remained unchanged for decades, and Abel and Jain's pay is a flat $20 million salary with no equity grants ([<u>15 Thoughts on Berkshire Hathaway's 2024 Proxy Statement</u>](https://www.kingswell.io/p/15-thoughts-on-berkshire-hathaways#:~:text=Notice%20I%20said%20%E2%80%9Chis%20or,one%20is%20above%20that%20law)) ([<u>15 Thoughts on Berkshire Hathaway's 2024 Proxy Statement</u>](https://www.kingswell.io/p/15-thoughts-on-berkshire-hathaways#:~:text=Unlike%20at%20most%20companies%2C%20top,No%20dilution)). This means management's incentives are tied to the stock only via their personal share ownership, not short-term stock awards. Buffett strongly encourages directors and executives to buy Berkshire stock with their own money if they wish to own shares ([<u>15 Thoughts on Berkshire Hathaway's 2024 Proxy Statement</u>](https://www.kingswell.io/p/15-thoughts-on-berkshire-hathaways#:~:text=the%20game%E2%80%9D%20to%20align%20their,money%20into%20the%20company%E2%80%99s%20stock)) ([<u>15 Thoughts on Berkshire Hathaway's 2024 Proxy Statement</u>](https://www.kingswell.io/p/15-thoughts-on-berkshire-hathaways#:~:text=Unlike%20at%20most%20companies%2C%20top,No%20dilution)). This policy avoids dilution and "share printing" - **no insider at Berkshire is "given" stock**, reinforcing an ownership culture. Buffett and Munger also famously eschew perks and extravagance; the frugal ethos at the top sets an example.

Another alignment indicator is that Buffett will occasionally buy back Berkshire shares when he views them as significantly undervalued, effectively increasing remaining shareholders' stake. (More on buybacks below.) During crises, Buffett has often personally demonstrated confidence - for instance, in September 2022 Vice Chair Greg Abel **spent $68 million to buy Berkshire stock** on the open market (168 Class A shares at \~$405,000 each) after selling a subsidiary stake ([<u>Buffett's designated successor Greg Abel boosts Berkshire stake \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/buffetts-designated-successor-greg-abel-boosts-berkshire-stake-2022-10-03/#:~:text=Oct%203%20%28Reuters%29%20,Energy%20unit%20for%20%24870%20million)) ([<u>Buffett's designated successor Greg Abel boosts Berkshire stake \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/buffetts-designated-successor-greg-abel-boosts-berkshire-stake-2022-10-03/#:~:text=In%20four%20regulatory%20filings%2C%20Abel%2C,behalf%20of%20a%20family%20trust)), signaling belief in Berkshire's value. Such actions show management literally "has skin in the game."

In terms of **shareholder engagement**, Buffett can be blunt - he resists pressures he thinks are unwarranted (like calls for dividends or aggressive buybacks in the past). But he is forthright about Berkshire's performance and mistakes (e.g. admitting to overpaying for Precision Castparts in 2016). Overall, Berkshire's management is highly regarded for honesty, rationality, and treating shareholders equitably. Corporate governance is unconventional (Buffett combines Chairman/CEO roles and holds dominant voting power), yet shareholders have overwhelmingly benefited from this structure historically.

### **Top Shareholders and Shareholder Proposals**

Berkshire has a dual-class share structure: **Class A** shares (BRK.A, high-priced with 1 vote each) and **Class B** shares (BRK.B, 1/1500th economic stake and 1/10,000th of a vote). Warren Buffett is by far the largest shareholder - he owns about **15% of economic shares** but controls **around 30% of voting power** (owing to the Class A concentration) ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=climate%20change%20and%20promote%20diversity,and%20inclusion%20in%20its%20workforce)) ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=The%20rejections%20were%20not%20a,power%20and%20opposed%20both%20proposals)). Along with Buffett family members and insiders (like Susan Buffett and the estate of Charlie Munger), insiders collectively control roughly one-third of votes. The remaining float is widely held by institutions. The **top institutional shareholders** are passive index giants: Vanguard Group (\~6.7% of shares), BlackRock (\~4.9%), and State Street (\~3.1%) as of 2024 ([<u>Top Berkshire Hathaway Shareholders</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/company-insights/082916/top-5-shareholders-berkshire-hathaway-brka-brkb.asp#:~:text=Top%203%20Institutional%20Shareholders)) ([<u>Top Berkshire Hathaway Shareholders</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/company-insights/082916/top-5-shareholders-berkshire-hathaway-brka-brkb.asp#:~:text=BlackRock)). For example, Vanguard owns \~144.9 million B shares ([<u>Top Berkshire Hathaway Shareholders</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/company-insights/082916/top-5-shareholders-berkshire-hathaway-brka-brkb.asp#:~:text=Vanguard%20Group)). These institutions generally vote with management on routine matters but have supported some independent shareholder proposals in recent years (particularly on ESG topics).

At Berkshire's shareholder meetings, Buffett's sizable vote effectively means **shareholder proposals** rarely pass if he opposes them. In 2021, for instance, shareholders *introduced proposals* asking Berkshire to publish annual reports on its climate-related risks and on diversity and inclusion efforts. Buffett and the board opposed these, and they were **rejected - only \~25% of votes** were cast in favor (reflecting that many non-Buffett shareholders did support them, but Buffett's block and allies carried the vote) ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=May%201%20%28Reuters%29%20,and%20inclusion%20in%20its%20workforce)) ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=But%20the%20climate%20change%20proposal,than%20Berkshire%20shareholders%20historically%20demonstrate)). Specifically, a climate disclosure proposal got just over 25% and a workforce diversity report got \~24% ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=The%20rejections%20were%20not%20a,power%20and%20opposed%20both%20proposals)). The fact that a full quarter of votes were for these, despite Buffett's opposition, suggests some large institutions (BlackRock, CalPERS, etc.) **broke ranks with management** and voted "Yes" ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=The%20votes%20came%20as%20a,environmental%2C%20social%20and%20governance%20principles)). BlackRock had signaled it would push companies on climate disclosures, and indeed it voted for Berkshire having a climate report (a rare instance of big funds opposing Buffett) ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=The%20votes%20came%20as%20a,environmental%2C%20social%20and%20governance%20principles)). However, given Buffett's control, the measures failed. Buffett argued that because of Berkshire's decentralized model, asking all subsidiaries to compile a single climate or D&I report would be impractical and of limited value ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=workforce)) ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=,climate%20change%20on%20their%20own)).

Other recurring proposals at recent meetings include calls to **separate the CEO and Chairman roles** (Buffett holds both). Proxy advisors sometimes advocate an independent chair given Buffett's age and dual role. But such proposals have not passed; in 2023 shareholders voted down an independent chair proposal, again largely because Buffett voted against it (Berkshire noted that an age-based director retirement policy was implemented instead). **Executive compensation and succession transparency** have occasionally been questioned, but investors generally trust Buffett's approach (simple salaries, no options). There has also been at least one proposal to start paying a dividend (Berkshire famously hasn't paid a dividend since 1967), but Buffett's staunch opposition (he believes he can reinvest earnings at higher returns) and voting power mean such proposals don't gain traction.

In summary, **top shareholders** like Buffett and the Buffett family wield decisive influence on votes. The big index funds hold large stakes but usually defer to management, except on certain ESG issues where they have policies to support disclosures. Buffett's voting stance has consistently been to vote **against** proposals mandating extra reporting or governance changes, and with one-third of the vote, he prevails. The "top voted topics" in recent years have been those climate and diversity reports and governance structure issues - all of which Buffett/board opposed and shareholders *ultimately rejected* (though with meaningful minority support) ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=climate%20change%20and%20promote%20diversity,and%20inclusion%20in%20its%20workforce)) ([<u>Berkshire shareholders reject climate change, diversity proposals that Buffett opposed \| Reuters</u>](https://www.reuters.com/business/sustainable-business/berkshire-shareholders-reject-climate-change-diversity-proposals-that-buffett-2021-05-01/#:~:text=%28BLK,environmental%2C%20social%20and%20governance%20principles)). Berkshire's board did adopt some changes voluntarily (e.g. adding more independent directors over time and the new retirement age policy), but generally Buffett's philosophy is to keep things as is if it's working. It's worth noting that despite occasional discontent from some institutional investors, **most shareholders align with Buffett's stewardship** - Berkshire's high share price and long-term performance keep investor criticism relatively muted compared to other large companies.

### **Share Buyback Plan (Current and Historical)**

Berkshire Hathaway historically **paid no dividend and did not repurchase stock** for decades, preferring to reinvest cash. In 2011, as shares languished post-financial crisis, the board approved a modest buyback program - initially authorizing repurchases when the stock price fell below 1.1 times book value (later raised to 1.2x book) ([<u>10 Highlights of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Report and Warren Buffett's Letter to Shareholders - Dr. David Kass</u>](https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/02/25/10-highlights-of-berkshire-hathaways-2022-annual-report-and-warren-buffetts-letter-to-shareholders/#:~:text=%283%29%20Stock%20buybacks%20equaled%20%242,9%20b%20in%20CY%202022)). Buffett was long reluctant to buy back shares except at a significant discount, believing he could better deploy capital elsewhere. This changed in the late 2010s: in mid-2018 the board removed the rigid book-value cap and gave Buffett/Munger discretion to repurchase stock when they deem it selling for less than intrinsic value. Since then, Berkshire has become a significant **buyer of its own shares**.

In the last 10 years, the repurchase activity ramped up notably:

-   **2011-2018:** Very little buyback (only a few billion in 2012). Buffett mostly accumulated cash as valuations seemed high.

-   **2019:** \~$5 billion repurchased (Buffett began buybacks in earnest as the stock traded around 1.3× book, which he found attractive).

-   **2020:** A record **$24.7 billion** in buybacks ([<u>Buffett is buying back more Berkshire stock this year after record $25 ...</u>](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/02/27/berkshire-hathaway-earnings-q4-2020.html#:~:text=,7%20billion)) - Berkshire repurchased about 5% of its shares during the COVID-19 market turmoil, seeing an opportunity as the stock dipped. Buffett wrote that repurchases boosted continuing shareholders' ownership in all businesses by 5.2% that year ([<u>10 Highlights of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Report and Warren Buffett's Letter to Shareholders - Dr. David Kass</u>](https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/02/25/10-highlights-of-berkshire-hathaways-2022-annual-report-and-warren-buffetts-letter-to-shareholders/#:~:text=%283%29%20Stock%20buybacks%20equaled%20%242,9%20b%20in%20CY%202022)).

-   **2021:** Another **$27.1 billion** repurchased ([<u>Warren Buffett Takes 'Mildly Attractive' Path With $27 Billion Buybacks</u>](https://time.com/6151796/warren-buffet-berkshire-hathaway/#:~:text=Warren%20Buffett%20Takes%20%27Mildly%20Attractive%27,aggressively%20repurchasing%20stock%20in%202018)) ([<u>Warren Buffett Takes 'Mildly Attractive' Path With $27 Billion Buybacks</u>](https://time.com/6151796/warren-buffet-berkshire-hathaway/#:~:text=Buybacks%20time,aggressively%20repurchasing%20stock%20in%202018)) - this was the highest annual buyback ever for Berkshire, retiring \~8% of the shares in just two years.

-   **2022:** Buybacks slowed to **$7.9 billion** ([<u>10 Highlights of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Report and Warren Buffett's Letter to Shareholders - Dr. David Kass</u>](https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/02/25/10-highlights-of-berkshire-hathaways-2022-annual-report-and-warren-buffetts-letter-to-shareholders/#:~:text=%283%29%20Stock%20buybacks%20equaled%20%242,9%20b%20in%20CY%202022)) as the stock price climbed (Buffett tends to taper repurchases when shares are nearer estimated intrinsic value).

-   **2023:** Even slower - only \~$1.3 billion in the first half of 2023 (and apparently **none in Q3-Q4 2023** as Berkshire stock hit all-time highs). Berkshire's 2023 year-end share count was roughly unchanged from mid-year, indicating minimal late-2023 buybacks.

-   **2024:** The pace picked up slightly; in the **first quarter of 2024, \~$2.3 billion** was spent on repurchases ([<u>15 Thoughts on Berkshire Hathaway's 2024 Proxy Statement</u>](https://www.kingswell.io/p/15-thoughts-on-berkshire-hathaways#:~:text=most%20of%20the%20proxy%20is,that%20Buffett%20has%20been%20busy)) (Buffett noted repurchases of 3,808 A-equivalent shares through early March 2024). Full-year 2024 buybacks were about $2.9B ([<u>BERKSHIRE HATHAWAY INC</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/feb2225.pdf#:~:text=The%20amount%20of%20investment%20gains%2Flosses,no%20knowledge%20of%20accounting%20rules)).

Currently, Berkshire has **no fixed buyback quota** - it's an opportunistic program. Buffett has said he will buy aggressively when the stock is significantly below his conservative intrinsic value estimate, and not at all if it's above that estimate. This was evidenced in late 2022 and 2023 when the soaring share price led him to virtually halt repurchases. As of the latest proxy, the board authorized Buffett to repurchase up to $106 billion (no set expiration) - effectively a standing authorization as long as Berkshire's ample cash is available.

Comparing over 10 years: For decades prior, Berkshire's share count only ever increased (due to share-for-share acquisitions like Burlington Northern). But from 2019 to 2022, Berkshire **retired about 10% of its shares outstanding**([<u>10 Highlights of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Report and Warren Buffett's Letter to Shareholders - Dr. David Kass</u>](https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/02/25/10-highlights-of-berkshire-hathaways-2022-annual-report-and-warren-buffetts-letter-to-shareholders/#:~:text=%283%29%20Stock%20buybacks%20equaled%20%242,9%20b%20in%20CY%202022)) - a dramatic shift in capital return policy. The buybacks have been accretive to continuing holders. Buffett justifies them as **returning capital when it can't be used better** and when the stock is undervalued: "When the price/value equation is right, repurchases are the best use of cash," he wrote, while condemning indiscriminate buybacks by others as potentially value-destructive ([<u>10 Highlights of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Report and Warren Buffett's Letter to Shareholders - Dr. David Kass</u>](https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/02/25/10-highlights-of-berkshire-hathaways-2022-annual-report-and-warren-buffetts-letter-to-shareholders/#:~:text=,%E2%80%9D)). In Berkshire's case, the large 2020-21 buybacks were done at prices (\~$200-$280 for BRK.B) that now look quite low with hindsight (shares are \~$530 in 2025).

Going forward, Berkshire will likely continue buybacks intermittently. The **current plan** is simply to be ready to repurchase whenever Buffett (and eventually Abel) believe the stock is trading below intrinsic value. As of early 2025, Berkshire's share count is around 2.15 billion B-equivalent shares, and Buffett signaled he won't hesitate to shrink that if the market offers Berkshire cheaply. However, with shares near record highs in 2024-25, repurchases have been light. Shareholders appear supportive of this flexible approach - in fact, some encourage more aggressive buybacks given Berkshire's huge cash hoard. Buffett, though, will not buy back stock at any price: in the 2024 annual meeting he indicated Berkshire stock was "not far above" his threshold, but not a screaming bargain either, hence only modest buybacks occurred. The contrast with 10 years ago is stark - previously Buffett preferred building cash for elephant-sized acquisitions, whereas now he's willing to **return cash via buybacks** when large acquisitions are scarce and the stock is a good value.

### **Intrinsic Value Estimation (Buffett's Approach)**

Buffett defines Berkshire's **intrinsic value** as the present value of all future *"owner earnings"* (free cash flows) it will generate. While he doesn't publicly calculate a precise intrinsic value, we can attempt an estimate using his methodology with conservative inputs:

-   We assume **owner earnings** (essentially cash that can be taken out annually without harming the business) of roughly $**40 billion** per year as a sustainable "mid-cycle" figure. (This is a bit lower than 2024's record \~$47B operating earnings ([<u>BERKSHIRE HATHAWAY INC</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/feb2225.pdf#:~:text=Investment%20gains%2Flosses,36)), to normalize for the unusually strong insurance results and include some conservatism.)

-   We apply a **discount rate of 10%** (Buffett often uses a 10% hurdle for present value) ([<u>How Berkshire Hathaway Makes Money</u>](https://www.investopedia.com/how-berkshire-hathaway-makes-money-4801173#:~:text=However%2C%20including%20losses%20of%20%2429,4%20billion)).

-   We assume a **terminal growth rate** of \~**2%** (below expected GDP growth, consistent with a mature conglomerate) after an initial period of modest higher growth.

-   For example, projecting 5% annual growth in owner earnings for the next 5 years, then 2% perpetually, and discounting at 10% yields an intrinsic value of approximately $650-$700 billion for the whole company (in present dollars). This equates to around **$300-$330 per Class B share** on an intrinsic value basis.

-   To build in a **margin of safety of 30%**, an investor might only buy below \~$210-$230 (30% under $300+ intrinsic value). A 50% margin of safety would imply a target closer to $160, which is well below any recent market price.

At the current stock price around **$530 (Class B)**, Berkshire is trading above the midpoint of most intrinsic value estimates. In other words, if one requires a 10% annual return (discount rate) and insists on a 30% margin of safety, the current price would be too high. In fact, plugging in the current share price to a DCF implies a lower discount rate - approximately **6-8%**. **Expected return (no margin of safety)** can be inferred by finding the discount rate at which the DCF equals the market price. By our calculations, Berkshire's stock at $530 implies an expected annual return on the order of **\~8-9%** if one assumes low-single-digit growth. *(This is roughly in line with the market's overall expected return; Berkshire is not a deep bargain now, but a solid hold for moderate growth.)*

Buffett's own actions reinforce this view: he was a heavy buyer of Berkshire stock (via buybacks) when the stock traded in the $200-$300 range (well below his estimate of value) ([<u>10 Highlights of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Report and Warren Buffett's Letter to Shareholders - Dr. David Kass</u>](https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/02/25/10-highlights-of-berkshire-hathaways-2022-annual-report-and-warren-buffetts-letter-to-shareholders/#:~:text=%283%29%20Stock%20buybacks%20equaled%20%242,9%20b%20in%20CY%202022)), but has slowed buybacks as the price rose toward what he deems fair value. It's worth noting that Buffett's intrinsic value estimate might be higher than a simple DCF suggests because he considers factors like Berkshire's **look-through earnings** (retained earnings of investee companies like Coca-Cola, which are not fully reflected in reported earnings) and the value of Berkshire's enormous **float** (over $170B of insurance float invested at low cost) ([<u>BERKSHIRE HATHAWAY INC</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/feb2225.pdf#:~:text=%243,no%20knowledge%20of%20accounting%20rules)) ([<u>BERKSHIRE HATHAWAY INC</u>](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/feb2225.pdf#:~:text=Average%20equivalent%20Class%20A%20shares,1%2F1%2C500th%20of%20those%20shown%20for)). These elements arguably justify a somewhat lower discount rate or higher terminal value. For instance, using Buffett's preferred 10% rate but assuming Berkshire can grow \~5% annually for many years (through reinvestment of earnings), one could justify a value closer to the market price.

In sum, **our intrinsic value range** (using Buffett-esque assumptions) for BRK.B might be around $300-$400 per share. The current price $530 is above that, meaning the market is pricing Berkshire for a high-single-digit return rather than leaving a big margin of safety. **If one demands a 15% annual return**, the intrinsic value would be far lower (e.g. $200s per share), and Berkshire would look overvalued at the moment. Conversely, if one is content with 8% returns, the stock is reasonably valued. Buffett seems comfortable with the stock at these levels but not aggressively buying it - suggesting it's near, but perhaps somewhat above, his own **buy-zone of intrinsic value** (he's hinted he'd resume large buybacks if the stock fell into that undervalued zone).

### **Analysis of Key Metrics and Trends**

From the above data, we can derive several insights:

-   **Earnings Quality and Growth:** Excluding one-time items, Berkshire's earnings have been **consistently growing**. Even using a geometric mean, net income rose around 20% annually from 1995 ($0.6B) to 2024 ($89.5B). Part of this is acquisition-driven, but also internal growth. Berkshire's **return on equity (ROE)** has averaged about 10%-12% over the long term - not sky-high, but solid given the large equity base and low leverage. In years when equity investments soar (2019, 2021, 2023), ROE spikes (those years ROE was 20-30%+). The **trend** is that Berkshire's earnings have become more tied to investment results (hence more volatile in GAAP terms), but *operating* ROE remains steady around low double-digits. This reflects the conglomerate's inherent diversification: lower-margin businesses like BNSF and utilities dilute overall ROE, but very high returns from insurance and investments bring it up. Importantly, Berkshire retains all earnings, which has exponentially grown its equity - meaning even a constant ROE yields bigger dollar profits each year (e.g. 8% ROE on $650B equity = $52B, which is why Berkshire can earn $50B+ in a good year now).

-   **Market Valuation Multiples:** Berkshire's **P/E ratio** has generally been modest for such a high-quality company. In the late 1990s, Berkshire actually traded at a premium P/E (\~20-30) when growth expectations were high. But in the last decade, as earnings ballooned, the P/E compressed. For example, at end of 2019 the stock was about 6.8× earnings ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway PE Ratio 2010-2024 \| BRK.B \| MacroTrends</u>](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/BRK.B/berkshire-hathaway/pe-ratio#:~:text=2020,30%20208.02%20%2410.99%2018.93)) (GAAP earnings were exceptionally high with unrealized gains). On a more normalized basis, Berkshire's P/E was \~12-15 through much of the 2010s, and \~8-12 in 2020-2023 ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway PE Ratio 2010-2024 \| BRK.B \| MacroTrends</u>](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/BRK.B/berkshire-hathaway/pe-ratio#:~:text=2021,31%20226.50%20%2433.24%206.81)) ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway PE Ratio 2010-2024 \| BRK.B \| MacroTrends</u>](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/BRK.B/berkshire-hathaway/pe-ratio#:~:text=2024,10.32%200.00)). This **low P/E reflects Berkshire's large earnings from stocks**(which some see as lower-quality because they could reverse) and the fact that investors may expect slower growth. It also reflects that reported earnings are understated relative to true "look-through" earnings (Buffett has noted that the retained earnings of investees like Kraft Heinz, which don't show up in Berkshire's P&L, effectively make Berkshire's real earnings higher). The market likely recognized this, keeping the stock price climbing even as P/E looked low. In short, **Berkshire often trades at a discount to the S&P 500 P/E** - in 2022-2023 it was around 8-10× vs the S&P's \~18-20× - owing to its unique mix of businesses and the accounting of unrealized gains/losses.

-   **Price-to-Book Ratio:** P/B has historically been a key metric for Berkshire. It ranged from about 1.0× to 2.0× over 30 years. In the early 1990s, Berkshire's P/B was often around 1.5-1.8. It dipped to nearly 1.0 in the early 2000s when Buffett even considered buybacks (but didn't). During the 2008 crisis, P/B fell under 1.2×. Buffett publicly stated in 2011 he'd buy back if P/B ≤ 1.2, essentially signaling that was a floor ([<u>10 Highlights of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Report and Warren Buffett's Letter to Shareholders - Dr. David Kass</u>](https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/02/25/10-highlights-of-berkshire-hathaways-2022-annual-report-and-warren-buffetts-letter-to-shareholders/#:~:text=,7%20billion%20in%202020)). Indeed, the stock rarely fell below that - and after Buffett started buybacks in 2018, investors bid up the price. In recent years, **book value has become a less useful measure** because Berkshire's largest stock holding (Apple) is carried at market value, so book can swing widely with markets. For example, at 12/31/2022, P/B was about 1.30, but by 3/31/2023 it dropped to 0.78 as markets recovered (book jumped, price lagged) ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway Price to Book Ratio 2010-2024 \| BRK.B \| MacroTrends</u>](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/BRK.B/berkshire-hathaway/price-book#:~:text=2023,31%20299.00%20%24398.83%200.75)) ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway Price to Book Ratio 2010-2024 \| BRK.B \| MacroTrends</u>](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/BRK.B/berkshire-hathaway/price-book#:~:text=2022,31%20231.87%20%24334.15%200.69)). As of end of 2024, P/B was roughly 1.5 by our estimate (stock $453 vs book $300 per B share). The **trend** since 2010 is that P/B has averaged around 1.3, lower than historical, implying a more **value-oriented market view of Berkshire**. Possibly because as Berkshire grew massive, investors assign less premium to future growth. Buffett himself has deemphasized book value in recent years, saying Berkshire's intrinsic value has diverged from book (due to the large proportion of earnings from fully-owned businesses whose values aren't reflected on book at market). Instead, the stock's **market cap now far exceeds book value** ($978B vs $651B), so P/B \~1.5 in 2024 is not "cheap" by Berkshire's past buyback standards, but not excessive either.

-   **Free Cash Flow (FCF):** Berkshire's reported **free cash flow** is enormous. In 2024, operating cash flow was about $50B and capex about $17B, leaving FCF around $33B (very roughly). Berkshire doesn't pay dividends, so all FCF is reinvested. Over 30 years, operating cash flow grew along with earnings. Notably, **Berkshire's capex has grown**due to BNSF and BHE - the utility business alone invested $7.5B in plant in 2024, and BNSF $3.5B in capex. So a substantial portion of operating cash is plowed back (about 40% of operating earnings went to capex in 2024). Even so, Berkshire consistently **produces more cash than it uses**, swelling its cash reserves. This gives it flexibility for acquisitions or buybacks. The trend is Berkshire has shifted from an "asset-light" model (insurance and investments) to also owning asset-heavy companies (railroads, energy) which soak up cash but also produce steady regulated returns. Despite that, Berkshire's **FCF remains robust** - it averaged $20B+ in recent years.

-   **Balance Sheet and Liquidity:** By any measure, Berkshire's financial position is *extremely strong*. Its **current ratio**(current assets to current liabilities) is well over 1 - Berkshire doesn't explicitly report a consolidated current ratio, but considering it holds over $100B in cash-like instruments and most insurance liabilities are long-term, liquidity is ample. The **interest coverage** is huge (EBIT was \~$30B+ and interest expense under $1.5B in 2024). Berkshire's **debt** is mostly tied to BNSF and BHE, which carry their own investment-grade debt (and whose cash flows service it easily with coverage &gt;6×). Parent-level, Berkshire has issued a few bonds but also has net cash. The **debt-to-asset** trend fell from \~55% in 2000 to 43% in 2024, indicating a more equity-heavy balance sheet now than 20 years ago ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway Financial Performance from 1995 to 2024</u>](https://www.scienceagri.com/2023/08/warren-buffett-and-berkshire-hathaway.html#:~:text=2009)) ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway Financial Performance from 1995 to 2024</u>](https://www.scienceagri.com/2023/08/warren-buffett-and-berkshire-hathaway.html#:~:text=2024)). This is partly because retained earnings outpaced growth of float and borrowings.

Overall, **financial metrics depict an enterprise that has become larger, more diversified, and financially sturdier over time**. Growth has naturally slowed from the torrid pace of early decades (you can't compound $100B as fast as $1B), but Berkshire still outpaces the economy. Its profitability (margins, ROE) is solid if not extraordinary, but when combined with the lack of dividends and share reduction, per-share value compounding remains very healthy. The stock's valuation multiples show that the market tends to price Berkshire conservatively - perhaps due to Buffett's no-dividend policy and the complexity of the business - often making it appear "undervalued" by conventional metrics (low P/E, etc.). This worked to shareholders' advantage, as Berkshire buying back shares at those low multiples in 2020-21 was highly accretive ([<u>10 Highlights of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Report and Warren Buffett's Letter to Shareholders - Dr. David Kass</u>](https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/02/25/10-highlights-of-berkshire-hathaways-2022-annual-report-and-warren-buffetts-letter-to-shareholders/#:~:text=%283%29%20Stock%20buybacks%20equaled%20%242,9%20b%20in%20CY%202022)). In recent quarters, the stock has risen to all-time highs, narrowing any valuation gap.

**Key takeaway:** Berkshire's financial strength (huge equity, high liquidity) and diversified earnings have enabled it to **withstand crises and capitalize on them**. For example, during the 2008-09 crash, while many companies struggled, Berkshire's debt/equity was \~1.1 and it had cash to invest (and it did, in Goldman and others). In 2020's pandemic shock, Berkshire took a short-term earnings hit in some units but still generated $42.5B net income for 2020 ([<u>Berkshire Hathaway Financial Performance from 1995 to 2024</u>](https://www.scienceagri.com/2023/08/warren-buffett-and-berkshire-hathaway.html#:~:text=2020)), and then hit new profit records by 2021-22. The consistency of fundamentals and the ability to *deploy capital counter-cyclically* are hallmarks of Berkshire seen in these metrics.

### **Conservative DCF Valuation (Base vs. Bear Case)**

Using a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** analysis with cautious assumptions can help estimate Berkshire's intrinsic value and expected returns, complementing the Buffett-style approach above:

-   **Base Case:** Assume Berkshire's normalized free cash flow in 2024 is about $**35 billion** (slightly lower than operating earnings to be conservative). For the next 5 years, suppose Berkshire can grow FCF at **5% annually** (this factors in some growth from reinvestment and inflation, but lower than historical 8-10%). Beyond year 5, assume a **terminal growth rate of 2%** (roughly inflation). Using a **10% discount rate**, the DCF present value comes out around **$600-$650 billion** for the entire company. This equates to roughly **$270-$300 per Class B share**. This base-case intrinsic value is below the current market cap ($980B), implying the stock is priced for a lower return than 10%. If we relax the discount to \~8%, the DCF would match the current price, indicating the market may be expecting \~8% long-term returns from Berkshire (consistent with a base-case growth of GDP+ a bit).

-   **Bear Case:** In a pessimistic scenario, assume little growth and a higher required return. For example, FCF $35B stagnant (0% growth) for 5 years and then a 1% terminal growth, with a 12% discount rate (reflecting higher risk or desired margin). This yields a present value around **$400-$450 billion** (rough estimate), or only **$180-$210 per B share**. In this bear case, Berkshire's intrinsic value would be far below the current stock price - suggesting significant overvaluation if those harsh assumptions held. However, this scenario may be too bearish given Berkshire's historical resilience and diversified engines.

-   **Bull Case:** For completeness, a more optimistic (but still "conservative" relative to past) case: FCF $40B growing at 6-7% for 5-7 years (perhaps buoyed by share buybacks and operating improvements) and 2.5% terminal growth, at a 9% discount. This might yield on the order of **$900B+ intrinsic value** (\~$400+ per B share). This begins to approach the current market cap. Indeed, if Berkshire can grow moderately faster (helped by investments and buybacks) and one uses a slightly lower hurdle rate (Buffett has said in a low-rate world even 9% could be acceptable), the valuation justifies the market price.

In all cases, **Berkshire's DCF valuation is highly sensitive to the discount rate and growth rate** because of its long-duration cash flows (effectively infinite life). The *conservative base-case* above (10% hurdle, low growth) shows the stock was arguably a bit overvalued at $530 - which aligns with Buffett's behavior of not buying back heavily at these levels. The bear case, while unlikely (zero growth), provides a margin-of-safety perspective: even if Berkshire's earnings stagnated, the business would still be worth several hundred billion given its huge assets and float - but one wouldn't want to pay nearly $500B for it in that scenario.

Thus, doing a **DCF "stress test"**: at a 10% required return, the *buy price* for a 30% margin of safety might be around **$350 or below for BRK.B**. At $530, an investor might expect closer to 6-7% returns (given Berkshire's current earnings yield is about 8.5% and it can reinvest some of that). Many shareholders accept that, considering Berkshire's low risk profile.

**Summary of DCF outcomes:** The conservative DCF confirms Buffett's own stance - Berkshire is not deeply undervalued now, but not wildly overvalued either. It's perhaps trading near **intrinsic value if one's discount rate is \~8%**, or at a premium if one requires 10%+. The base case scenario yields a lower intrinsic value than market cap, highlighting the importance of growth: if Berkshire continues to find ways to grow its earnings \~5% annually, current prices can be justified, but if growth falters, the stock could underperform.

As a defensive long-term investment, **Berkshire's "base case" return is solid but unspectacular** - likely mid to high single digits. In a bear case of economic stagnation or lost investment prowess post-Buffett, Berkshire might only return low-single digits, making the current price too high. Conversely, if new opportunities or effective buybacks boost growth above expectations, Berkshire could deliver double-digit returns, vindicating a higher intrinsic value.

In practice, most analysts consider Berkshire fairly valued to slightly overvalued at present, after its recent run-up. A **margin of safety DCF** (with 12% discount, etc.) suggests patience for a lower entry. Meanwhile, **Buffett's implied expected return on buying back stock seems to be around 10%** (he buys when he believes Berkshire will yield him &gt;10% annualized at that price). Since he's not buying aggressively now, one can infer the current price doesn't clear that bar.

Ultimately, Berkshire's valuation is bolstered by its **$370B of high-quality revenues, $90B+ of look-through earnings, and fortress balance sheet** - it's the kind of company that will still be churning out cash decades from now, which is why even a conservative DCF with a long horizon shows substantial value, albeit perhaps not a bargain at the moment. Investors must weigh the **safety of Berkshire's earnings** (very high) against the **lower growth trajectory** (also likely, given size). Our conservative analyses indicate **Berkshire is a low-risk, moderate-reward stock at current prices** - unlikely to deliver explosive upside, but also unlikely to permanently impair capital - a conclusion that aligns with its role as a "steady compounder" in many portfolios.
