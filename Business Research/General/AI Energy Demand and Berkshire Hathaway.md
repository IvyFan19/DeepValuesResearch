# Berkshire Hathaway Energy: Capitalizing on the Exponential Energy Demands of Artificial Intelligence

### I. Executive Summary: The Infrastructure Moat in the Age of AI

The dramatic and nonlinear rise in computational demand driven by Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Generative AI models, necessitates an unprecedented build-out of supporting power infrastructure. This surge translates directly into a massive, mandatory capital expenditure (CAPEX) opportunity for regulated utilities in key geographic regions. Berkshire Hathaway (BRK) is uniquely positioned to capture the intrinsic value of this trend through its subsidiary, Berkshire Hathaway Energy (BHE).

BHE’s competitive advantage stems from its structure as a vast, geographically diversified owner of regulated utility assets, including PacifiCorp, MidAmerican Energy Company, and NV Energy. The regulated utility model assures stable, predictable returns on the substantial capital required to serve this specialized industrial load, effectively insulating BRK from volatility typically associated with the technology sector. BHE is transitioning from a steady, low-growth utility holding company into a critical, high-CAPEX infrastructure enabler for the world’s leading technology companies.

The growth trajectory is particularly acute in BHE's service areas. Key territories in Iowa and Nevada are facing electricity demand requests that could potentially double or even triple their existing system capacity in the near future, compelling rapid, multi-billion dollar infrastructure investment. This mandated investment rapidly expands BHE’s rate base—the core financial mechanism guaranteeing long-term returns. Furthermore, BHE’s immense scale, robust financial strength, and proactive investment in critical transmission and distribution (T&D) infrastructure grant it a distinct advantage. This positioning allows BHE to expedite crucial grid connections, circumventing the multi-year grid delays (sometimes exceeding seven years) that often frustrate the aggressive timelines of hyperscale data center developers operating in other markets. This combination of regulated earnings and infrastructure dominance solidifies BHE’s role as an indispensable cornerstone of BRK’s exposure to the macro growth of AI.

### II. The Unprecedented Surge in AI Power Demand: A Structural Grid Shock

The electricity consumption required to train and operate advanced AI models represents a structural, long-term shock to the global energy grid, shifting utility planning paradigms from slow, linear growth to accelerated, non-linear demand increases.

#### A. Quantification of Exponential AI Load Growth

Current forecasts indicate that the AI sector is driving a monumental shift in electricity consumption profiles across the United States. Immediate demand horizons point to rapid, substantial needs. For instance, a forecast from AI lab Anthropic estimates that merely training the latest AI models will require 50 gigawatts (GW) of new power capacity by 2027, with significantly more required for operational deployment. This is compounded by testimonies indicating data centers will require an additional 29 GW by 2027, climbing to 67 GW more by 2030, just in North America.

On a longer timeline, the scale of expected growth is unprecedented. Deloitte projects that power demand from AI data centers in the United States could grow more than thirtyfold, rising from 4 GW in 2024 to an estimated 123 GW by 2035. While data centers historically accounted for a relatively small fraction of global energy use (around 0.5%) , the sheer volume and density of these new AI facilities are the primary concerns. Industry specialists estimate five-year data center demand growth, spanning various types of facilities, could range from 10 GW to as much as 65 GW through 2029.

Crucially, this demand is specifically driven by specialized AI computational requirements. Generative AI models, such as GPT-4, are growing exponentially larger, demanding increased data center energy for both training and processing. While AI currently represents only 10% to 20% of total data center load, its share of *new* demand is disproportionately large. McKinsey estimates that AI will account for 70% of total new data center power demand between 2023 and 2030, pushing AI’s total share to about 40% of the overall data center load. This concentration of intensive, high-growth load mandates accelerated utility planning and investment.

#### B. The Infrastructure Bottleneck: Transmission and Distribution Constraints

The challenge presented by AI load is not solely a question of generation capacity, but critically, one of distribution and infrastructure robustness. AI data centers utilize specialized graphics processing units (GPUs) which require dramatically more energy per square foot than traditional centers; a five-acre facility might see its energy usage soar from 5 megawatts (MW) to 50 MW simply by augmenting its processors. This high-density load creates intense, localized stress on the existing grid and necessitates immediate, substantial upgrades to transmission and distribution (T&D) networks.

Expert analysis indicates that the near-term hurdle in serving AI load is the inability to distribute power effectively, rather than an absence of generating capability. Generating new power, particularly renewable power situated remotely, is often faster than securing approvals and physically upgrading long-distance transmission lines. This dynamic elevates BHE’s control over vast T&D assets into a fundamental competitive advantage. BHE and its subsidiaries, such as PacifiCorp, manage extensive infrastructure, including 215,200 circuit miles of electric transmission and distribution lines.

For utilities, control over and capacity to rapidly expand T&D—a core competence of BHE—is the essential factor determining speed to market for large customers. Conversely, other regions and utilities face interconnection delays of up to seven years or more, rendering them unattractive to hyperscalers with aggressive deployment schedules. BHE recognizes this dynamic and is strategically investing heavily in the U.S. power grid specifically to support the massive power demands of AI data centers, positioning itself as a foundational enabler of the entire AI ecosystem. ### III. Berkshire Hathaway Energy: A Comprehensive Asset and Financial Profile

Berkshire Hathaway Energy’s portfolio of companies, vast asset base, and unique financial structure position it as a stable engine for long-term capital deployment within the BRK conglomerate.

#### A. Organizational Structure and Financial Role within BRK

BHE operates as a privately held portfolio comprising locally managed energy businesses, unified by a common vision and a shared emphasis on safety and reliability. The company is led by President and CEO Scott W. Thon and overseen by Gregory E. Abel, who serves as Chair of BHE and is designated as Warren Buffett’s successor to lead the parent company, Berkshire Hathaway. This structural relationship ensures BHE has direct access to BRK’s immense financial resources and long-term strategic perspective.

While BHE is often viewed as a stable, utility-based earnings stream, its financial contribution remains significant. In fiscal year 2024, BHE generated $26.35 billion in revenue, representing 7.1% of Berkshire Hathaway's total revenue. Although this percentage is smaller than that contributed by the Insurance Group or Manufacturing segments, BHE’s strategic role is not focused on maximizing short-term profits. Instead, it functions as a massive, counter-cyclical, and financially anchored entity for BRK’s overall portfolio. The predictable, regulated nature of its earnings provides a stable foundation. The current structural demand for AI infrastructure reinforces this financial reliability by creating a mandatory pathway to deploy BRK’s significant cash reserves into long-term, interest-bearing assets—the growing rate base—at guaranteed rates of return, effectively transforming stored cash into enduring economic assets.

#### B. Scale and Diversity of Physical Assets

BHE's physical infrastructure spans vast geographic regions and various segments of the energy value chain, serving over 13 million customers and end-users globally. The core regulated utility segment includes major operators critical to the current AI development boom: PacifiCorp, which is the largest grid owner/operator in the West, serving 2.1 million customers across six western states (Oregon, Washington, California, Utah, Idaho, and Wyoming) ; MidAmerican Energy Company, serving 1.6 million customers in key Midwestern states (Iowa, Illinois, South Dakota, and Nebraska) ; and NV Energy, which provides electricity and natural gas service to approximately 90% of Nevada’s population.

As of December 31, 2024, BHE’s footprint included a total diversified generation capacity of 37,397 MW, encompassing both operating assets and projects under construction. Of this total, 31,339 MW are owned by the regulated electric utility businesses. Crucially, BHE’s infrastructure is not limited to electricity generation. It operates an expansive network of assets, including 215,200 circuit miles of electric transmission and distribution lines. Furthermore, the BHE Pipeline Group, comprising entities like Northern Natural Gas, Kern River Gas Transmission Company, and BHE GT&S, owns and operates over 21,000 miles of interstate natural gas pipeline, supporting natural gas transmission and storage across the U.S..

#### C. The Green Energy Mandate and AI Alignment

The convergence of AI demand and corporate sustainability targets creates a significant opportunity for BHE’s renewable energy portfolio. BHE Renewables owns and operates substantial clean generation assets, including solar, wind, geothermal, and hydroelectric projects. The portfolio currently boasts 4,198 MW of clean generation capacity , including some of the largest solar projects in the U.S., such as the Topaz Solar Farms and Solar Star.

BHE’s corporate strategy aligns directly with the needs of hyperscalers. The company’s energy mix has undergone a profound transformation, with non-carbon generation growing from 15% of total generation in 2005 (pro forma for acquisitions) to 45% by 2024, with expectations to reach approximately 60% by 2030. This decarbonization trajectory is vital because major technology firms driving the AI boom have aggressive climate commitments, frequently demanding 24/7 carbon-free energy supply for their massive data center campuses. BHE has invested $34.1 billion in renewable energy projects through 2023 and has already ceased coal operations at 18 coal-fired power stations, with plans to retire 15 more between 2025 and 2030. This strategic commitment to clean power, combined with infrastructure scale, positions BHE as a preferred partner for ESG-conscious tech giants, accelerating the mandated capital investment in BHE's clean generation and firming capacity.

### IV. Direct Exposure Analysis: BHE’s Critical Service Territories

The financial benefit to BHE is highly localized, concentrated in service territories where sudden, immense AI-driven load growth requires immediate, high-value CAPEX.

#### A. NV Energy (Nevada): The Tripling Demand Crisis

Nevada, particularly the Las Vegas/Reno area, is a major hub experiencing an unprecedented data center boom. The scale of load requests currently dwarfs NV Energy’s existing capacity. According to NV Energy’s director of business development, the utility's current peak demand is approximately 9,000 MW, but tech companies have submitted requests for an additional 21,000 to 22,000 MW. If fulfilled, this requested load would nearly triple the existing peak demand of the utility’s grid.

NV Energy has acknowledged that these are "unprecedented times" and has committed to meeting a portion of this load, having already agreed to secure approximately 3,000 to 4,000 MW of power. To meet this surge, the utility is aggressively advancing the **Greenlink Expansion Project**, a massive infrastructure build designed to add 5,000 MW of capacity, with partial availability expected by 2027. The urgency of this build is demonstrated by the fact that NV Energy secured regulatory approval to charge Southern Nevada customers to help fund the Greenlink project, reflecting the essential nature of the upgrade. NV Energy is also negotiating with large tech companies to have them directly contribute to paying for the necessary upgrades themselves.

This accelerated expansion strategy is further supported by innovative rate structures. NV Energy, in collaboration with Google and geothermal developer Fervo Energy, pioneered the **Clean Transition Tariff (CTT)**. This novel mechanism allows large customers to pay a premium for firm power derived from emerging energy technologies, such as Fervo's 115 MW enhanced geothermal project. This structure serves multiple strategic purposes: it de-risks the investment for NV Energy by ensuring a long-term, high-value contract ; it accelerates the deployment of specialized, 24/7 carbon-free power required by hyperscalers; and it ensures that the costs associated with commercializing new technologies are borne by the demanding corporate customers, rather than residential ratepayers.

#### B. MidAmerican Energy (Iowa): The Doubling Heart of AI Training

MidAmerican Energy Company’s service territory in Iowa has quietly become a critical center for AI computation, notably hosting significant infrastructure, including the Azure supercomputer utilized for training models like GPT-4, located west of Des Moines.

The implications for mandatory CAPEX in this region are staggering. Gregory E. Abel, Chair of BHE, confirmed that demand associated with AI and data centers in the Iowa utility territory is expected to **double** by the mid-2030s. Abel noted that this doubling of energy demand—which took over 100 years to reach the current level—in a little over a decade "will require substantial amounts of capital from MidAmerican and its shareholders". This statement signifies a guaranteed, long-term CAPEX cycle that will rapidly inflate MidAmerican's rate base.

MidAmerican must navigate regulatory challenges, as the accelerated growth creates friction within the community. Public concern exists regarding whether residential customers are effectively subsidizing the massive energy costs of profitable corporate data centers. This public sensitivity requires MidAmerican to carefully manage its resource evaluation study (RES) processes and secure necessary regulatory approvals for siting and ratemaking, a process that typically requires significant lead time (9-10 months for advanced ratemaking principles approval). Nonetheless, the utility is committed to meeting contracted data center loads and developing ramp schedules that it can reliably accommodate, confirming that the substantial capital build-out is mandatory to ensure resource adequacy for all customers.

#### C. PacifiCorp and Western Interconnection Capacity

PacifiCorp, serving 2.1 million customers across six western states, is the largest grid owner/operator in the West and a critical component of BHE's ability to facilitate regional power flows. Its integrated energy system is vital for developing and delivering low-cost, reliable power, particularly as the region shifts towards massive renewable energy generation.

BHE, through PacifiCorp and other entities, operates over 25,000 miles of transmission lines. The company has historically identified and worked to mitigate transmission capacity constraints, which can adversely affect consumers and inhibit the connection of new generation resources. For AI load, which is often situated close to or requires power delivered from remote renewable energy zones, PacifiCorp’s robust T&D ownership is essential for reliable interconnection. Furthermore, PacifiCorp is integrating energy storage projects in key areas, such as Oregon and Utah. Storage is vital for converting intermittent renewable energy into the 24/7 firm capacity required by demanding data center operations, ensuring grid resilience and stability as load scales up.

### V. Financial Impact and The Regulated Utility Model: BHE’s Competitive Moat

The most significant benefit derived by Berkshire Hathaway from the AI energy revolution is the stable, predictable, and accelerated growth of BHE's rate base, enabled by regulatory frameworks and fortified by superior infrastructure assets.

#### A. The Rate Base Mechanism: Predictable Earnings Growth

The power industry is structured such that regulated utilities are guaranteed a stable rate of return on approved capital investments, a mechanism central to BHE's long-term financial stability. The sheer volume of mandated capital required to accommodate the "shockingly large increases" in data center load forecasts serves as a powerful accelerator for this model. When BHE commits to massive, non-discretionary CAPEX—such as the Greenlink expansion or the doubling of capacity in Iowa —that investment is added to the rate base. This process transforms a portion of BRK’s immense cash holdings into long-term, interest-bearing assets with predictable profit margins, regardless of broader economic volatility.

This financial strength creates a crucial operational advantage that functions as an infrastructure "moat." While transmission and distribution constraints are limiting factors for rivals, leading to multi-year waits for grid connections , BHE’s financial capacity allows it to undertake and fast-track the necessary T&D and generation upgrades. For hyperscalers operating on aggressive deadlines, the ability of BHE to provide rapid, reliable interconnection (the essential competitive differentiator) makes BHE the vendor of choice. This superior speed leverages BHE’s capital strength directly into market share and accelerated rate base growth.

#### B. Ancillary Revenue and Resource Integration

Beyond traditional regulated earnings, BHE leverages its scale for ancillary benefits. BHE Renewables maintains a robust portfolio that generates energy for the wholesale market and for customers under long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs). Furthermore, the integration of AI models themselves offers potential efficiency gains, allowing BHE to use predictive analytics to optimize wind patterns and manage solar farm output.

A particularly compelling strategic move is BHE’s pursuit of **vertical integration within the critical minerals supply chain**. The 24/7 reliability required by high-density AI data centers necessitates massive battery energy storage systems, which are dependent on lithium. BHE Renewables is actively advancing research into lithium production in California’s Imperial Valley, specifically aiming to extract lithium carbonate from the geothermal brine processed at its 350 MW geothermal power plants. By developing a domestic source of lithium, BHE mitigates commodity supply chain risks for essential grid storage components. This vertical integration directly bolsters the long-term reliability and cost-effectiveness of BHE’s rapidly expanding clean energy fleet, ensuring BHE can sustainably meet the highly demanding operational requirements of the AI industry.

### VI. Strategic Headwinds and Regulatory Friction

Despite the immense opportunity, BHE must proactively manage significant regulatory, environmental, and execution challenges that accompany such explosive, localized load growth.

#### A. Regulatory and Social License to Operate

The fundamental challenge BHE faces is balancing the extraordinary capital requirements of serving new industrial mega-loads with its primary obligation to maintain affordable rates for its existing residential and commercial customer base. In territories like Iowa, public sentiment expresses concern that residential rates are being used to subsidize large, profitable data centers. This public backlash risks triggering intense regulatory scrutiny, which can delay the approval processes for necessary CAPEX—such as the advance ratemaking principles approval, which can take 9 to 10 months.

To mitigate this political and regulatory friction, BHE is adopting innovative strategies aimed at cost responsibility. As demonstrated by NV Energy, the utility is collaborating with big tech companies to have them directly share the cost burden for the required grid upgrades. Furthermore, innovative mechanisms like the CTT in Nevada are designed to shift the financial risk and commercialization cost of new, specialized generation directly onto the demanding hyperscale customers. These measures are essential for preserving BHE's social license to operate while simultaneously accelerating rate base expansion.

#### B. Resource Constraints and Environmental Hurdles

Environmental resource scarcity, particularly water, poses a critical constraint on data center development in certain BHE territories. Data centers commonly use hundreds of millions of gallons of water, primarily for evaporative cooling systems. In Nevada, this practice presents a severe threat due to the critical water shortages affecting the Colorado River basin. Although a legislative effort to ban the most water-intensive cooling centers was defeated in 2024, no such centers have been approved since February of that year.

This pronounced water scarcity in arid regions creates a geographical bias in load growth. While Nevada (NV Energy) is a high-demand hub, the severe constraints on water access could compel hyperscalers to prioritize alternative locations, such as the Midwestern region served by MidAmerican Energy, where water resources are generally more abundant. This external environmental factor influences the relative growth trajectories of BHE's subsidiaries, resulting in a strategic competitive dynamic within the BHE portfolio itself, driven by the varying difficulty of securing permits for water-intensive operations.

#### C. Execution and Supply Chain Risk

The necessity of meeting the compressed, aggressive development schedules demanded by hyperscalers introduces significant execution and supply chain risks. BHE must successfully navigate complex procedures, including securing favorable regulatory approvals, timely permitting, and managing the logistics of equipment procurement (e.g., specialized transformers and transmission line components). The pace of utility construction, which is inherently slow due to scale and regulatory oversight, must accelerate dramatically to match the speed of technological adoption. Moreover, the concentrated nature of AI load, with high energy density at specific sites, presents unique challenges to grid resilience, demanding that BHE prioritizes system robustness and redundancy while ensuring service reliability for its existing customer base.

### VII. Conclusion: BHE as the Cornerstone of BRK's AI Exposure

Berkshire Hathaway’s investment in the AI revolution is implemented not through speculative exposure to high-growth chip stocks or software developers, but through its controlling interest in Berkshire Hathaway Energy. This strategy aligns with the core philosophy of acquiring and holding capital-intensive, wide-moat, regulated businesses.

The explosive demand for energy driven by AI data centers—manifesting as requested load increases that threaten to double or triple existing grid capacity in key BHE service territories—serves as a powerful, non-discretionary growth catalyst. This structural grid shock mandates multi-billion dollar capital deployment by BHE. Because BHE operates within a regulated framework, this mandatory investment translates directly into guaranteed, predictable earnings growth via the accelerated expansion of the rate base.

BHE’s position as a large-scale owner of existing T&D infrastructure, combined with its profound financial strength, allows it to overcome the current industry bottleneck of grid interconnection delays. By rapidly building out essential infrastructure like the Greenlink Expansion and pioneering innovative, risk-sharing tariffs like the Clean Transition Tariff, BHE establishes itself as the indispensable partner for hyperscale companies.

In conclusion, the AI energy boom validates the fundamental value of BHE to BRK. The company provides a reliable mechanism for deploying a significant portion of BRK's substantial cash reserves into long-term, utility-grade assets, securing a stable and accelerating growth trajectory for the next decade. BHE’s strategy—focusing on infrastructure dominance, clean energy alignment, and regulatory innovation—positions it as the critical, low-risk infrastructure enabler required to power America’s AI future.
