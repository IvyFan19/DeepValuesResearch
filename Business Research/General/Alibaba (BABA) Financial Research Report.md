## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Alibaba’s most recent earnings (for the quarter ended March 31, 2025) showed solid growth and profitability improvements. **Revenue** reached RMB236,454 million (≈US$32.6 billion), up 7% year-over-year . **Income from operations** nearly doubled to RMB28,465 million (US$3.92 billion), a 93% jump , benefiting from improved operating efficiency and lower share-based compensation expenses. Adjusted EBITA (non-GAAP) grew 36% YoY, indicating enhanced core profitability . Net income attributable to shareholders was RMB12,382 million (US$1.71 billion); **net income** (after one-off items) spiked to RMB11,973 million (US$1.65 billion), which was **+1203% YoY** due to a favorable swing in investment valuations . Excluding those one-time gains, **non-GAAP net income** was RMB29,847 million (US$4.11 billion), up 22% YoY . Diluted earnings per ADS came in at RMB5.17 (US$0.71), and non-GAAP EPS was RMB12.52 per ADS (US$1.73), up 23% .

Operational cash flow was robust – net cash from operations for the quarter was RMB27.52 billion, up 18% . **Free cash flow (FCF)** was RMB3.743 billion (US$516 million), lower by 76% YoY as Alibaba ramped up cloud infrastructure capital expenditures . The company emphasized its strategic focus on “user first, AI-driven” growth. Notably, Cloud segment revenue grew 18% YoY (with AI-related products climbing triple-digits for a 7th straight quarter) and customer management revenue in the domestic e-commerce platforms (Taobao/Tmall) rose 12% . Management remains confident in the outlook and is investing in core businesses and emerging AI opportunities . The quarter also highlighted **shareholder returns** – Alibaba repurchased US$11.9 billion worth of shares in FY2025 (reducing the share count by 5.1%) and declared a total of US$4.6 billion in dividends . Overall, the latest quarter showed Alibaba’s growth stabilizing at mid-single-digits with improving margins and strong cash generation, even as the company invests heavily in technology and future growth drivers.

## **Business Model**

Alibaba Group operates a diversified business model centered on e-commerce and technology platforms. Its ecosystem spans online marketplaces, cloud computing, digital media, logistics, and more. **The main sources of income** are its commerce platforms – especially Taobao and Tmall (China retail marketplaces), which generate revenue from commissions and advertising (customer management) services. This China retail segment contributed roughly *46% of Alibaba’s revenue in FY2023* , making it the core revenue source. Other significant segments include Alibaba’s **International commerce retail** platforms (like **AliExpress** and regional marketplaces such as Lazada and Trendyol), **Cloud computing** (Alibaba Cloud), and the **Cainiao** logistics network – each accounting for about *11% of revenue* . The **Local consumer services** segment (delivery and map services like Ele.me and Amap) adds \~6%, while wholesale commerce (domestic and international) and digital media/entertainment each contribute low single-digits (\~2% each) . The remaining \~9% comes from “other” businesses and new initiatives. The revenue breakdown is illustrated in the chart below.

Alibaba’s **customers** reach enormous scale. Across its platforms, Alibaba served about **1.31 billion annual active consumers globally** in FY2022 – including over **1 billion consumers in China** and \~305 million consumers overseas . By 2023 the user base remained in that range (over 1.3 billion) , reflecting Alibaba’s saturation of the Chinese e-commerce market and continued international expansion. On the seller side, Alibaba’s marketplaces host **millions of merchants** (Taobao alone had over 10 million *active sellers* as of a few years ago). The company’s scale is evident in its **transaction volume** – in FY2022, Alibaba’s platforms handled **GMV of RMB8.317 trillion** (≈US$1.3 trillion) , by far the largest globally after Amazon. In terms of “units” sold, this translated to tens of billions of parcels delivered annually via its Cainiao network (e.g. over *2.3 billion orders were placed during the 2020 Singles’ Day event* alone). Alibaba also has growing *subscription-like services*: notably its 88VIP **membership program** (which offers perks across Taobao/Tmall, Youku, etc.) reached **50 million paying members** by early 2025 , reflecting a highly engaged cohort of loyal shoppers. Additionally, **Alibaba Cloud** serves hundreds of thousands of enterprise customers (including 90,000+ using its AI models in 2024 ), though cloud revenues are usage-based rather than fixed subscriptions. Overall, Alibaba’s business model monetizes **huge user traffic** across commerce (via commissions and ads), plus **value-added services** like cloud computing fees, logistics services, and media content monetization – leveraging an ecosystem with unprecedented scale in users and transactions.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

Alibaba faces several headwinds that could impact its performance in the short run as well as structural risks longer term:

-   **Short-Term/Temporary Factors:** In the near term, Alibaba’s business can be buffeted by **macroeconomic and cyclical conditions** – for example, a slowdown in Chinese consumer spending or COVID-related disruptions have periodically weakened e-commerce growth. China’s economy in 2022–2023 experienced slower retail sales and rolling lockdowns, which dampened Alibaba’s commerce revenue (it actually saw a rare revenue decline in FY2023) as consumers pulled back . **U.S.–China trade tensions** are another ongoing overhang; tariffs or export restrictions can reduce cross-border commerce and make Chinese goods less competitive . Geopolitical news (like U.S. sanctions on Chinese tech or investor sentiment shifts due to political rhetoric) has added to Alibaba stock volatility. In addition, Alibaba has **ramped up investments and subsidies** in the short term to defend market share – for instance, in 2023 it committed billions to an “instant commerce” (grocery delivery) subsidy program and accelerated spending on AI R&D . These initiatives temporarily squeeze margins, as seen in recent quarters’ lower profits, but are aimed at spurring future growth. Inflation in logistics and labor costs can also pressure near-term profits. However, these factors are considered transitory. As Alibaba’s CFO noted, the company is focused on **“sustainable, high-quality revenue growth and optimizing cost structure”** even amidst uncertain times , implying that short-term earnings dips from investments or macro slowdowns are expected to normalize once conditions improve.

-   **Longer-Term/Permanent Risks:** Alibaba also contends with structural challenges that could permanently influence its business model. One major factor is **regulatory and political risk in China**. In late 2020 and 2021, Chinese authorities undertook a sweeping **tech sector crackdown** – Alibaba paid a record **RMB18.2 billion (\~$2.8 billion) antitrust fine** in 2021 for alleged monopolistic practices , and regulators imposed new e-commerce rules (like prohibiting forced exclusivity for merchants). The government also took a 1% “golden share” stake in an Alibaba media subsidiary , signaling tighter oversight. While Alibaba has **aligned itself with regulators** (it promptly paid the fine and pledged compliance), the episode highlighted the risk of policy changes. Future regulatory actions – whether antitrust, data security (Alibaba had to improve how it handles user data), or even VIE structure limitations – could cap Alibaba’s growth or profitability. Another long-term risk is **intensifying competition**. Domestically, rivals like *Pinduoduo* and *JD.com* have eroded Alibaba’s e-commerce dominance by undercutting prices or innovating new models (e.g. Pinduoduo’s viral group-buying). Even *ByteDance’s* Douyin (TikTok) has gained traction in social commerce, diverting user attention and ad budgets. Alibaba’s market share in China online retail has stabilized but no longer growing, and it must invest heavily to retain users (e.g. waiving certain merchant fees ). On the cloud side, Alibaba Cloud competes with Tencent Cloud and Huawei Cloud in China, and Amazon AWS and Microsoft Azure globally – formidable rivals with deep pockets. **Global geopolitical tensions** also pose a strategic risk: the U.S. has at times threatened to delist Chinese ADRs (including BABA) over auditing disputes (resolved in 2022), and American institutional investors may limit exposure to Chinese firms due to perceived national security concerns. Export controls on advanced chips and AI technology could slow Alibaba Cloud’s tech advantage in the long run if it can’t access leading U.S. semiconductors (important for AI training). Furthermore, **changing consumer behavior** presents a risk – e-commerce in China is maturing, and growth has shifted to new channels like livestreaming commerce and community group buying. Alibaba must adapt its platforms (recently integrating with social app Xiaohongshu ) to stay relevant, or risk permanently losing younger shoppers to newer apps. Lastly, Alibaba’s sprawling empire faces **execution complexity**: its 2023 restructuring into six separate units is meant to increase agility, but also means parts of the business could be spun off or face differing futures. Long-term investors must monitor if Alibaba can maintain its **ecosystem synergy** or if the sum of the parts diminishes with separation.

In summary, **short-term headwinds** like China’s economic fluctuations, temporary cost surges (from subsidies or COVID) and external tensions can impact Alibaba’s quarterly results but are not expected to derail its fundamental business. **Long-term risks**, however, such as regulatory changes (e.g. stricter antitrust enforcement or data laws), new competitive paradigms in commerce and cloud, and geopolitical decoupling, could **permanently reshape Alibaba’s growth trajectory**. For example, U.S. trade restrictions remain a “Sword of Damocles” that could limit cross-border e-commerce and cloud expansion , and domestic competition from Pinduoduo and others is steadily eroding its market share moat . Alibaba’s ability to navigate these rough seas – by innovating, engaging regulators, and globalizing its business – will determine whether it can sustain long-term value creation despite the headwinds.

## **Cost Structure**

Alibaba’s cost structure reflects its mix of platform businesses and more traditional commerce operations. The company enjoys inherently high **gross margins** in its core marketplace model (since it mainly provides an online platform for third-party sellers), but as it has expanded into lower-margin areas like direct retail, logistics, and brick-and-mortar, its costs have risen significantly.

**Fixed vs Variable Costs:** A large portion of Alibaba’s expenses are **fixed or semi-fixed** in nature – for instance, the heavy investments in technology infrastructure (data centers for Alibaba Cloud, servers and AI computing for its platforms) and product development are largely fixed costs. Alibaba spends substantially on **R&D** (product development expenses were about US$8.7 billion in FY2022, \~7% of revenue ) to improve technology and innovate new services; these costs do not directly scale with short-term transaction volume. Similarly, overhead like corporate staff, headquarters, and depreciation of infrastructure are fixed. However, Alibaba also incurs significant **variable costs** tied to transaction volume and revenue. Its **cost of revenue** (which includes traffic acquisition costs, payment processing fees, logistics fulfillment costs for certain businesses, content costs for media, etc.) moves with business scale. In FY2022, cost of revenue was RMB539.45 billion (≈US$85.1 billion), about **63% of revenue** – up from 59% a year prior . This increase was due to consolidating more cost-intensive businesses (like supermarket chain Sun Art and other direct sales) and higher logistics costs. In general, running a platform like Taobao has low incremental cost per order, but **running new retail stores or providing free shipping has much higher unit costs**. Thus, Alibaba’s cost structure has a mix: the traditional marketplace operations are very scalable (each additional GMV yields high profit), but newer businesses bring more variable costs.

**Cost Components:**

-   *Cost per unit/order:* For pure marketplace transactions, Alibaba’s cost per unit is minimal – it mainly covers maintaining the website/app and payment processing. These costs are far less than the commission or ad revenue Alibaba earns, yielding high gross profit. However, for Alibaba’s direct sales (like its Tmall Supermarket, Freshippo grocery stores, and cross-border retail where Alibaba owns inventory), cost per unit sold includes the **cost of goods**and supply chain – often 80–90% of sales for groceries/electronics. For example, overall **gross profit margin**declined from \~62% in FY2017 to \~37% in FY2022 as direct sales and low-margin segments became a bigger portion of revenue. Similarly, in local consumer services (food delivery) Alibaba bears fulfillment costs, making those transactions low-margin. Essentially, Alibaba’s **unit economics** vary widely by segment – an online ad click on Taobao has near-100% margin, whereas delivering a meal via Ele.me might have almost no margin.

-   **Labor Expenses:** Alibaba’s workforce is a major cost driver. The company had about **205,000 employees** as of March 2024 , a number that grew rapidly over the decade as it expanded into new areas. Staff costs include salaries and a large amount of **share-based compensation (SBC)** for talent retention. In earlier years, SBC was significant – in FY2022, Alibaba’s total operating expenses included RMB46.5 billion in SBC (approx 5% of revenue) which was one reason operating margins declined . R&D personnel (engineers for cloud, AI, etc.) are a big component of fixed costs – product development expense was \~US$10.9 billion in FY2021 , reflecting thousands of highly paid engineers. Alibaba’s **general and administrative (G&A)** expenses also include substantial payroll – G&A was \~US$7.4 billion in FY2022 . Labor cost trends: Alibaba has started to **rationalize headcount** since 2022 (headcount actually fell by around 4% in 2022) to improve efficiency after years of growth. However, it still must pay up for skilled tech workers and has *notable employee benefit costs* (it set up a RMB100 billion “common prosperity” fund in 2021 at government urging, effectively an extra expense spread over years). Overall, while not broken out explicitly, we can infer labor is one of the largest cost elements after cost of goods – perhaps on the order of 15–20% of revenue when including SBC.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** Alibaba spends heavily on **sales and marketing (S&M)** to acquire users and promote its platforms, especially in newer initiatives. In FY2022, sales and marketing expense was RMB119.8 billion (\~US$18.9 billion), which was **14% of revenue** . This was up from \~11% of revenue a year earlier , as Alibaba increased promotions and subsidies to fend off competitors (e.g. spending on Taobao Deals, community group-buy deals, etc.) . Marketing spend includes online/offline advertising, user coupons and loyalty rewards (like the 88VIP program discounts), and merchant incentives. In recent years, Alibaba’s S&M surged with initiatives like Taobao Deals (targeting lower-tier cities) and Lazada’s expansion in Southeast Asia. While these costs are partly variable (e.g. a targeted rebate per transaction), they also represent strategic investment waves. Importantly, **marketing costs tend to rise in competitive downturns** – for instance, to counter JD.com’s campaigns or Pinduoduo’s subsidy war, Alibaba sacrificed short-term margins by offering **RMB7 billion in merchant fee rebates and subsidies in 2022** . As competition stabilizes or if customer acquisition saturates, we could see marketing expense growth moderate. Historically, Alibaba enjoyed very low marketing ratios when it had organic traffic dominance, but now S&M is one of the fastest-growing costs.

-   **Most Significant Costs:** The single largest cost item is **Cost of Revenue**, which includes payments to partners and cost of inventory for direct sales. It was 63% of revenue in FY2022 (over $85 billion) , dominating Alibaba’s P&L. Within cost of revenue, major components are: **inventory costs** for Alibaba’s self-operated retail (e.g. Sun Art grocery merchandise), **logistics and fulfillment costs** (Cainiao’s shipping expenses, warehouse operation, delivery personnel fees), **content acquisition costs** (for Youku streaming rights), and **traffic acquisition costs** (payments to third-party sites or apps that drive traffic to Alibaba’s platforms). After cost of revenue, the next biggest expense is typically **Sales & Marketing** (\~14% of revenue in 2022) . Product development (R&D) and G&A are each on the order of 5–7% of revenue . So in summary, the largest costs for Alibaba are those related to **fulfillment and the goods it sells** (especially in newer retail formats), followed by **marketing outlays**. These have been the primary drivers of margin compression in recent years.

-   **Trends Over Time & Economic Conditions:** Alibaba’s cost structure has evolved from asset-light to more asset-heavy. During its high-growth phase (2014–2017), Alibaba had **extremely high margins** – operating margin was \~30%+ – because its revenue was mostly commission and ad fees with minimal direct costs. Over the past 5 years, margins fell as costs grew faster than revenue: for instance, cost of revenue rose from 57% to 63% of sales from FY2021 to FY2022 , and S&M rose from 11% to 14% . This reflects heavy spending during economic downcycles (like the COVID-hit 2020–2022 period) when Alibaba aided merchants with fee reductions and invested in user growth even as revenue growth slowed to single digits . During **strong economic periods**, Alibaba’s fixed-cost leverage becomes evident – e.g. in FY2021, revenue jumped 41% and although costs rose too, the company could absorb them and still post solid profits (operating profit +32% YoY) . In **weak periods**, Alibaba has shown a willingness to protect its ecosystem at the expense of margins (as seen in FY2022, when profit fell sharply due to investments and one-off fines ). Another trend is **greater cost discipline** in recent quarters – management has pledged to trim expenses in areas like user acquisition and headcount in response to slower growth . Indeed, in FY2023 Alibaba managed to keep operating income roughly flat despite a revenue dip, implying some cost cutting offset the top-line pressure . Over a full cycle, we’d expect Alibaba’s cost ratios to improve in good times (when revenue growth outpaces cost growth) and worsen in downturns (when it invests counter-cyclically and faces deleverage). The **cloud segment** also influences cost dynamics: building out cloud infrastructure requires high fixed capex and depreciation, which suppresses near-term margins (cloud had been barely breakeven). As cloud scales, its fixed costs per unit drop, improving margins. For example, Alibaba’s depreciation and amortization was about 12% of gross profit in FY2020, then peaked at \~15% during heavy cloud buildout , and has started to decline toward \~11% . In summary, Alibaba’s cost structure is now a balance of **platform economics (with high fixed costs but low variable costs)** and **commerce operations (with high variable costs)**. Economic booms amplify its platform operating leverage (driving huge profit on incremental sales), whereas economic challenges or competition force it to spend more on low-margin areas, compressing profitability. The company’s long-term strategy is to keep investing in technology and user experience – meaning it may tolerate elevated costs in the short run, but historically Alibaba has demonstrated an ability to adjust and improve efficiency when needed.

## **Management & Governance**

Alibaba’s management team blends its founding entrepreneurs with experienced professionals, overseen by a unique partnership governance structure.

**Who are the key leaders?** Alibaba was co-founded by **Jack Ma** in 1999, who served as CEO and later Executive Chairman until retiring in 2019 . Today, the top executives include:

-   **Daniel Zhang** – he was Alibaba’s CEO from 2015 to September 2023 and Chairman from 2019 to 2023, known for driving key initiatives like the Singles’ Day shopping festival. (Zhang stepped down in 2023 amid the restructuring and briefly led the Cloud division before departing fully.)

-   **Joseph “Joe” Tsai** – a co-founder and long-time executive, Tsai is now *Executive Chairman (since September 2023)* . He has been Alibaba’s vice chairman and is one of the principal architects of its financial and legal structure. Tsai provides continuity as a close confidant of Jack Ma and major shareholder .

-   **Eddie Yongming Wu** – one of Alibaba’s earliest employees (a technologist who developed the Aliwangwang messaging platform), Eddie Wu became *Chief Executive Officer in September 2023* . He heads the daily operations now and also chairs the core Taobao & Tmall e-commerce group . Wu is considered a “next-generation” leader but with founder status (part of the Alibaba Partnership) .

-   **Toby Xu** – the current Chief Financial Officer (since 2022). He succeeded Maggie Wu (who was CFO for a long tenure). Toby Xu has been vocal about capital allocation, buybacks, and improving shareholder returns .

-   Other notable executives include **J. Michael Evans** (President and Director – a former Goldman Sachs exec focused on international expansion) , **Maggie Wei Wu** (former CFO, now on the Board of Directors) , and **Jiang Fan** (head of the International Digital Commerce unit). The **Board of Directors** is chaired by Joe Tsai and includes independent directors like Yahoo co-founder **Jerry Yang** , former PepsiCo executive **Wan Ling Martello**, private equity leader **Weijian Shan**, among others . Alibaba’s governance is influenced by the **Alibaba Partnership** – a group of 29 partners (founders and management) with the right to nominate a majority of board members . This structure, while unconventional, has allowed management continuity but also means Jack Ma and his allies exert significant control despite Ma currently holding no formal title.

**Past Performance and Trustworthiness:** Alibaba’s management has a mixed but generally positive track record in executing goals. On one hand, they delivered spectacular growth for over a decade, often exceeding targets (for example, meeting the goal of serving 1 billion Chinese consumers by 2024). Former CEO Daniel Zhang was known for **operational excellence**, launching new businesses (like Freshippo grocery and Cainiao’s expansion) and he didn’t shy away from acknowledging challenges on earnings calls. The leadership has, for the most part, **been transparent with investors** about core business metrics (Alibaba voluntarily began reporting annual active consumers, cloud segment results, etc., to boost clarity ). That said, there have been instances that raised questions. **Jack Ma’s 2011 spin-off of Alipay** to a separate entity (controlled by him) without immediately informing major shareholders Yahoo and SoftBank caused an uproar and concerns about governance integrity . Alibaba later resolved it by compensating those investors, but the incident highlighted that management would take bold actions which might disadvantage some shareholders in the short term. In general, since becoming a public company in 2014, Alibaba has not had major accounting scandals – it was investigated by the SEC in 2016 over how it accounted for logistics affiliate Cainiao and Singles’ Day GMV , but the company provided more disclosure and no enforcement action ensued (Alibaba’s increased transparency in response was noted ). Management does **set high goals and usually follows through**. For instance, Jack Ma famously spoke about focusing on long-term value; indeed Alibaba consistently reinvested profits into new ventures as promised, even if it hurt near-term margins . On investor calls, executives like Maggie Wu (ex-CFO) and Daniel Zhang answered most analyst questions directly, often addressing issues of competition and regulation head-on.

One critique has been that Alibaba **can be slow to admit problems** – e.g., they were initially dismissive of new rival Pinduoduo, then later had to catch up. However, by FY2022 Zhang openly discussed increased competition and the need to adapt strategy , showing a pragmatic approach. The management’s credibility took a bit of a hit during the Ant Group IPO cancellation saga – Jack Ma’s public criticism of regulators in 2020 led to Ant’s IPO being halted, catching investors off guard. Since then, Ma stepped back and Alibaba’s tone with authorities became very compliant (for example, Alibaba **accepted the 2021 antitrust penalty without contest** and stated it “would correct its errors”). This indicates management is keen to align with government expectations, which is crucial in China but can conflict with Western investor interests at times.

As for **tenure**, Alibaba’s top team has remarkable longevity. Joe Tsai and Eddie Wu have been with the company since inception (over 20 years). Many business unit leaders (like Trudy Dai, who now leads industrial e-commerce, or Jianhang Jin in public affairs) are long-serving “Aliren” as well. This stability tends to foster a deep understanding of the ecosystem. There has been some turnover in recent years (e.g. CFO Maggie Wu handed off to Toby Xu, cloud division saw a new CEO after Zhang’s exit), but overall continuity is strong. **No major integrity scandals** have been publicly raised about current management’s personal conduct – unlike some companies, there haven’t been fraud allegations against Alibaba’s books that held up (short-seller Jim Chanos questioned some metrics in 2016, but nothing materialized beyond the SEC inquiry ). Alibaba’s culture, instilled by Jack Ma, emphasizes customer trust and long-term thinking, which management has generally upheld.

**Alignment of Values with Shareholders:** Alibaba’s leadership, particularly the co-founders, have significant skin in the game. *Co-founder Joe Tsai* is the second-largest individual shareholder with about **1.4% of shares (309 million shares)** – at current prices worth around $3.5–4 billion. Jack Ma, though no longer an executive, still held an estimated **4.8% stake as of 2020** (he has sold some since, but likely retains a few percent) . Collectively, insiders (via the partnership) controlled roughly 10%+ of Alibaba’s equity before SoftBank’s exit. This means management’s wealth is largely tied to Alibaba’s stock performance. Importantly, **Alibaba instituted large share buybacks** which indicate management’s confidence that the stock is undervalued – e.g., in 2022 the board upsized the repurchase program to $25 billion . Rather than paying themselves big cash dividends historically, they reinvested earnings, implying belief in higher returns from growing the business. In fact, **Jack Ma and Joe Tsai personally bought additional shares during stock dips** – it was reported that in Q4 2022, Ma acquired $50 million worth in HK and Tsai’s family office bought **$151 million worth of US ADRs**when the stock plunged to multi-year lows. They even postponed planned stock sales because they felt the shares were “strongly below intrinsic value” . Such actions strongly align their interests with long-term shareholders, as they essentially “bought the dip” with their own money.

Alibaba’s top management compensation is also structured to favor long-term performance. For example, Daniel Zhang’s pay was modest relative to Alibaba’s size (reportedly under $2 million salary) with most rewards in stock units vesting over time. Many executives are Alibaba founders or early employees who became billionaires from stock appreciation, not high salaries. While Alibaba did not pay dividends until the recent special payout in 2025, management signaled future return of capital via share buybacks and that special dividend of $4.6 billion – a move welcomed by investors. This suggests that at mature stages, management is willing to return excess cash, showing regard for shareholder interests beyond growth.

That said, a potential governance concern is the **partnership control**: it allows insiders to nominate board members and potentially override public shareholders on director elections . This dual-class-like structure means shareholders rely on management’s benevolence. So far, Alibaba’s insiders used this power largely to maintain strategic stability (e.g. ensuring Jack Ma’s philosophy continues). However, one could argue this entrenches management – if there were serious missteps, replacing the board/management would be difficult without their consent. Critics (including corporate governance experts) have noted this structure may not “function to benefit all investors” if not for the reputation and commitments of the founders . The good news is Alibaba’s founders have by and large maintained a decent reputation and have not abused minority shareholders – there have been no related-party deal scandals or self-dealing allegations since IPO. The **integrity of management** is occasionally questioned on forums regarding transparency (for example, some investors wanted clearer Ant Group ownership accounting), but by and large Alibaba’s management is considered high-caliber and **committed to Alibaba’s long-term value**. The company’s motto of “customers first, employees second, shareholders third” might rank shareholders last, but the logic (often explained by Jack Ma) is that focusing on customer value ultimately benefits shareholders. Indeed, Alibaba’s leadership has reinvested in improving user experience continuously (e.g. investing in logistics speed, AI capabilities) rather than chasing short-term profit, which long-term investors like Charlie Munger initially praised.

In summary, **Alibaba’s management team** is seasoned and largely trustworthy, albeit with an unconventional governance setup. They have executed ambitious goals (global expansion, cloud leadership in Asia) well, with some stumbles (regulatory run-ins) along the way. They generally do not hide problems – for example, during the regulatory crackdowns, management openly discussed impacts and even split the company structure to appease regulators . The leaders have **aligned incentives** given substantial equity ownership and have demonstrated alignment by buying shares personally and enacting buybacks when the stock was undervalued . The key for investors is that this management has a **long-term vision** (sometimes at the expense of short-term metrics), and so far they have delivered growth and adapted strategy when needed. Barring any major governance surprises, Alibaba’s management appears well-intentioned and capable of steering the company through both competitive and regulatory challenges in a way that ultimately benefits patient shareholders.

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns**

Alibaba’s shareholder base has evolved over time from concentrated early investors to mostly broad institutional ownership today. The **top shareholders** historically included strategic investors like **SoftBank**, co-founder **Jack Ma**, and co-founder **Joe Tsai**, as well as large asset managers.

-   **SoftBank Group** (Japan) was Alibaba’s largest shareholder from its early investment in 2000 until 2022. At IPO SoftBank held \~34% of Alibaba, which it gradually reduced. As of 2024, SoftBank’s stake was down to **14.2%**(≈2.7 billion shares) , and by mid-2023 SoftBank reportedly sold most of its remaining shares via forward contracts (potentially leaving it with &lt;2% now) . SoftBank’s presence on Alibaba’s board ended in 2020 when CEO Masayoshi Son stepped down, and since then SoftBank has been a passive investor focused on monetizing its stake. SoftBank did not engage in activist voting against Alibaba’s management – in fact, it consistently supported Alibaba’s board nominations and strategy (SoftBank and Alibaba’s partnership was long-term and amicable). The decision by SoftBank to sell down was driven by its own financial strategy (raising cash after Vision Fund losses), not dissatisfaction with Alibaba. This means **SoftBank did not mount any opposing votes**; it generally voted with management on AGM resolutions. For instance, before exiting, SoftBank would have voted in favor of Alibaba’s annual director slate and other proposals, which routinely passed with &gt;95% approval . Their stance was aligned with management (given SoftBank’s board representation for many years). SoftBank’s large share sales in 2022–2023 put some pressure on Alibaba’s stock price but were executed via off-market deals so as not to disrupt the market.

-   **Jack Ma** and **Joe Tsai** remain significant individual shareholders. As of 2020, Jack Ma owned \~4.8% of Alibaba , though he has since reduced it (in 2023 he was said to plan selling a small portion via his foundation, but later postponed it because he felt the stock was undervalued ). Joe Tsai currently holds about **1.4%** of shares (309 million shares) , making him the largest *insider* holder. Neither Ma nor Tsai hold management roles now (Ma has no formal role; Tsai is executive chairman), but they are Alibaba Partnership members with board nomination power . They generally **vote their shares in line with management proposals** – effectively *they are management*, so there’s no divergence. There haven’t been contentious shareholder proposals from them; instead, they have used influence internally (e.g. deciding on buybacks or leadership changes). For example, when Alibaba’s board elections occur, Ma and Tsai’s shares help easily elect the nominated directors (AGM voting results show \~99% support for each director candidate in 2024 , reflecting major holders’ alignment). Neither founder has attempted to wield votes against minority shareholder interests – if anything, their significant voting power (roughly 6–7% combined when SoftBank was around) has ensured continuity of Alibaba’s preferred governance.

-   **Institutional Investors:** With SoftBank’s exit, **PrimeCap Management** (a US-based fund) was noted as a top institutional holder with \~21.9 million shares (\~0.9%) and **Sanders Capital** with \~18.4 million shares (\~0.77%) as of late 2024. However, these percentages are small. The bulk of Alibaba’s float is held by large index-tracking institutions like **BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street**, etc., each likely holding 2–3% through various funds. For instance, Yahoo Finance data shows BlackRock and Vanguard as among top shareholders but none above \~3%. These institutions tend to be passive and vote with proxy advisor recommendations. Given Alibaba is Cayman-incorporated and has the partnership structure, there have been *no significant shareholder-initiated proposals or proxy fights*. The annual meeting typically has only management’s proposals (director elections, etc.). **Voting record:** At the 2024 AGM (General Meeting on Aug 22, 2024), all resolutions were passed by wide margins . For example, the special resolution to amend the Articles (to accommodate the restructuring) passed with 99.3% votes in favor . The ordinary resolution to adopt the 2024 Equity Incentive Plan passed with 95.0% approval . Even potentially sensitive issues like share issuance mandates got \~97% approval . These high approval rates indicate that top shareholders **voted in lockstep with management’s recommendations**. There has been no instance of a significant institutional owner publicly opposing Alibaba’s management or pushing an agenda via voting. Alibaba’s top owners also include Chinese institutions (like government-linked funds) to some extent: for example, an arm of the China Investment Corporation took a small stake. But none hold enough to challenge the partnership.

-   **Shareholder Activism or Topics:** Unlike some Western companies, Alibaba hasn’t faced external activist campaigns. One semi-exception: in early 2022, *minority investor Ryan Cohen* (famous for GameStop saga) reportedly built a small stake and pushed for more buybacks and capital return. Not through formal votes, but via letters, it’s said he urged Alibaba to boost its buyback given its cash hoard and low valuation . Management did in fact enlarge the buyback to $25B around that time . It’s unclear if Cohen’s pressure influenced that decision, but management’s actions aligned with what value investors wanted. As for AGM topics, standard items like re-electing Joe Tsai, Michael Evans, and others to the board in 2024 all sailed through . Major shareholders like SoftBank (before exit) and the Alibaba Partnership would have voted *for* these. Another key topic was Alibaba’s **restructuring into six units** announced in March 2023. This did not require an AGM vote for implementation (it’s an internal re-org), but any future IPO/spin-off of units might. As of the latest AGM, no shareholder votes on spin-offs have occurred yet. But management indicated each unit could seek independent financing, which shareholders mostly welcomed as unlocking value. Given high insider ownership alignment, if any such vote comes (e.g. a unit IPO requiring shareholder approval), top shareholders are expected to support management’s plan.

In terms of **shareholder rights execution**, Alibaba’s general meetings have been formalities with very high quorums (in 2024, \~14.5 billion Ordinary Shares were present, \~76% of shares) , which is huge turnout – likely reflecting that SoftBank’s and insiders’ shares were voted. There hasn’t been a case of top shareholders voting against management. Even on potentially dilutive proposals like authorizing the board to issue up to 10% new shares, there was \~97% approval , indicating large holders (who could be diluted) were comfortable giving that flexibility.

**Top Voted Topics and Stances:** At the 2024 AGM, notable resolutions included the **Share Issuance Mandate** (passed with 97.2% for ), the **Share Repurchase Mandate** (99.7% for ), and adoption of a new equity incentive plan (95.0% for ). The near-unanimous support shows top shareholders want Alibaba to have the ability to manage capital actively (issue shares if needed, but more importantly continue buybacks). On the equity plan (which can potentially dilute shares by granting stock to employees), there was slightly lower approval (95% – likely some institutional investors routinely vote against large option pools on governance principle), but still a vast majority in favor, including all major insiders.

In short, **top shareholders have essentially executed their votes in line with management on all major issues**. None of the biggest shareholders have agitated publicly for changes via voting. Instead, any influence has been behind the scenes (like minor pressure for buybacks). As of 2025, the largest external holder, SoftBank, has cashed out, leaving Alibaba’s ownership more in the hands of the Alibaba Partnership and dispersed institutional investors. This likely means continued smooth sailing for management at shareholder meetings. We should expect ordinary resolutions (like director elections, auditor ratifications) to keep passing comfortably. If there were any contentious topic in future – say a proposal to delist from NYSE or move primary listing to Hong Kong – how would shareholders react? Given the trust in management and insiders’ control, they would likely back it if framed as in Alibaba’s best interest. One possible slight friction could be if Alibaba’s units go for IPO – public shareholders might want shares of those or dividends from proceeds. But it’s premature; management has indicated value will accrue to Alibaba shareholders either via spin-offs or improved conglomerate value. Major investors have not opposed that plan.

In conclusion, Alibaba’s **top shareholders are supportive and aligned**. Insiders (Ma, Tsai) and long-term holders voted consistently *for* management’s proposals. There has been no activist rebellion or significant no-vote on any topic – for instance, in 2024 all directors (including independent ones) were elected with \~95-99% approval , reflecting broad confidence. The “top voted topics” at recent AGMs have mostly been routine corporate matters, all receiving overwhelming support. This unity is partly thanks to Alibaba’s partnership structure which concentrates voting power with insiders who have a long-term outlook and partly due to the lack of viable alternative proposals from any large shareholder. Thus, Alibaba’s management enjoys strong shareholder backing, and the company’s strategic decisions (buybacks, restructuring, etc.) have been endorsed by its largest owners.

## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

Warren Buffett’s investment criteria can be applied to Alibaba to evaluate whether it qualifies as a “great company at a fair price.” Below is a checklist of key Buffett-style standards and how Alibaba measures up, based on the historical metrics above:

1.  **Valuation Discipline:**

    -   **P/E ≤ 15 (vs. firm’s history):** Alibaba’s *current* price-to-earnings ratio is around 14.6× (Aug 2025) , which falls under the &lt;=15 threshold. However, this is largely due to depressed earnings in recent years. For most of the past decade, Alibaba traded at much higher multiples – often 20×–50× earnings . During its high-growth phase, P/E was well above 15 (e.g. \~30–40× in 2017–2018) so it wouldn’t have met this criterion then. Only after the stock plummeted 70% from its peak did P/E drop into value territory. On a forward basis, if we normalize earnings (excluding one-offs), Alibaba’s P/E is in the low teens now, which **passes** this benchmark. But one should note that the sub-10 P/Es seen in 2022–2023 were influenced by temporarily low earnings (Buffett would consider “look-through” earnings). Today’s valuation ($280B market cap vs \~$18B net income TTM) is finally reasonable . Historically though, Alibaba often failed this rule until recently.

    -   **P/B ≤ 1.5:** Alibaba’s current price-to-book is about 1.9× (and was as low as \~1.1× in late 2022 ). That is slightly above the &lt;=1.5 bar. During 2022–2023 when sentiment was worst, P/B actually briefly went under 1.5 (even near 1.1, meaning market cap nearly equaled book value ). Over most of the decade, Alibaba had a P/B between 5× and 10× in its high-growth days , far above 1.5. It wasn’t until the stock collapse by 2022 that P/B came into classic “value” range. As of FY2024, P/B \~1.14× , so it *did* pass then. With the stock’s rebound in 2023–2025, P/B climbed back closer to \~2× – a bit higher than the strict cutoff. So on a current basis, **nearly passes** (just slightly high). On average of last 10 years, no – but Buffett would care about *today’s* price, which is arguably still low relative to book (especially considering Alibaba’s book value likely understates intangible asset value).

    -   **Margin of Safety (Pay ≥25% below conservative intrinsic value):** This is subjective, but many value investors argued Alibaba’s intrinsic value is far above its 2022–2023 price. For instance, using a sum-of-parts or DCF, one might estimate Alibaba’s intrinsic value north of $200/share, meaning the stock in late 2022 at \~$60–80 had a 50%+ margin of safety. Buffett likes a margin of safety; given Alibaba’s stock traded at \~0.7× book at one point and under 10× normalized earnings, one could say it had a significant margin of safety then. Today at \~$120/share, the margin of safety is smaller. If we assume a conservative intrinsic value (say \~$160, roughly 15× forward earnings of \~$10/share), the current price is \~25% below that. It appears that **at its lows, Alibaba certainly offered &gt;25% margin of safety**, and even now it may be around that threshold. This criterion seems **arguably met** – Alibaba in 2022–2023 was priced for disaster, which hasn’t materialized, so an investor would have had a cushion. (Of course, this depends on one’s intrinsic value estimate; given the uncertainties, some may hair-cut the value heavily due to regulatory risk). Overall, in 2021 and before, Alibaba did *not* have a MOS (it was popular and richly priced). But as of mid-2020s, it does appear undervalued relative to fundamental assets and earnings, satisfying Buffett’s valuation discipline in hindsight.

2.  **Cash Generation & Allocation:**

    -   **Free Cash Flow positive each year; FCF/Revenue &gt;5%:** Alibaba has generated positive free cash flow every single year in the past decade . Even in tougher years, FCF margins remained well above 5% – for example, the lowest was \~16% in FY2024 , and often it was in the 30%+ range during earlier years . This easily **passes**. Alibaba’s business throws off billions in FCF (FY2023 had $29.2B FCF, \~23% of revenue ). So it’s a strong cash generator, a positive in Buffett’s eyes. In terms of allocation, Alibaba historically reinvested most of this cash into growth (via capex, acquisitions, etc.), but in recent years it has also ramped up share repurchases. By Buffett’s standard, the key is that cash is generated consistently and not squandered. Alibaba’s robust net cash position and buybacks (nearly $10B+ repurchased annually in 2022–2023 ) show good use of cash. So the **FCF criterion is clearly met**.

    -   **Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:** This metric examines how effectively earnings retained by the company translate into additional earnings. Over 2015–2021, Alibaba retained virtually all earnings (no dividends until 2025) and grew net income from $3.9B to $22.9B . That’s an increase of \~$19B on cumulative retained earnings of \~$79B (from $25B to $164B equity, excluding new capital) . Roughly, that’s about a 24% return on retained earnings – which is excellent. However, from 2021 to 2024, retained earnings grew further but net income actually fell (from $22.9B to $11.0B) due to the big profit dip in 2022. So if measured up to 2024, the recent return on incremental retained earnings looks poor (earnings did not grow, they shrank despite more capital). But adjusting for one-off losses and now seeing FY2025 rebound to $17.8B , it appears Alibaba is getting earnings back up. Over the whole 10-year span, Alibaba turned \~$90B of retained profit into about $14B/year higher profit (from $3.9B to $17.8B), which is like a \~15% CAGR of earnings – not far off from 12%. Given the uneven ride (big overshoot then decline), it’s a mixed picture. One could argue from inception Alibaba easily cleared 12% ROE and ROIC historically , but recent years were sub-12%. On balance, Alibaba’s long-term reinvestment track record is strong (cloud, logistics, etc., are starting to pay off with segment profitability improving). **Verdict:** somewhat borderline recently, but over longer term probably **yes** – Alibaba’s retained earnings have generally earned high returns (until the regulatory storm knocked them). With 12% being a high bar, Alibaba around 2015-2020 was far above it; 2022 fell below; 2023–2025 recovering. I’d cautiously say **passes** (especially if one normalizes out extraordinary issues). Buffett might penalize the inconsistency, but the fundamental earning power on those investments (e.g. cloud, fintech stake, etc.) could yield &gt;12% if things normalize.

3.  **Growth & Predictability:**

    -   **EPS positive & rising every year for 10 yrs:** Alibaba’s *reported* EPS has **not risen every year**. It was positive each year (no losses), but not a smooth rise – notably, EPS dropped in FY2017 (due to heavy SBC and investment losses) and FY2022 (due to big write-downs and fine). For example, EPS (diluted) went from $0.45 in 2015 to \~$3.59 in 2021 (growing overall), but then down to \~$0.92 in 2022 before recovering. Buffett likes consistent upward EPS; Alibaba fails that consistency. Even ignoring one-offs, adjusted EPS also dipped in 2017 and 2022 . So **does not pass** this stringent test of steady growth. The business grew tremendously overall, but volatility in margins and one-time charges caused earnings swings – something Buffett’s ideal companies (like Coca-Cola) didn’t have.

    -   **Book value per share 10-yr CAGR ≥ 10%, no year of decline:** Alibaba’s book value per share compounded at \~25% annually from 2014 to 2024 (from $9.69 to $62.71) , well above 10%. However, book value per share *did* dip slightly in recent years – FY2023 equity was lower than FY2022, and FY2024 lower again . The declines were small (-5.8% in 2024 ) and due to share buybacks (reducing equity) plus currency translation and valuation losses. But Buffett’s criterion of “no year of contraction” fails here because Alibaba’s shareholder equity peaked in 2022 then fell a bit (they paid out cash for buybacks/dividends). On the other hand, *per share* book value might not have declined because buybacks shrink shares; indeed BVPS stayed roughly flat ($63) from 2022 to 2024 . Actually BVPS ticked down marginally (from $63.37 to $62.71) . So there was a slight per-share decline (\~1%) in FY2024 – technically a contraction. Overall, growth rate of BVPS is excellent (&gt;10%), but the streak was broken at the end. So **partially passes**: long-term yes, but not a perfect streak. Buffett usually forgives a small dip if due to good reasons (like buybacks or FX) – and in Alibaba’s case, the equity dip was largely due to returning capital to shareholders (a positive). So one could argue it’s not a red flag. The key is book value grew enormously over 10 years, implying reinvested earnings created value – that’s a check in the Buffett box except for the minor recent blip.

    -   **Owner’s earnings positive every year, OE/Market Cap ≥ 10%:** Alibaba’s owner’s earnings (proxied by free cash flow here) were positive each year (huge positives) , so no issues on generating owners’ cash. However, the ratio of OE to market cap has not been ≥10% historically until recently. For much of the decade, Alibaba’s market cap was so high that FCF yield was low-single-digit. For example, in 2020 FCF \~$23.8B vs market cap \~$600B, yield \~4%. In 2021, FCF $35B vs cap $320B, yield \~11% – actually crossed 10% briefly when stock dropped . In 2022–2023, with stock depressed, FCF yield shot up: e.g. FY2023 FCF $29.2B vs cap \~$200B, \~14.5% . At FY2024, $20.9B on $214B cap \~9.8% – just under 10%. Currently (2025) $22.9B FCF vs \~$290B cap \~7.9%. So not consistently ≥10%. The spirit of this criterion is to ensure you’re getting a 10% owners earnings yield on price paid – which was definitely not the case at Alibaba’s peak valuations, but was the case for investors who bought near the bottom (they could get a double-digit FCF yield). Buffett likely would want to see a high OE yield at purchase – today’s \~8% FCF yield is decent but slightly shy. So **not definitively passed** at the current market cap (but close). If one believes normalized FCF is higher (say including Ant Group stake value), then it could be &gt;10%. But strictly, it’s borderline. Summing up: Alibaba has strong owner earnings generation, but the stock wasn’t cheap enough to offer &gt;=10% yield except during the trough.

4.  **Balance-Sheet Safety:**

    -   **Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:** Alibaba’s **debt/equity is around 0.6** in recent years . During 2015–2017 it was lower (0.46–0.58 range) , but after consolidating more debt (like loans at Cainiao, Sun Art, etc.) it’s now \~0.60. So it slightly fails the &lt;0.5 test at the moment. However, consider that Alibaba’s “debt” here likely includes **all liabilities** (macrotrends uses total liabilities as “long term debt”) . If focusing on interest-bearing debt only, Alibaba’s net debt is negative (it has more cash than borrowings). Alibaba held $65B cash and equivalents vs roughly $25B in long-term borrowings (bonds, loans) as of 2025 – effectively zero net debt. So by that measure, it’s very safe. Buffett’s &lt;0.5 D/E is a conservative gauge – Alibaba is just above it in accounting terms, but financially it’s not leveraged. So I’d lean that **it passes** in substance (very low leverage risk), even if raw D/E slightly exceeds 0.5 lately . The trend also shows equity growing faster than debt until recently (when equity dipped from buybacks).

    -   **Interest Coverage (EBIT / Interest ≥ 3×):** Alibaba easily covers interest expenses. Its EBIT was \~$17B in FY2024 , and interest expense was only a few hundred million (the company actually had net interest *income*some years from cash). So coverage is enormous, well above 3× – more like 20× or higher. For example, in FY2022, EBIT \~$15B vs interest expense \~$600M (estimated from financials), coverage \~25×. So **passes strongly**. No danger of inability to pay interest.

    -   **Interest Expense / Operating Income ≤ 15%:** This is similar to above. Alibaba’s interest expense is a tiny fraction of operating income – on the order of 5% or less . In FY2024, interest expense was around $1B (estimated) vs $17.16B op income , \~6%. In years of net interest income, it’s negative. So definitely **passes**.

    -   **Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:** Alibaba’s current ratio was above 1.5 for most of the past decade except 2019 (dipped to \~1.3) . As of Mar 2024 it was 1.79 and at June 30, 2023 was 1.81 – comfortably above 1.5. Only in FY2019 did it fall to 1.30 due to a large amount of customer advances (liabilities) from consolidating acquired businesses, but it improved thereafter. Alibaba holds massive cash on hand (over $65B) and has relatively smaller current liabilities (largely merchant deposits and payables). So liquidity is not an issue. Thus **passes** (minor blip in one year notwithstanding, overall it’s quite liquid). Buffett would likely see Alibaba’s balance sheet as very strong – lots of cash, minimal debt – so all these safety criteria check out.

5.  **Profitability & Efficiency:**

    -   **Gross Margin ≳ 40%:** Alibaba’s gross margin was well above 40% historically (even \~60-70% in earlier years) . However, it has come down to high-30s in the last 3 years . FY2022 and FY2023 gross margins were \~36-37% , below the \~40% threshold, though FY2025 ticked up to \~40% again . The criterion says “approximately 40% or more” – Alibaba is right around that mark. If we exclude certain low-margin segments, the core commerce gross margin is higher (services like ads have near 100% gross margin, product sales lower). But strictly, it fell short in 2022-23. Buffett typically loves high gross margins indicating a strong moat; Alibaba’s decline reflects changing business mix. At \~38%, it’s close. We might mark this as **borderline pass** – not consistently &gt;=40% recently, but very close and possibly rising again. Certainly earlier years it passed easily. It’s something to watch; not a fail by a huge margin.

    -   **Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:** Alibaba’s net margin was &gt;20% in the mid-2010s (25-30% range) , but it dropped below 20% after 2019. FY2020 was \~29% due to one-time gains, FY2021 \~21% , then FY2022 \~5.7% (hit by fines/investment losses), FY2023 \~8.3%, FY2024 \~8.5% (low due to still-suppressed earnings) – all below 20%. Even looking at non-GAAP net margin (excluding one-offs), it was around 20% in FY2021, but \~16% in FY2023. So Alibaba fails the strict ≥20% net margin lately. This reflects increased competition and costs. It’s uncertain if it will get back to 20%+ sustained. Buffett likes consistently high net margins as sign of durable moat – Alibaba’s were high, but not stable. So **does not pass** currently. However, if one adjusts for abnormal items, the underlying net margin might be a bit higher (perhaps mid-teens). Still below 20%. So this is a notable shortcoming in Buffett terms.

    -   **ROE ≥ 15% average for 10 yrs:** Alibaba’s average ROE over 2015-2021 was well above 15% (ranged 14-34% most years, average maybe \~20%+) . But including 2022-2024, the average drops – ROE was \~5.7% in FY2022 , \~6.7% in FY2023 , \~7.0% in FY2024 . The 10-year average ROE is still probably around 15-18%. However, Buffett also values consistency – and Alibaba’s ROE collapsed post-2021 due to profit decline and equity growth. So it fails the consistency element. But if one averaged out the anomaly, the business historically was high-ROE. Given the criterion says average 15% for 10 years, Alibaba likely *just about*meets that (the first 7 years were mostly &gt;15%, the last 3 years were &lt;&lt;15%). If we weigh equally, maybe slightly under 15%. In conclusion, **borderline/failing** by a small margin. We’d lean that Buffett would not be impressed by the recent low ROE, though he might understand the reasons. The long-run potential ROE remains good if margins normalize.

    -   **ROIC ≥ 12% 10-yr avg:** Alibaba’s return on invested capital is tricky to calculate from outside. But using ROI (as defined by macrotrends for net income relative to debt+equity) – it was very high (15-20+%) pre-2020 , then dropped to \~7-10% in 2021-2023 . Over 10 years, average ROI likely \~12-15%. But recent years dragged it down. So similar to ROE, it’s not consistently above 12%, but the overall average might be near that threshold. Buffett tends to prefer consistently high ROIC businesses; Alibaba’s got diluted by heavy asset investment. I’d say **not a clean pass** due to volatility, though arguably the core commerce still generates high ROIC, weighed down by newer segments. A cautious investor would mark this as a concern.

    -   **ROA ≥ 6%:** Alibaba *mostly* meets this. Pre-2021 ROA was well above 6% (8-15% range) . It dipped to \~4% in 2022-2024 during the profit trough. But now is \~7.1% in FY2025 , back above 6%. The 10-year average ROA is probably around 9-10%. So overall **passes**, aside from the one slump year. Buffett’s 6% ROA bar is relatively low for a tech platform – Alibaba clears that except when external hits depressed earnings temporarily. Given the rebound, we can say it’s likely to continue above 6% going forward (especially as cloud turns profitable etc.). So this criterion is fine.

    -   **Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:** Depreciation+Amortization was around $6-7B in recent years , vs gross profit \~$46-55B , which is \~12-13%. In earlier years, depreciation was lower relative to gross profit (e.g. FY2015 \~$0.7B on $8.45B gross, \~8%; FY2020 \~$4B on $32B gross, \~12%). The threshold 10% was exceeded in FY2018-2022 when Alibaba invested heavily in cloud infrastructure (for instance, FY2018 D&A \~$3.5B vs $22.8B gross, \~15%; FY2021 D&A \~$8B vs $45.2B gross, \~17%). Now that capex has slowed, this ratio improved to \~10.7% in FY2025 (D&A \~$5.85B vs $54.85B gross) – almost at 10%. But historically it was above 10% for a good part of the decade, meaning Alibaba’s business required significant capital investment relative to gross profit – not as light as pure software companies. Buffett generally prefers businesses with low depreciation relative to earnings, as it indicates low capital intensity. Alibaba’s capex wave for cloud made it quite capital intensive for a while (e.g. huge data centers depreciating). So by strict measure, **it fails** the &lt;=10% consistently (only early and maybe now it’s close). However, one could consider a large portion of that depreciation was growth capex rather than maintenance, which Buffett might adjust for – i.e. the “maintenance capex” might be lower. If we consider just maintenance capex, Alibaba’s ratio might be under 10%. But since we don’t have that breakdown, we lean on reported D&A. So not a strong pass here.

**Summary of the Buffett Checklist:**

-   **Valuation:** Alibaba’s stock finally trades at a value-oriented P/E and P/B in recent times, whereas historically it did not. So today it looks reasonably valued or cheap (likely satisfying Buffett’s value test now), with a margin of safety arguably &gt;25% if one believes its intrinsic value is far higher . On valuations, Alibaba **now passes** (low double-digit P/E, \~2x book, high FCF yield), but during most of its public life it wouldn’t have.

-   **Cash Flow & Allocation:** Excellent FCF generation every year (far above the minimum 5% revenue yield), so **passes**. Management has begun returning cash via large buybacks, which is a shareholder-friendly move akin to Buffett’s preferences. Return on retained earnings historically was good (Alibaba reinvested to grow EPS significantly) but the recent hiccup makes the 10-year metric borderline – still, likely a **pass in principle** given the long-term growth from retained earnings was strong (the stumble was external/regulatory, not due to unwise reinvestment by management in failing projects – their investments in cloud, logistics are paying off now, albeit slower).

-   **Growth & Predictability:** Alibaba falls short on the smooth earnings growth criterion – earnings were highly positive but not steadily rising each year. EPS had volatility (especially down in 2022). Book value per share grew at over 25% CAGR, but had a minor dip recently due to buybacks – which in context isn’t a negative, it’s capital return. But Buffett’s checklist is literal: no year of contraction would mark that as a *fail* (if being strict). So **does not fully pass** the consistency tests. Alibaba’s business faced unpredictable swings (regulations, etc.), which would knock it down in Buffett’s eyes compared to a See’s Candies with linear growth. The huge active user base and diverse segments also introduce variability. So on predictability, Alibaba is weaker – its future earnings are not as predictable as a consumer staples firm given regulatory and competitive uncertainties.

-   **Balance Sheet Safety:** Alibaba is financially very strong. Virtually no net debt, lots of cash, healthy current ratio, easily covers interest. It **passes all balance sheet safety** marks (maybe except a slightly high D/E by formula, but effectively it’s unlevered). Buffett would be comfortable with Alibaba’s solid balance sheet and cash-rich position.

-   **Profitability & Efficiency:** Mixed results. Gross margin around 40% is good (though down from past), net margin currently in single digits – well below Buffett’s ideal. ROE and ROIC have been good historically but slumped in recent years; they might recover to mid-teens, but currently under 10%. That, plus an elevated depreciation relative to gross profit for a period, signals Alibaba’s profitability isn’t as high or consistent as Buffett’s favorite companies. Essentially, Alibaba sacrificed some profitability for growth and now is re-focusing on efficiency (e.g. adjusted EBITA margin improved last quarter). But given the last few years, it **does not strictly pass** the &gt;20% net margin or steady &gt;15% ROE tests.

In conclusion, on Buffett’s qualitative measures, Alibaba shows **great strengths in cash generation and financial strength**, and its franchise (leading e-commerce ecosystem in China) gives it some durable competitive advantages. However, it *falls short on stability and consistently high profitability metrics*. The regulatory environment and competition injected unpredictability into results, which Buffett typically avoids. Additionally, Buffett tends to avoid companies he doesn’t fully understand or that operate in fast-changing tech landscapes – and Alibaba, being a Chinese tech giant subject to government whims, likely violates the “circle of competence” and “stable moat” requirement in practice (indeed, Charlie Munger invested via Daily Journal, but Buffett himself never bought BABA for Berkshire, possibly due to these concerns).

Summing up checklist outcome: Alibaba **meets some of Buffett’s standards** (strong free cash flow, decent ROE long-term, low debt, now a reasonable price) but **fails others** (earnings consistency, recent profitability metrics, full predictability). At today’s price, it arguably offers a margin of safety and solid returns on capital, which Buffett would find attractive – but the unpredictable regulatory climate and episodic profit declines mean it doesn’t perfectly fit the mold of a “wonderful company” with unwavering metrics. If one were grading Alibaba on Buffett’s checklist, it would get a mixed score: **some boxes ticked (value, FCF, balance sheet) and some not (stable growth, consistently high margins/ROE)**.

## **DCF Analysis**

To estimate Alibaba’s intrinsic value, we perform a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis under three scenarios – **Conservative**, **Nominal (Base)**, and **Optimistic** – based on Alibaba’s free cash flow (FCF) growth history. We use the past 10 years’ FCF growth rates to inform future projections:

-   In the **best year**, Alibaba’s FCF grew tremendously (e.g. +73.6% from FY2022 to FY2023 in RMB terms ). The highest sustained growth period was in the mid-2010s (FCF CAGR \~50% from 2015–2019). However, we will take a more normalized “best case” growth reflecting the business reaccelerating but not back to hyper-growth.

-   In the **worst year**, FCF actually fell (e.g. –48.6% from FY2021 to FY2022 in RMB due to the regulatory impacts). Over 10 years, Alibaba’s lowest FCF growth was –43% (FY2022) . We use that as a low baseline.

-   The **average** FCF growth (geometric mean) over the last 10 years is high (\~30%+), but that was from a smaller base. For a mature Alibaba, future growth will be lower. We’ll use a more moderate figure for the base scenario.

**Assumptions common to all scenarios:** We take Alibaba’s trailing twelve-month free cash flow as our starting point – approximately **$22.9 billion** in FY2025 . We assume a **discount rate** of 10% (reflecting a reasonable required return for equity, given some risk factors). We’ll project FCF over 5 years using the growth rates specified for each case, then assume a terminal growth rate thereafter of 3% (about the long-run global GDP growth, also acknowledging Alibaba’s large size making high growth hard to sustain indefinitely). We discount all future cash flows to present value and divide by shares outstanding (\~2.45 billion ADRs) to get an intrinsic value per share. Finally, we apply a 25% margin of safety to suggest a *buy price*.

**Conservative Case:** This scenario assumes Alibaba grows FCF at the *lowest* rate observed historically (or even lower, to be cautious). We use **0% FCF growth for the next 5 years** – essentially assuming Alibaba’s FCF stagnates at \~$22.9B annually (this is very conservative given the company is still growing revenues \~5% and focusing on cost cuts which should lift FCF). This scenario might correspond to a situation where competition and regulation keep squeezing margins, and Alibaba only treads water in cash generation. After year 5, we still apply a 3% terminal growth (to reflect inflationary growth at least).

-   Year 1–5 FCF: $22.9B each year (no growth).

-   Terminal Year FCF (Year 6 onward): grows at 3%.

We discount each of the 5 years and the terminal value at 10%. The PV of 5 years of flat $22.9B is simply $22.9B \* (Present value factor of an annuity). Calculating: PV factor for 5 years at 10% ≈ 3.79. So PV of first 5 years ≈ $22.9B \* 3.79 = $86.8B. The terminal value at end of year 5 = FCF\_6 / (discount – terminal) = $22.9B \* 1.03 / (0.10 – 0.03) = $23.587B / 0.07 = $337.0B. Discounting that back 5 years: PV\_terminal = $337.0B / (1.10^5) ≈ $337.0B / 1.6105 = $209.3B. Sum PVs = $86.8B + $209.3B = **$296.1B** intrinsic market cap. Divide by 2.45B shares = **$121 per share intrinsic value**. Applying a 25% margin of safety, a prudent *buy price* would be $121 \* 0.75 ≈ **$91 per share**.

This conservative case basically says if Alibaba never grows FCF beyond current levels, it’s still worth around $120/share. Notably, $121 is near the current market price (\~$120), suggesting the market may be pricing in very low growth (which aligns with this conservative case). Thus, downside seems limited assuming Alibaba can at least maintain its cash flows .

**Nominal (Base) Case:** For the base scenario, we use a growth rate closer to Alibaba’s average but tempered for maturity. Let’s assume **8% FCF growth** annually for the next 5 years. This 8% reflects that Alibaba could grow FCF roughly in line with nominal GDP plus some margin expansion, but not spectacularly – a reasonable middle ground given its cloud and international segments could drive some growth. (For context, Alibaba’s revenue is expected to grow mid-single-digits near-term ; FCF could grow slightly faster if margins improve post-restructuring and capex efficiencies).

-   Year 1 FCF: $24.7B (8% above $22.9B).

-   Year 2: $26.7B

-   Year 3: $28.8B

-   Year 4: $31.0B

-   Year 5: $33.5B (each year 8% higher).

-   Terminal FCF in Year 6: $33.5B \* 1.03 = $34.5B, growing at 3% thereafter.

Now discounting:

PV of years 1-5 = $24.7/1.1^1 + $26.7/1.1^2 + … + $33.5/1.1^5. We can compute that sequentially or use formula. For brevity, approximate:

1: $22.45B PV,

2: $22.08B,

3: $21.64B,

4: $21.13B,

5: $20.55B. Sum ≈ $107.9B.

Terminal value at end of year 5 = $34.5B / (0.10 – 0.03) = $34.5B / 0.07 = $492.9B. PV\_terminal = $492.9B / 1.1^5 ≈ $492.9B / 1.6105 = $306.0B. Total PV = \~$107.9 + $306.0 = **$413.9B**. Per share = $413.9B / 2.45B = **$169 per share intrinsic value**. With a 25% MOS, buy target would be \~$127.

So in the base case, **intrinsic value ≈ $170**, implying the stock (at \~$120) is somewhat undervalued – about 30-40% upside to fair value. The base case assumes Alibaba can achieve high-single-digit growth, which is plausible if it resumes moderate growth in e-commerce and especially if cloud (currently \~8% of revenue and returning to double-digit growth) picks up momentum . This scenario sees Alibaba as moderately undervalued today.

**Optimistic Case:** For a bullish scenario, we consider something closer to Alibaba’s historical best growth – perhaps not the extraordinary 50%+ of early years, but say **15% FCF growth** for 5 years. This could happen if: China’s economy rebounds strongly, Alibaba’s cloud unit scales profitability, the regulatory environment stays benign, and Alibaba’s efficiency moves significantly boost cash flows (and maybe spinning off units unlocks value and growth potential). 15% annual FCF growth would take FCF from $22.9B to about $46.0B in year 5 – ambitious but not impossible (that’s roughly doubling in 5 years).

-   Year 1: $26.3B

-   Year 2: $30.3B

-   Year 3: $34.8B

-   Year 4: $40.0B

-   Year 5: $46.0B

-   Terminal Year 6 FCF: $47.4B (3% growth).

Now:

PV of first 5 years = $26.3/1.1 + $30.3/1.1^2 + $34.8/1.1^3 + $40.0/1.1^4 + $46.0/1.1^5.

Numerically:

Year1 PV = $23.9B

Year2 PV = $25.0B

Year3 PV = $26.1B

Year4 PV = $27.3B

Year5 PV = $28.6B

Sum = $130.9B (approx).

Terminal value at end of year 5 = $47.4B / 0.07 = $677.1B. PV\_terminal = $677.1B / 1.6105 = $420.3B. Total PV = \~$130.9 + $420.3 = **$551.2B**. Per share = $551.2B / 2.45B = **$225 per share intrinsic value**. Margin of safety 25% =&gt; *buy at* \~$169 or below.

Under this optimistic case, Alibaba’s intrinsic value would be around $225, nearly double the current price. This assumes robust growth and execution. It’s akin to saying Alibaba will re-accelerate and perhaps regain investor confidence, possibly driven by unlocking value via IPOs of its units (e.g. if Ant Group eventually IPOs or the cloud unit is valued independently, etc.). While this case is not guaranteed, it shows potential **upside** if Alibaba fires on all cylinders.

**Summary of DCF results:**

-   **Conservative (no growth)**: Intrinsic value ≈ **$120/share**, buy below ≈ **$90**. This essentially matches current price, suggesting limited downside if Alibaba’s FCF just stagnates.

-   **Base (8% growth)**: Intrinsic value ≈ **$170/share**, buy below ≈ **$127**. Implies \~40% upside from current levels.

-   **Optimistic (15% growth)**: Intrinsic value ≈ **$225/share**, buy below ≈ **$169**. Suggests nearly 2× upside if Alibaba re-enters a solid growth trajectory.

These DCF outcomes illustrate that Alibaba’s stock appears **undervalued in both the base and optimistic scenarios**, and even in a very pessimistic scenario it’s roughly fairly valued. The **margin of safety** at current \~$120 price is decent – the market seems to be pricing in a near no-growth scenario. For long-term value investors, this provides a cushion: if Alibaba merely maintains current cash flows (and 0% growth is quite cautious), you’re paying about intrinsic value; if it grows even modestly, there’s substantial upside.

It’s important to highlight **calculation basis** and potential risks:

-   We assumed a 10% discount rate which is standard for equity; if one used a higher rate due to country/industry risk (say 12%), intrinsic values would be a bit lower (\~15-20% lower).

-   We assumed a continuing 3% terminal growth. If Alibaba’s long-run growth is less (or if government interference caps profit growth), terminal value might be lower.

-   The optimistic scenario may be too bullish if competition or new regulations hit again.

-   Conversely, we haven’t included potential hidden assets like Alibaba’s stake in Ant Group or other equity investments explicitly; those could add value (Ant’s implied valuation could be tens of billions). One could argue our DCF is based solely on operating FCF, so any unlocking of Ant or other pieces would be upside beyond the model.

Step-by-step, we explained the FCF growth assumptions and present values. This gives confidence in the results: even if Alibaba’s growth is mediocre, the stock isn’t overpriced – in fact, in the base and optimistic cases, **intrinsic value is significantly above the current market price**, indicating a margin of safety.

In summary, **DCF analysis suggests** Alibaba’s current stock price (\~$120) is at or below intrinsic value under reasonable assumptions, and well below intrinsic value under more favorable assumptions. The conservative case yields an intrinsic value just about equal to the market price – implying the market might be pessimistically assuming little growth. The base and optimistic scenarios show **intrinsic values 40% to 90% higher** than the current price, highlighting why many value investors (like Charlie Munger) found Alibaba attractive around these levels. With a prudent margin of safety (25%), one might consider accumulating Alibaba up to roughly $125 (base MOS) or even $169 (opt. MOS), though ideally closer to $90 (cons. MOS) for maximum safety – but the stock never got that low except briefly in panic sell-offs.

Thus, the DCF analysis (in USD, as presented) supports the thesis that **Alibaba offers a favorable risk-reward**: limited downside if it stagnates, and substantial upside if it can resume even moderate growth. An investor demanding a 25% MOS might aim to buy under \~$100–120 (which the stock was for much of 2022–early 2023). As of mid-2025, the stock is slightly above some MOS thresholds, but still well below base and optimistic valuations.
