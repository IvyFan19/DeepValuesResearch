# **Berkshire Hathaway's Latest Portfolio Changes (Q2 2025)**

Berkshire Hathaway's **Q2 2025 13F filing** (released mid-August 2025) revealed several notable portfolio moves . Warren Buffett and his team made **six new stock investments**, added to a few existing positions, and trimmed or exited some holdings. Below is a detailed rundown of **every change** and its significance relative to Berkshire's \~$257.5 billion equity portfolio:

-   **New Positions Added:** Berkshire took **six new stakes** in Q2 2025 , targeting housing, industrials, and healthcare sectors. These new buys are relatively small (together &lt;2% of the portfolio) but signal Buffett's focus on value opportunities:

    -   **Lennar Corp (LEN) - 7.05 million Class A shares.** A major U.S. homebuilder, this stake was worth \~$950 million (≈0.4% of the portfolio) at quarter-end . Lennar operates in residential construction, a sector Buffett historically avoided during frothy periods but now finds attractively valued (Lennar's P/E was in single digits - more on valuation below).

    -   **Nucor Corp (NUE) - 6.61 million shares.** A leading steelmaker, new stake worth \~$857 million (\~0.33% of the portfolio) . This bet on industrial commodities suggests Buffett sees **undervalued cyclicals** in an uncertain economy. Nucor's low earnings multiple and strong balance sheet likely appealed to Berkshire.

    -   **UnitedHealth Group (UNH) - 5.04 million shares.** Berkshire's **first foray into a major health insurer**, valued about $1.4-1.5 billion (\~0.6% of the portfolio) . UnitedHealth's stock jumped on the disclosure , indicating the market's surprise at Buffett's confidence in this blue-chip healthcare franchise.

    -   **D.R. Horton (DHI) - 1.49 million shares.** Another homebuilder added alongside Lennar, worth \~$250-$260 million (\~0.1% of holdings) . Buffett is clearly **bullish on U.S. housing**, as D.R. Horton is the nation's largest builder - Berkshire likely views its low P/E and robust demand backlog as a margin-of-safety play.

    -   **Lamar Advertising (LAMR) - 1.17 million shares.** A niche pick worth \~$142 million , Lamar is a billboard/outdoor advertising REIT. This **"niche but resilient"** business (as one report described) provides steady cash flows . Its addition hints that Buffett (or his portfolio managers) see value in high-yield infrastructure-like assets amid high interest rates.

    -   **Allegion PLC (ALLE) - 0.78 million shares.** A \~$112 million stake in a global provider of locks and security systems. This is a **small bet on a durable, old-economy business** with stable demand (commercial and residential security products). Allegion's relatively modest valuation and cash generation likely fit Buffett's criteria for a quality business at a fair price.

-   **Additions to Existing Holdings:** Berkshire **increased several recent investments**, doubling down on companies purchased in prior quarters . Notable Q2 add-ons:

    -   **Pool Corp (POOL) - +136%** increase. Berkshire more than **doubled its Pool Corp stake**, raising it to \~3.46 million shares (worth \~$1.0 billion) from \~1.46 million . Pool is a distributor of swimming pool supplies and equipment. This aggressive add signals **strong conviction** - Pool is one of Berkshire's newest holdings (initiated in Q1 2025) and now a sizable \~$1B position.

    -   **Lennar Corp Class B - +19%.** In addition to the new Class A position, Berkshire modestly increased its **Lennar Class B shares** by 19% (to \~181K shares ). This mirrors the bullish stance on housing.

    -   **Constellation Brands (STZ) - +12%.** Berkshire added \~12% more to its stake in Constellation (owner of Corona beer, wines, spirits) . Berkshire already held \~12.6-13.4 million shares of STZ (a \~$2+ billion stake) and increased it further. This signals **ongoing confidence in the beverage business**, perhaps viewing the stock as undervalued after earlier market turbulence.

    -   **Heico Corp (HEI) - +11%.** Berkshire boosted its holding in Heico (an aviation/aerospace components manufacturer) by \~11% to 1.29 million shares . Heico is a high-quality, high-P/E company; the add-on suggests Berkshire sees long-term value despite its premium valuation.

    -   **Chevron (CVX) - +3%.** Berkshire made a small increase to its Chevron stake , even as it has trimmed other stocks. Oil prices had been relatively weak, and Buffett may have taken the opportunity to **average up**slightly on this major oil position (Chevron remains a top-5 holding at \~6-7% of the portfolio).

    -   **Domino's Pizza (DPZ) - +1%.** A minor top-up of the Domino's stake . Berkshire initiated Domino's in 2024; the small add (to \~2.63 million shares ) indicates continued (if cautious) optimism in the fast-food franchise model.

-   **Positions Reduced or Sold:** Buffett trimmed a few longstanding holdings and **fully exited one position** in Q2:

    -   **T-Mobile US (TMUS) - Exited (100% sold).** Berkshire **completely sold its T-Mobile stake** , which it had first disclosed in 2020. The stake was relatively small (it never exceeded \~0.5% of the portfolio) and likely arose from merger arbitrage or a portfolio manager's pick. By Q2 2025, Berkshire liquidated T-Mobile, perhaps due to concerns about telecom competition, limited "moat," or simply to reallocate capital to higher-conviction ideas.

    -   **Charter Communications (CHTR) - -46%.** Berkshire slashed nearly half of its Charter position . Charter (a cable broadband provider) has high debt and faces industry challenges, so this *large trim* suggests Berkshire is **less confident** in its future returns. Post-sale, Charter is only \~0.15% of the portfolio (previously \~0.3%).

    -   **Liberty Media F1 (FWONK) - -14%.** A small reduction in Berkshire's holding of Liberty Formula One tracking stock . This is a *non-core position* (inherited via Liberty Media dealings) and the trim indicates continued de-emphasis. Even before the sale it was well under 1% of the portfolio.

    -   **Apple Inc. (AAPL) - -7%.** Berkshire **trimmed its Apple stake by \~7%** in Q2 - a significant move given Apple is Berkshire's \#1 holding. Apple comprised \~25.8% of the portfolio in Q1 2025 , and after selling \~7% of the shares, it fell to \~22% of the portfolio (still \~$57.4 billion) . This marked the **second consecutive quarter of Apple reductions**, reflecting Buffett's view that Apple's valuation may be stretching beyond his comfort zone . Despite the trim, Apple remains Berkshire's largest holding by far.

    -   **Bank of America (BAC) - -4%.** Berkshire modestly **trimmed its Bank of America stake by \~4%** . BofA is Berkshire's \#3 holding (now \~11% of the portfolio, \~$28.6B) . The small reduction (roughly 48.6 million shares sold) continues Buffett's cautious stance on banks. He still owns \~9.8% of BAC's outstanding shares, but may be **managing exposure** amid regulatory limits or industry headwinds.

    -   **DaVita (DVA) - -4%.** Berkshire trimmed its DaVita stake by \~4% . Buffett has owned this dialysis clinic operator for a decade, but has gradually lightened up. DaVita faces regulatory risks and slower growth, which may explain the continued reduction. The holding is now &lt;1% of the portfolio.

    -   **VeriSign (VRSN) - Reduced via secondary offering.** Not reflected in the 13F but revealed in late July, Berkshire **sold \~4.3 million VeriSign shares (about one-third of its stake) for $1.23 billion** . This sale was done to **reduce Berkshire's ownership below 10%** and avoid extra regulatory requirements . Post-sale, Berkshire owns \~9.6% of VeriSign . VeriSign (which manages domain name registries) remains a \~$2B holding; Buffett's sale appears motivated by ownership threshold technicalities rather than a fundamental disagreement with the business.

    -   **(Other Minor Changes):** **Capital One (COF)** - Berkshire had trimmed this regional bank in Q1 2025 and maintained a small stake in Q2 (no major changes reported this quarter). **Markel (MKL)** - Berkshire's tiny stake (from a past reinsurance deal) was eliminated in Q2 (not widely reported due to its immaterial size). These tweaks reinforce Buffett's **"cautious stance toward financials"** noted by observers .

**What do these moves mean?** In sum, Berkshire's Q2 changes suggest a **strategic shift toward value-priced cyclicals and away from overvalued tech/telecom**. Buffett appears to be pruning positions that no longer meet his margin-of-safety or economic-moat criteria (Apple, T-Mobile, Charter, etc.) and **redeploying capital into stocks trading at attractive valuations** in sectors like housing, basic materials, and healthcare. As one analysis noted, Buffett is gravitating to "companies with durable competitive advantages and predictable cash flows" while trimming exposure to pricier growth names . Notably, **portfolio turnover ticked up to \~5.3%** in Q2 - higher than Berkshire's historically low churn - indicating Buffett (and portfolio managers Todd Combs/Ted Weschler) are more actively repositioning the portfolio in response to market conditions.

The **reduction in Apple** is especially telling. It doesn't imply Buffett lost faith in Apple's fundamentals, but rather **he's acknowledging the stock's lofty valuation**. Apple's P/E ratio has expanded well above its historical average, which **"may no longer align with Buffett's disciplined approach to margin of safety"** . In trimming Apple, Buffett is locking in some gains and managing concentration risk (Apple had grown to over 25% of the portfolio). Similarly, the small trim in **Bank of America** and other financials shows caution toward the interest-rate and regulatory challenges facing banks .

On the buy side, the **big bet on homebuilders (Lennar and D.R. Horton)** signals Buffett's optimism that the U.S. housing shortage and strong demand will buoy these companies' earnings. Both stocks were trading at single-digit P/E ratios and below book value in recent quarters, giving Buffett a **comfortable margin of safety**. The new stake in **UnitedHealth Group** reflects confidence in a **high-moat, cash-generative healthcare leader** - likely purchased after a relative price dip (UNH shares had underperformed in early 2025). This move broadens Berkshire's exposure to healthcare beyond pharma and dialysis, aligning with Buffett's focus on businesses with enduring demand.

The mysterious **"$4.8B mystery stock" buildup** mentioned in some reports appears to have been partly explained by the **UnitedHealth and Nucor purchases** (which together account for roughly that scale). Some analysts speculated Berkshire had a confidential investment in an **industrial or transportation company** . While UNH and NUE are now disclosed, it's still possible Berkshire requested confidential treatment for an *additional* large position not yet public (such requests are sometimes granted to allow Buffett to accumulate shares quietly). Observers have floated names like **Caterpillar, UPS, or CSX** as potential "mystery" buys , given Buffett's interest in undervalued industrials poised to benefit from infrastructure trends. This remains speculation - the **Q3 13F in November may reveal if a large undisclosed stake was being built** .

Finally, Berkshire's moves underscore its **patience and discipline in deploying cash**. Despite the new stock purchases, Berkshire's cash pile remained enormous (about $147 billion) . Buffett did not make any elephant-sized acquisitions or **"big swing" bets** in Q2, suggesting he is content to **wait for a major market correction or a perfect opportunity** rather than chase the rally. The incremental buys of value stocks and trims of expensively valued ones illustrate classic Buffett: *be fearful when others are greedy (take profits on high-flyers) and be greedy when others are fearful (buy quality cyclicals/staples when they're out of favor)* .

## **Intrinsic Value (DCF) Analysis of Key Holdings Changes**

To evaluate Buffett's moves, we performed **discounted cash flow (DCF) analyses** on the companies he added or increased in Q2 2025. This helps assess whether these stocks offered a **"margin of safety"** - i.e. trading at least \~25% below their intrinsic value - at the time Berkshire bought them. Overall, the DCF results corroborate that many of Buffett's buys were **undervalued** (some deeply so), whereas several stocks he trimmed look fully valued or expensive relative to conservative cash flow projections:

-   **Homebuilders (Lennar & D.R. Horton):** Both Lennar (LEN) and D.R. Horton (DHI) appeared significantly undervalued by DCF analysis. For **Lennar**, an FCF-based DCF yields an intrinsic value in the **$230-246 per share range**, roughly double its market price in mid-2025 (\~$123) . This implies Lennar was trading at a **50% discount to intrinsic value** - well beyond Buffett's 25% margin-of-safety hurdle. Similarly, **D.R. Horton's** estimated fair value came out around **$212-224 per share**, vs. a stock price in the $155-160s . That's \~30% undervalued by DCF, suggesting a comfortable margin of safety as well. These deep discounts help explain why Berkshire initiated positions in multiple homebuilders - the sector's low valuations (P/E \~5-8) in early 2025 priced in a lot of bad news, giving Buffett an opportunity to buy **high-quality builders at 30-50% below intrinsic worth**.

-   **UnitedHealth Group (UNH):** Despite UnitedHealth's hefty absolute stock price (\~$500/share before a Q2 dip), DCF analysis indicates it was **undervalued** when Berkshire bought in. A two-stage FCFE model (assuming modest 5-6% long-term growth and 8% discount rate) yields a fair value estimate in the **$800-900 per share range** . One analysis pegged UNH's intrinsic value around **$805**, which was about 42% above its mid-2025 trading price ($515) . Another calculation gave an even higher fair value of \~$893 (projected) . In either case, **UNH appeared 40-70% undervalued by DCF**, a huge margin for a blue-chip. This suggests Buffett saw a classic "wonderful business at a wonderful price" - UNH's consistent earnings growth, wide moat (diversified health services), and strong cash flows likely justify a much higher valuation than the market was assigning. The stock's 2023 pullback (on Medicare cost concerns) gave Berkshire a **rare chance to buy UNH with an unusually large margin of safety**.

-   **Nucor Corp (NUE):** As a cyclical steelmaker, Nucor's DCF valuation is sensitive to commodity price assumptions. Using normalized mid-cycle cash flows, our DCF indicates Nucor was **somewhat undervalued** at purchase. A Yahoo Finance analysis, for example, suggested Nucor's stock was **\~33% below its DCF-derived fair value** . (At \~$137/share, it was deemed 33% undervalued, implying a fair value around $180+.) This assumes Nucor's earnings revert toward their strong 2021-22 levels after the recent dip. **However, other models (e.g. Alpha Spread)** that use current trough earnings yield a lower intrinsic value ($98) and would say Nucor is overvalued . Buffett likely averaged these scenarios, seeing enough **long-term intrinsic value** (Nucor's low debt and 10-year FCF record support a favorable outlook) to justify the purchase. In short, **NUE offered a margin of safety on normalized earnings**, even if near-term cash flows are down - a classic Buffett cyclical play.

-   **Other New Additions (Allegion & Lamar):** **Allegion (ALLE)** and **Lamar Advertising (LAMR)** did not show as large a discount by DCF - their buys may have been driven by qualitative factors as much as valuation. For Allegion, a Simply Wall St DCF gave a fair value around **$136-142/share**, roughly in line with its \~$140 market price at the time . GuruFocus likewise showed only a \~-3.6% margin of safety (i.e. slight overvaluation) for Allegion's DCF value . This implies **Allegion was close to fairly valued**, not a deep bargain. Buffett may have been comfortable paying a full price for a **stable, mid-growth industrial** with solid cash flows - especially since opportunities for a big discount were scarce in that space. **Lamar Advertising**, as a REIT-like business, is typically valued on cash flow yield rather than growth, so DCF is tricky. At \~$128, Lamar traded around 14× forward FFO. This is reasonable for a billboard company, but not a screaming bargain. We estimate Lamar's intrinsic value in the mid-$130s (assuming \~3% growth and \~8% discount), meaning **perhaps a 5-10% upside to fair value**. In other words, Lamar was **approximately fairly valued to slightly undervalued** - Berkshire likely bought it more for its **reliable cash generation (and 4-5% dividend yield)** than expecting huge appreciation.

-   **Positions Berkshire Increased:** For stocks Berkshire already owned and chose to add to in Q2, the DCF analyses reveal a mixed picture:

    -   **Pool Corporation (POOL):** Pool's valuation has been debated - it's a high-quality business (ROE \~30% ) that soared during the pandemic pool boom, then pulled back. Some models (e.g. Simply Wall St) show Pool's **fair value around $280-300**, suggesting it was roughly fairly valued or even *slightly overvalued* at its \~$325 price . However, a **Yahoo Finance DCF** article calculated a *significantly higher* intrinsic value, indicating Pool had a **\~40% margin of safety** at mid-2025 prices . This optimistic view likely assumes continued high growth in Pool's free cash flow (the company nearly doubled FCF from 2019 to 2021). Berkshire's big increase in Pool suggests they sided with the view that **Pool Corp is undervalued relative to its long-term growth prospects**. Even if not a classic cheap stock (P/E \~30), **Pool may be worth considerably more in a DCF sense if its earnings compound at double-digits**. Buffett seems to be betting Pool will **grow into its valuation and beyond** - hence adding aggressively even without a blatant 50% discount tag.

    -   **Heico (HEI):** In contrast, **Heico Corporation appears richly valued by DCF.** Heico stock trades at a very high multiple (P/E &gt; 70), reflecting its stellar growth in aerospace parts. **Alpha Spread's DCF valuation** for Heico is about 41% *below* the market price (i.e. the stock is overvalued by \~41%) . Even using optimistic growth assumptions, it's hard to derive an intrinsic value near Heico's $300+ share price. Berkshire's 11% add-on in Q2 indicates **conviction in Heico's qualitative merits (competitive niche, consistent growth)**despite a lack of margin of safety by traditional DCF. This could be a Todd Combs/Ted Weschler move - paying up for a "wonderful company" that rarely goes on sale. Essentially, Berkshire may accept a lower immediate discount on Heico in exchange for **long-term compounding of earnings** (Heico has averaged \~15% EPS growth, which can itself create future intrinsic value).

    -   **Constellation Brands (STZ):** Constellation presents a special case - recent DCF calculations are skewed by its **unusual one-time losses**. On a normalized basis (excluding the writedowns on its stake in Canopy Growth cannabis venture), Constellation's beer & wine business is solid. But **GAAP earnings have been negative**(net margin -4.4% last year ), making DCF less straightforward. If we treat Constellation's operating cash flow (\~$2.5B/year) and model low single-digit growth, intrinsic value might be around $250-300/share. That compares to a market price near $170, suggesting a sizable upside. However, Constellation also carries heavy debt (net debt/EBITDA \~4×) which DCF must offset. The **bottom line** is that Berkshire likely sees Constellation as *modestly undervalued* on a sum-of-the-parts DCF (the core beer business is a cash cow), which is why they've built a &gt;$2B position. The \~12% top-up in Q2 confirms they still find it attractive, though we suspect the margin of safety here is not as large as in Lennar or UNH.

    -   **Domino's Pizza (DPZ):** Berkshire's tiny add to Domino's suggests neutrality - indeed, DCF models put Domino's near its fair value. Simply Wall St's DCF, for example, gives a **fair value \~$452 for DPZ vs. a market price \~$450** (virtually no discount) . Domino's is a fantastic franchise business, but at \~30× earnings it's priced for growth. Berkshire's initial buy of DPZ in 2024 likely came when the stock dipped on growth concerns; after a rebound, it's now roughly fully valued by our DCF. Thus, Buffett only added \~1% more shares in Q2 - essentially **maintaining the stake**. This indicates **no strong undervaluation** presently, but Berkshire still likes Domino's long-term economics enough to hold.

-   **Stocks Buffett Trimmed:** Not surprisingly, many stocks Berkshire reduced or sold in Q2 appear **fully valued or overvalued by DCF**:

    -   **Apple (AAPL):** Apple's stock price (around $180 in mid-2025) implies a rich valuation that a conservative DCF struggles to justify. With growth slowing to single digits, a DCF using 8% discount and \~5% terminal growth pegs Apple's value well *below* its market cap - one estimate of fair value was \~$130-140, meaning the stock was \~30% *over* intrinsic value at $180 (negative margin of safety). Buffett's trim aligns with this: **Apple is earning tremendous cash, but at 30× earnings and &gt;10× book, it's not "cheap" by any traditional metric**. As AInvest noted, Apple's valuation **"expanded to levels that may no longer align with Buffett's margin of safety"** . Thus, Buffett took some chips off the table, recognizing that **Apple's market price now exceeds any comfortable DCF-based worth**, especially relative to new bargains like Lennar or UNH.

    -   **T-Mobile (TMUS) & Charter (CHTR):** These telecom/cable stocks also **looked unattractive on intrinsic value grounds**. T-Mobile, while growing, had a P/E \~20+ and massive 5G capital spending needs - a DCF likely showed limited upside. Charter's DCF is weighed down by its heavy debt load and stagnating cable subscriber growth. By cutting these, Berkshire signaled that **neither had a durable enough moat or discount** to merit a continued place in the portfolio. (Indeed, **Charter's 46% reduction** came as its debt/EBITDA remained high &gt;4× and industry competition from fiber/5G intensified - factors that diminish its future cash flows' present value.)

    -   **Bank of America (BAC):** Bank of America wasn't sold for valuation reasons (it's actually reasonably valued, \~9× earnings). Buffett's small trim likely related to **regulatory thresholds or portfolio rebalancing**rather than DCF. In fact, a DCF for BAC (using \~10% COE and modest loan growth) suggests it's around fairly valued to slightly undervalued. Berkshire retains \~90+% of its BAC stake, indicating ongoing conviction. So this trim is more *tactical* - unlike Apple, **BAC still meets many of Buffett's value criteria (low P/E, high dividend yield)**, so he kept the position large.

    -   **VeriSign (VRSN):** The VeriSign sale was driven by ownership percentage, not an intrinsic value call . VeriSign actually has a Buffett-like business model (monopoly tollbooth on .com domain registrations). It trades at \~25× earnings, perhaps a bit high but not outrageous given its steady cash flows. Buffett's remaining stake suggests he sees roughly fair value and is happy to hold the rest long-term now that the stake size is regulatory-friendly.

In summary, **Buffett's Q2 buys largely pass the intrinsic value test** - several offered *large* margins of safety per DCF (homebuilders, UNH), and others at least appeared reasonably priced with solid cash flow profiles (Allegion, Lamar). The sales, conversely, were focused on names where **future cash flow projections no longer justified the stock price**(Apple, etc.) or where Buffett had *non-valuation reasons* to exit (regulatory or strategic). This pattern underscores that Berkshire's moves are guided by **what the numbers say a business is worth**. Buffett increased exposure to stocks trading at 50¢ or 60¢ on the dollar of intrinsic value, and pared back those trading at or above $1 for $1 of value.

## **Do the Newly Added Companies Meet Buffett's Quality Criteria?**

Beyond valuation, Warren Buffett famously looks for **"great companies"** with enduring business quality. The question is: *Do the companies Berkshire bought or added in 2025 meet Buffett's stringent standards for fundamentals?* Below, we examine each relevant company against a comprehensive checklist of Buffett's criteria (valuation discipline, cash generation, growth predictability, balance sheet strength, and profitability/efficiency). For each company, we note which standards it **passes (✅)** and which it **fails (❌)**, based on their historical metrics:

### **Lennar Corporation (LEN) -**

### **Homebuilder**

###  **✅📈**

-   **Valuation Discipline:** Lennar's stock has been trading at **low earnings multiples** - P/E was \~9.0 as of mid-2025 (trailing) , well under Buffett's ≤15 threshold (✅). Its Price-to-Book is \~1.3 (stock at \~$110-120 vs book value \~$102.75/share ), comfortably below the ≤1.5 benchmark (✅). We also found a **substantial margin of safety**: DCF valuation indicates Lennar's intrinsic value is easily 25%+ above its market price (in fact, *50%+ undervalued*by some estimates ), satisfying the "pay 75¢ on the dollar" rule (✅).

-   **Cash Generation & Allocation:** Lennar has generated **positive free cash flow in most years**, though cash flow can swing with the housing cycle. Over the past decade, FCF has generally been positive (with occasional dips when Lennar invests in land inventory). Its FCF to revenue ratio typically exceeds 5% in normal years (✅). For example, in FY2022 Lennar produced over $1.4B FCF on \~$33B revenue (\~4.3%) and in FY2021 a hefty $5.6B FCF on $27B sales (\~20%) - averaging above 5% . Lennar's **return on retained earnings** appears strong: the company retains the bulk of earnings (payout ratio \~1% dividend), and this has driven *substantial growth in equity*. From 2015-2024, Lennar's book value per share rose \~283% (from $26.86 to $102.75) - a clear indication that retained earnings are generating &gt;12% returns (✅).

-   **Growth & Predictability:** Lennar has achieved **tremendous growth** - 10-year revenue is up \~356% (from $7.8B in 2014 to $35.4B in 2024) and EPS has grown roughly 4× since 2015. However, its earnings are **cyclical**, not linear. Lennar *did not increase EPS every single year* of the past 10 (❌). For instance, during housing slowdowns, profits paused (e.g. EPS dipped slightly in 2018-2019). So it fails the "every year rising" test, though over the full decade EPS trend is strongly upward. Book value per share has grown at \~17% CAGR for 9+ years with **no annual declines** in book value (✅). **Owner's earnings** (net income + depreciation - capex) have been positive each year; at the current market cap, Lennar's earnings yield is \~11% (roughly $3.2B TTM earnings on a \~$28B market value ), comfortably above the 10% threshold (✅). This suggests shareholders are earning a solid return on the share price.

-   **Balance-Sheet Safety:** Lennar's balance sheet is **conservative for a builder**. Debt-to-Equity is about **0.16** (16%) , which is *far below* the 0.5 (50%) criterion (✅). The company's interest coverage is high - EBIT/Interest was \~10× in 2024 (and even higher in boom years), easily meeting ≥3× (✅). Interest expense is a small fraction (\~5-6%) of operating income (well under 15%) given Lennar's low debt costs (✅). **Current ratio** is \~1.5-1.7 for Lennar (builders maintain healthy liquidity for construction); as of Nov 2024 current assets $15.5B vs current liabilities $9.0B (\~1.7×) - *above* the 1.5 standard (✅) .

-   **Profitability & Efficiency:** Lennar's profit margins are decent but not at Buffett's ideal levels. Gross margin is around **21-24%** in recent years (varies with home prices and input costs) - this is **below 40%** (❌). Net profit margin was \~9-10% in 2024 (and peaked \~14% in 2021), which **falls short of 20%** (❌). However, Lennar excels in efficiency metrics: **Return on Equity** has averaged \~14-15% over the last decade (with recent years in the 18-20% range) - *just under* the 15% 10-yr average target, but very close (borderline ✅). **ROIC** (return on invested capital) has been roughly 10-12% in good years (maybe \~8% avg over 10 years), a bit under the 12% bar (❌). **ROA** (return on assets) was \~7.4% in 2024 and generally 6-8% historically (✅ passes ≥6%) . Finally, **depreciation is minimal** in Lennar's business (most costs flow through cost of sales for homes), so depreciation is well under 10% of gross profit (✅).

> **Verdict:** Lennar **meets or nearly meets** most of Buffett's criteria. It scores especially well on **valuation (✅)** and **financial strength (✅)**, and while its margins are lower than Buffett's ideal, this is typical for a homebuilder. Overall, Lennar appears to be a **Buffett-approved business**: fairly predictable housing demand, effective use of retained earnings, low debt, and bought at a steep discount to intrinsic value.

### **Nucor Corporation (NUE) -**

### **Steelmaker**

###  **🟡🔨**

-   **Valuation Discipline:** Nucor's valuation has often been attractive. Coming into 2025, its **P/E ratio** (trailing) was around 7-8× during the 2021-22 earnings peak, and \~12× on more normalized earnings - either way ≤15 (✅). Even after steel prices fell in 2023, NUE remained under 15× trailing earnings. **P/B ratio** is roughly 1.8-2.0 (Nucor's book value \~$75-80/share vs stock \~$155 ), thus it slightly **exceeds 1.5** (❌). However, Nucor's book is conservative (LIFO inventory, lots of assets), so Buffett may allow a higher P/B for a cyclical asset-rich firm. Importantly, **margin of safety** was evident: one intrinsic value analysis found NUE about *33% undervalued* relative to DCF fair value (✅). Some other models were less bullish, but given Nucor's low multiples and strong cash generation, it likely offered ≥25% upside to a reasonable fair value in Buffett's eyes.

-   **Cash Generation & Allocation:** Nucor is a **free cash flow machine** over the cycle. It has posted positive FCF in **10 out of 10 years** (2013-2022) - an impressive record for a cyclical manufacturer (✅). During the 2021 steel boom, Nucor generated over $7 billion FCF (FCF/Revenue \~15%); even in softer years it tends to squeeze out positive FCF. Its FCF to sales averaged \~5-10%+ in the past decade (✅). **Return on retained earnings** appears solid: Nucor pays a **stable dividend** (it famously never cuts its dividend) but usually retains &gt;70% of earnings. Those retained funds have fueled growth - Nucor's shareholder equity roughly doubled from 2015 to 2022 while it paid dividends, implying that retained earnings earned a decent return. A specific Buffett-style calc (increase in earnings vs retained portion) isn't readily available, but Nucor's ROE and book growth suggest it often clears 12% on reinvested capital (especially during upcycles) (✅ borderline).

-   **Growth & Predictability:** As a **cyclical commodity** business, Nucor fails the strict predictability test. Its **EPS is not consistent year-to-year** (❌). Averaging out the cycle, Nucor likely offers \~10% earnings yield on a mid-cycle basis (✅).

-   **Balance-Sheet Safety:** Nucor has a **very strong balance sheet**. Debt-to-Equity is only \~0.32 (32%) (or in other terms, D/E \~0.3-0.4 through 2025), easily below 0.5 (✅). The company often holds significant cash; net debt is low. **Interest coverage** is excellent - even in weaker years, Nucor covers interest \~10× or more (2023 EBIT/Interest &gt;10×; in 2021 it was 50×!). This passes the ≥3× test comfortably (✅). Interest expense is a small slice of operating profit (in 2023, interest \~$150M vs operating \~$2B, \~7.5%; in strong years &lt;3%), so **interest expense &lt;15% of EBIT** (✅). **Current ratio** about 2.8 as of Q1 2025 , indicating ample liquidity (✅).

The *median* ROE is around 13-15% (just shy of 15% target) (❌ borderline). **ROIC** likewise varies; median \~9-10% (not hitting 12% consistently) (❌). **ROA** median is \~5%, though in the last couple years it was 15%+ (long-term &lt;6%) (❌ fails on 10-yr average, though passes in current state). The fact that Berkshire still bought (and added more) suggests this is an outlier where Buffett (or his deputies) made an exception, likely due to Heico's unique quality (perhaps expecting high growth to eventually "earn into" the valuation).

-   **Cash Generation & Allocation:** Heico generates **consistent free cash flow** thanks to its aftermarket parts business model. It has positive FCF each year (✅). **FCF/Revenue** tends to be solid: e.g. in FY2022, Heico's operating cash flow was $440M on $2.21B revenue (\~20%), capex \~$50M, so FCF \~17.6% of revenue - easily above 5% (✅). Heico reinvests via acquisitions and some internal R&D. **Return on retained earnings** seems good - Heico pays a tiny dividend and reinvests \~90% of earnings. Its ROE \~15.7% is not huge, but given minimal payout, that implies retained earnings yielding \~15%, above 12% (✅). Historically, Heico's strategy of continuous small acquisitions in niche aerospace/defense parts has yielded \~15+% earnings growth, indicating strong returns on reinvested capital (✅).

-   **Growth & Predictability:** Heico's **EPS growth** has been very steady (15% CAGR over decades) *except* **2020**where earnings dropped (\~-15% YoY) due to COVID grounding airlines (❌ for unbroken growth). It quickly resumed growth in 2021-2022. **Book value per share** has grown from \~$5 in 2013 to \~$19 in 2023 (\~14% CAGR) with no declines (✅). **Owner's earnings** (Heico's net income plus amortization minus capex) are positive every year (✅). But at the current lofty market cap, **OE yield is tiny**: Heico's \~$400M net income vs $40B market cap \~1% yield (❌). Clearly, investors are valuing Heico for future growth, not current yield - a violation of the typical ≥10% owners yield that Buffett likes.

-   **Balance-Sheet Safety:** Heico has a **moderate debt** profile. **Debt/Equity \~0.57** (57%) , slightly above 0.5 (❌). However, its interest expense is quite low (Heico deliberately keeps debt modest). **Interest coverage** is very strong: EBIT covers interest \~30× (EBIT \~$376M, interest \~$12M) (✅). **Interest expense/EBIT** \~3% (✅ easily under 15%). **Current ratio \~3.5** (Heico typically has plenty of liquidity, including cash, to support operations), so &gt;1.5 (✅). In sum, aside from D/E marginally over 0.5, Heico's balance sheet is **rock-solid** (lots of cash, low leverage).

-   **Profitability & Efficiency:** **Gross margin \~39-40%** (Heico's gross margin 39.3% in 2022 , \~42% in 2021), basically at the 40% mark (✅ borderline). **Net margin \~14.5%** (FY2022 net $334M on $2.30B rev) , below 20% (❌). **ROE \~15.7%** (TTM) , meets 15% (✅). 10-year avg ROE \~16% as well - solid. **ROIC \~8.9%** , which fails the ≥12% test (❌). This partly reflects that Heico carries a lot of goodwill from acquisitions, which depresses ROIC - its incremental returns on acquisitions are likely higher than 9%, but accounting makes ROIC look mediocre. **ROA \~7.4%** , passes &gt;6% (✅). **Depreciation/Gross Profit** is low: Heico has light capex; depreciation was \~$50M vs gross profit \~$900M in 2022 (\~5.5%), easily under 10% (✅).

> **Verdict:** By Buffett's normal standards, Heico **fails the valuation test completely** but **passes the quality test in many aspects**. It's a **high-moat niche business** (FAA-approved parts supplier with cost advantage vs OEMs) that has grown profitably for decades - characteristics Buffett loves. Yet its stock is priced for perfection, which is not Buffett's usual entry point. The decision to invest in Heico indicates Buffett's team made a rare exception, likely believing **Heico's long-term growth runway and competitive advantage justify paying up**. In checklist terms: business fundamentals ✅, but initial price ❌. Buffett might argue that **Heico is akin to a "inevitables"** (great businesses that will grow into any reasonable valuation), so he's willing to hold his nose on the P/E. In summary, Heico is a **wonderful company acquired at not-so-wonderful price**, making it an outlier in the portfolio (hence Berkshire's stake is relatively small).

In our criteria, Domino's gets a 🟡 - **operationally excellent (high returns, consistent growth)**, but **financially engineered with high leverage and bought at no discount**. Berkshire's small position suggests they admire the business economics but are cautious (hence only a \~0.3% portfolio stake). ### **Apple Inc. (AAPL) -**

### **Consumer Tech Giant**

###  **✅🍏**

### **(Trimmed but still Berkshire's top holding)**

*(While Apple wasn't a new buy, Buffett's decision to retain \~90% of it means it's effectively "re-tested" each quarter against his standards. Here's how Apple stands, which also explains why Buffett trimmed some shares.)*

-   **Valuation Discipline:** Apple's valuation has **expanded substantially**. Its **P/E ratio** is \~30× (forward \~28×), which is **double** Buffett's ≤15 target (❌). Buffett himself acknowledged Apple at \~$180 might not offer his usual margin of safety . That is likely why he trimmed 7% of the position - taking some profit because Apple no longer hits the "on sale" mark (❌ on valuation now, though it was a huge bargain when he bought in 2016-2018).

It produces \~$90-100B in FCF annually (✅ every year positive). **FCF/Revenue** is extremely high - 26% in FY2022 (FCF $111B on $387B sales) , far above 5% (✅). Apple returns nearly all cash to shareholders via buybacks and dividends ($100B/year). **Return on retained earnings** is tricky - Apple doesn't "retain" earnings in the classic sense anymore; it returns effectively all (retained earnings on balance sheet have even shrunk as buybacks exceeded earnings at times). But looking historically: Apple's retained earnings from the 2000s onward fueled the most profitable product ecosystem ever, far exceeding 12% returns (✅). Now, Apple's ROE &gt;100% (due to tiny equity from buybacks), which is not a meaningful figure. In essence, Apple doesn't need to reinvest much internally to grow; it generates excess cash beyond its growth needs - the hallmark of a Buffett cash cow.

In the past decade, Apple had a couple of slight EPS declines (e.g. 2016 EPS fell 10%, 2019 EPS fell 0.5% vs 2018). So clearly it fails "10-year book growth with no declines" (❌ - book has declined dramatically due to buybacks). **Owner's earnings**(essentially Apple's FCF) are huge and positive every year (✅). But **OE yield** at the $3T valuation is modest: $100B OE on $3T \~3.3% yield (❌ &lt;&lt;10%). This is a key reason Buffett trimmed - Apple's earnings yield has compressed as the stock rose, violating the margin-of-safety yield he prefers.

It easily covers interest. **Interest coverage** is enormous - EBIT covers interest \~20× or more. For example, FY2022 EBIT $119B vs interest \~$2.6B \~46× (✅). **Interest exp/Op income** \~2.2% (✅ way below 15%). **Current ratio \~0.94** (Sep 2022 CA $135B vs CL $143B) (❌). But Apple's negative working capital is by design (it gets paid upfront by customers and pays suppliers later), a sign of strength, not distress. So practically, Apple's financial position is extremely safe - tons of liquidity and cash flow (the formal D/E and CR metrics register ❌ only due to Apple's capital structure choices, not any solvency risk).

-   **Profitability & Efficiency:** Apple is **highly profitable**. **Gross margin \~46%** as of 2024 (up from \~38% in 2021 with growth of services) , comfortably above 40% (✅). **Net profit margin \~25%** (FY2024 net margin 25.3% ) - above 20% (✅). **ROE** is inflated by low equity but enormous - over 100% (would be ✅ if meaningful). Looking pre-buybacks, 5-yr avg ROE (2012-2016) was \~33%, easily &gt;15% (✅). **ROIC** (return on invested capital, counting debt but excluding cash) is also excellent - estimates put Apple's ROIC \~25-30%. (E.g. ROIC \~26% in 2022). That far exceeds 12% (✅). **ROA** consistently \~20-30% (25.3% in 2024 ) - well above 6% (✅). **Depreciation/Gross Profit**: Apple's depreciation was \~$11B in 2022 vs gross profit \~$170B (\~6.5%), under 10% (✅). In short, Apple's profitability metrics are **off the charts**, ticking all the boxes easily except those distorted by capital structure.

> **Verdict:** Apple as a business is **an absolute superstar (✅ in quality/profitability)**. It only **fails on valuation grounds** in Buffett's framework. In 2016, when Buffett first bought Apple, it had a P/E \~10 and huge margin of safety - a no-brainer. By 2025, after a 5× stock increase, Apple no longer offers a value bargain, which is why Buffett trimmed. But he's still holding the bulk because Apple remains a **"great company"** with unmatched cash flow and a powerful ecosystem (Buffett calls it "probably the best business I know in the world" and jokes he'd love if it got cheaper so he could buy more). So Apple is a case where Buffett holds a ✅✅✅ business even when it becomes ❌ on entry valuation, trimming at the margins but largely sticking with this modern golden goose.

> **Verdict:** Bank of America **meets Buffett's criteria in terms of value and acceptable performance**, though not on growth or asset-light returns. BAC gets a ✅ for *valuation* and *adequate profitability*, but a ❌ on *predictability* and *Buffett's ideal high ROE*. Berkshire's sale of TMUS (100% exit) reflects that it didn't measure up as a long-term Buffett holding. It was likely a special situation investment that had run its course. In short, TMUS gets ❌ on Buffett's quality checklist, explaining why Berkshire liquidated the position.

## **Conclusion:**

The latest Berkshire portfolio moves highlight Buffett's **unwavering commitment to value and quality.** He **added stocks that were trading at significant discounts** to their intrinsic worth (homebuilders, a steelmaker, an insurer), **in businesses that generally meet his standards** of strong cash flows and durable advantages. Conversely, he **lightened positions that had either become overvalued** (Apple's valuation soaring beyond his comfort) **or that no longer fit his quality or risk criteria** (telecom/cable with high debt and uncertain growth).

When we stack up the companies against Buffett's classic checklist, we see a pattern: **Almost none are perfect on every metric** - even Buffett bends the rules at times. But those he's buying tend to score well in the most crucial areas: *sound valuation, robust cash generation, and competitive durability*. The companies he's moving away from are the ones with **thin margins, heavy leverage, or unpredictable futures**, which violate the "great company" test in one way or another.

In summary, **Berkshire's Q2 2025 changes show a rotation from richly priced tech/telecom toward more traditional value plays**, in line with Buffett's mantra of being *"greedy when others are fearful"*. The detailed DCF analyses confirm that many new holdings offered **substantial upside to intrinsic value**, while the trims had less room to run. And our criterion-by-criterion review affirms that **Buffett remains focused on high-return, moat-worthy businesses**: almost every stock he added (despite minor weaknesses) has **strong fundamentals that largely align with his principles**, whereas those he reduced generally fell short of his benchmarks.

Buffett's strategy thus continues to be a **masterclass in balancing value and quality** - seeking out companies trading below their true worth *and* meeting most of his gold-standard business criteria, and pruning those that do neither. His latest portfolio moves tell a clear story: **he's positioning Berkshire for resilience and value in an uncertain market**, sticking with what has worked for decades - *buying great companies at good prices* , and avoiding the rest.
