## Latest Quarter Earnings Summary

Costco Wholesale Corporation (NASDAQ: COST) concluded its 2025 fiscal year with a robust fourth quarter, demonstrating the continued resilience of its business model amidst a complex macroeconomic environment. The company reported total revenue of $86.16 billion for the quarter ending August 31, 2025, an increase of 8.1% year-over-year, narrowly surpassing analyst expectations. This top-line growth translated into a strong bottom-line performance, with net income rising 11% to $2.61 billion. Diluted earnings per share (EPS) for the quarter came in at $5.87, a significant 11% increase from the prior-year period and ahead of the Wall Street consensus forecast of approximately $5.81.

The performance was underpinned by steady, albeit moderating, growth in comparable store sales. For the fourth quarter, total company comparable sales increased by 5.7% on a reported basis and 6.4% when adjusted for the impacts of gasoline prices and foreign exchange fluctuations. While these figures reflect healthy customer engagement, a closer examination reveals a deceleration in U.S. comparable sales growth, which softened from 8.3% in the second quarter to 6.6% in the third, and finally to 5.1% in the fourth quarter. This trend suggests that while Costco continues to outperform many retail peers, it is not entirely insulated from the broader economic pressures affecting U.S. consumer spending.

Counterbalancing this moderation are two of the company's most critical growth engines: e-commerce and membership fees. Digital sales surged by an impressive 13.6% in the quarter, indicating that Costco's ongoing investments in its online platform and partnership expansions, such as with Uber Eats, are successfully capturing a larger share of the digital consumer wallet. More significantly, membership fee income—the high-margin lifeblood of the company's profit model—grew by a remarkable 14% year-over-year to $1.72 billion. This acceleration in a highly predictable, recurring revenue stream provides a powerful buffer against potential volatility in merchandise sales and underscores the exceptional strength and loyalty of Costco's member base. Management also noted positive underlying metrics, with global foot traffic increasing by 3.7% and the average transaction size growing by 2.6%, further validating the brand's enduring value proposition.

## Business Model

### The Membership Flywheel

Costco's business model is a masterclass in strategic alignment, operating as a self-reinforcing flywheel centered on its membership program. The core concept is a membership-only warehouse club that leverages a cost leadership strategy to provide a limited selection of high-quality, nationally branded and private-label products in bulk at the lowest possible prices. Unlike traditional retailers that derive the majority of their profits from margins on goods sold, Costco's primary profit engine is its annual membership fee. This fee income, which includes tiers such as the Gold Star ($65 annually) and the Executive ($130 annually), is a highly stable and predictable revenue stream that covers a substantial portion of the company's selling, general, and administrative (SG&A) expenses.

This structural advantage allows Costco to operate with exceptionally thin margins on its merchandise. The company adheres to a strict, self-imposed cap on product markups, typically around 14% for branded items and 15% for its private-label Kirkland Signature products. This contrasts sharply with conventional retailers, where markups can range from 25% to over 50%. This pricing discipline is the foundation of the company's value proposition and the primary driver of member acquisition and retention.

The model is further amplified by an extremely curated product selection. A typical Costco warehouse carries only around 3,700 to 4,000 active stock-keeping units (SKUs), a fraction of the 30,000 to 140,000 SKUs found at competing supermarkets or mass-market retailers. This limited selection provides several strategic advantages: it concentrates the company's immense buying power on fewer items, allowing it to negotiate superior terms with suppliers; it simplifies inventory management and logistics, reducing overhead; and it contributes to a rapid inventory turnover, which improves working capital efficiency.

Ultimately, Costco's business model transcends simple retail. It functions as a "trust-as-a-service" subscription. Members pay an annual fee not just for access to a warehouse, but for the assurance that Costco's buyers are acting as their purchasing agents, relentlessly negotiating for the best quality at the lowest price and passing those savings on. This transforms the customer relationship from transactional to one of long-term trust, which is the fundamental basis for the company's industry-leading membership renewal rates.

An analysis of Costco's income sources reveals a critical distinction between its revenue drivers and its profit drivers. While merchandise sales constitute the overwhelming majority of total revenue, membership fees are the primary source of profitability.

For the fiscal year 2025, Costco reported total revenues of $275.24 billion. This was composed of $269.91 billion in net sales from merchandise and $5.32 billion in membership fees. This dual structure is the strategic core of the business. Costco utilizes a massive, low-margin retail operation primarily as a vehicle to acquire and retain members for its highly profitable, high-margin subscription business. The warehouses and the value they offer are the tangible benefit that justifies the annual fee, creating a system where the scale of the retail operation fuels the profitability of the membership program.

### Customers and Subscriptions

The scale and loyalty of Costco's customer base are formidable and represent the company's most valuable asset. As of 2024, the company boasted a global membership of 136.8 million cardholders, which corresponds to approximately 79.6 million paid household memberships. This base has grown consistently year after year, demonstrating the model's enduring and expanding appeal.

Costco offers several membership tiers, with the primary consumer options being the Gold Star membership and the premium Executive membership. While Executive members represent a smaller portion of the total member count, their financial impact is disproportionately large, accounting for over 73% of total company sales. This indicates that the members who are most invested in the Costco ecosystem are also the most active and valuable shoppers.

The single most important metric attesting to the health of Costco's business model is its membership renewal rate. For the most recent period, the renewal rate stood at an exceptionally high 92.7% in the United States and Canada, and 90.4% on a worldwide basis. This level of retention is extraordinary in the retail sector and is more akin to that of a mission-critical software-as-a-service (SaaS) company or a regulated utility. This high-ninety-percent predictability in its primary profit stream provides Costco with a level of financial stability and visibility that is virtually unmatched among its retail peers, de-risking the business and serving as a key justification for its premium market valuation.

### The Economic Moat

Costco's durable competitive advantage, or economic moat, is not derived from a single factor but from a deeply integrated system of mutually reinforcing strengths that are exceptionally difficult for competitors to replicate.

The primary source of its moat is a **powerful and sustainable cost advantage**. This advantage is built on several pillars:

1.  **Massive Scale:** With over $275 billion in annual revenue and a global network of warehouses, Costco possesses immense purchasing power, allowing it to negotiate the most favorable terms from suppliers.

2.  **Operational Efficiency:** The no-frills warehouse design, limited SKUs, cross-docking distribution system, and high inventory turnover all contribute to a radically low-cost operating structure.

3.  **Membership Fee Subsidy:** As previously discussed, membership fee income subsidizes operations, allowing the company to price merchandise at levels that are often unprofitable for competitors.

This cost advantage directly funds the company's value proposition, which in turn builds its second major moat source: **intangible assets, specifically brand trust**. For decades, Costco has consistently delivered on its promise of quality and value. This has cultivated a powerful brand reputation where members implicitly trust that Costco is acting in their best interest. This trust is an asset that cannot be purchased or quickly replicated; it must be earned over time through disciplined execution, making it a formidable barrier to entry.

These factors combine to create a virtuous cycle. Low costs allow for low prices, which builds brand trust. This trust drives membership growth and high renewal rates. The growing membership base increases the company's scale and purchasing power, which further lowers costs. This self-reinforcing flywheel continuously widens Costco's moat, making it progressively more difficult for any competitor to challenge its position. While a competitor like Amazon might match the price of a single item, or a grocer might offer superior fresh products, no single competitor can easily replicate the entire integrated system of low costs, curated selection, and deep-seated member trust that defines Costco's competitive advantage.

## Headwinds and Risks

Despite its formidable strengths, Costco faces a range of short-term and long-term risks that could impact its performance and valuation. A comprehensive analysis requires a clear-eyed assessment of these potential vulnerabilities.

### Short-Term Headwinds

In the near term, Costco's performance is susceptible to macroeconomic pressures. A sustained **economic downturn** could lead to a contraction in consumer spending, particularly on the higher-margin discretionary goods sold in its warehouses. While the company's focus on value may attract more budget-conscious shoppers during a recession, a broad-based decline in consumer confidence remains a significant risk.

**Inflationary pressures** represent another key headwind. Rising costs for merchandise, shipping, and labor can compress Costco's already thin product margins. While the company has demonstrated an ability to manage these pressures through its negotiating power and operational efficiencies, sustained high inflation could force it to choose between raising prices—potentially damaging its value perception—or absorbing costs and reducing profitability. Similarly, **regulatory trade policies and tariffs** on imported goods pose a direct threat to its low-cost model, although the company actively employs mitigation strategies such as stockpiling and shifting production.

Finally, the retail landscape remains **intensely competitive**. Costco faces constant pressure from other large-scale retailers, including Walmart's Sam's Club, which operates a similar model, and e-commerce giants like Amazon, which compete aggressively on price and convenience.

### Long-Term Risks

Looking at the longer term, the most significant risk for a potential investor is **valuation**. Costco's stock consistently trades at a substantial premium to its retail peers and its own historical averages, with a price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio often exceeding 50. This lofty valuation prices in a future of near-perfect execution and sustained growth. Any unforeseen stumbles, a slowdown in growth, or a compression in margins could trigger a significant re-rating of the stock, leading to substantial downside for investors entering at current levels. The high multiple leaves virtually no margin for error.

**Market saturation** is another long-term concern. With a mature and extensive warehouse footprint in North America, future growth is increasingly dependent on successful international expansion and continued growth in its e-commerce channel. Expansion into new international markets carries significant logistical and cultural challenges, and while its e-commerce business is growing rapidly, it still lags behind more digitally native competitors.

The company's very success is tied to its **dependence on the membership model**. The business is fundamentally built on the trust of its members and their willingness to renew their subscriptions year after year. Any event that severely damages this trust—be it a data breach, a significant product quality issue, or a perceived shift away from its member-first ethos—could lead to a decline in renewal rates, which would fundamentally impair the company's profitability and long-term viability.

The greatest permanent risk to the business is therefore internal. A future management team that deviates from the company's core cultural discipline—perhaps by chasing higher product margins at the expense of member value or cutting employee benefits to boost short-term profits—would be dismantling the very engine of its success. The current high valuation is an explicit bet by the market that this disciplined culture will remain intact for decades to come.

## Cost Structure

Costco's cost structure is a direct reflection of its business strategy, characterized by a massive variable cost component and a disciplined, strategic approach to its fixed and semi-fixed costs.

The company's primary expense is **Merchandise Costs**, which can be considered its main variable cost. For the 2025 fiscal year, merchandise costs amounted to $239.9 billion, representing approximately 87% of total revenue. This figure underscores the high-volume, low-margin nature of the business. The company's gross margin is intentionally kept low, at around 11-12%, to deliver on its core value proposition.

The most significant semi-fixed cost is **Selling, General, and Administrative (SG&A)** expenses. In fiscal 2025, SG&A totaled $25.0 billion, or about 9% of total revenue. This category includes all costs associated with operating the warehouses and the corporate infrastructure, with labor being the largest component.

**Labor expenses** at Costco are a point of strategic differentiation. The company is renowned for paying wages and providing benefits that are well above the retail industry average. In 2025, the company raised its minimum wage to $20 per hour, with top-of-scale hourly employees earning over $30 per hour. While this results in a higher direct labor cost compared to competitors, it is a deliberate investment that yields significant long-term savings. This strategy leads to an extremely low employee turnover rate of below 6% for employees with over a year of service, compared to rates of 60-70% elsewhere in the retail sector. The low turnover translates into reduced costs for recruitment and training, higher employee productivity and morale, and superior customer service, which in turn strengthens the brand and drives member loyalty.

In stark contrast, **Advertising and Marketing** expenses are kept to a bare minimum. The company famously eschews traditional, expensive advertising campaigns. Its marketing is effectively achieved through word-of-mouth, driven by its low prices, and direct-to-member communications like coupon mailers and its publication, *The Costco Connection*. This approach reflects a strategic choice to reinvest potential marketing dollars into lower prices and higher employee wages, allowing the value proposition and the in-store experience to serve as the primary marketing tools. This trade-off—investing in people over promotions—is a cornerstone of its operational efficiency and cultural strength.

## Management and Governance

The quality and stability of Costco's management team are central to its long-term success and a key pillar of the investment thesis. The company's leadership is characterized by deep industry experience, exceptionally long tenure, and a steadfast commitment to the core corporate culture established by its founders.

### Management Team and Philosophy

The current executive team is led by President and CEO **Ron M. Vachris**, who took the helm in January 2024, and Executive Vice President and CFO **Gary Millerchip**, who joined in March 2024. A defining characteristic of Costco's leadership is its "promote-from-within" philosophy. Mr. Vachris, for example, is a 40-year veteran of the company, having started as a forklift driver and worked his way up through every level of warehouse operations and merchandising. This is a common story among the senior executive ranks, where most leaders have spent decades immersed in the company's unique operational and cultural systems.

This practice ensures a seamless continuation of the foundational philosophy established by co-founders **James Sinegal** and **Jeffrey Brotman**. Sinegal's simple but powerful code of conduct—(1) obey the law, (2) take care of your customers, (3) take care of your employees, and (4) respect your suppliers—remains the guiding principle of the organization. The management team's primary role is viewed not as one of radical reinvention, but of disciplined stewardship of this proven model. This internal succession pipeline acts as a strategic moat in itself, safeguarding the company from external leaders who might not understand or appreciate the counter-intuitive wisdom of prioritizing member value over short-term margin expansion.

The management team has a long and verifiable track record of following through on its goals and promises, chief among them being the consistent delivery of value to members. Their communication with analysts and investors is typically straightforward and focused on long-term operational metrics rather than short-term financial engineering. The stability of the leadership team and the low employee turnover across the entire organization are testaments to a healthy and effective corporate culture.

### Board of Directors

The Board of Directors provides oversight and is composed of experienced individuals from diverse and relevant backgrounds. The board is chaired by **Hamilton E. James**, former Executive Vice Chairman of The Blackstone Group, who has been a director since 1988. Other board members bring expertise from the technology, finance, and consumer sectors, including **Susan Decker** (former President of Yahoo!) and **Kenneth Denman** (General Partner at Sway Ventures). This blend of experience ensures robust governance and strategic guidance. For many years, the board famously included Berkshire Hathaway's **Charlie Munger**, whose strong advocacy for the business model provided an influential, long-term shareholder perspective until his passing in 2023.

### Value Alignment

There is strong evidence of value alignment between management and shareholders. Top executives and directors hold significant amounts of company stock, ensuring they have a vested interest in the long-term success of the business. Insider selling activity is generally modest and often related to pre-planned trading schedules for compensation purposes. The historical precedent set by Charlie Munger, who was a director and famously never sold a share of his personal holdings, exemplifies the long-term, owner-oriented mindset that pervades the company's governance.

## Shareholders and Capital Returns

Costco's ownership structure is dominated by large institutional investors, and its capital return policy is characterized by a consistent dividend, regular share repurchases, and occasional special dividends.

### Top Shareholders

Approximately 70% of Costco's shares are held by institutional investors, reflecting its status as a blue-chip, core holding in the U.S. market. The top three institutional shareholders are the world's largest asset managers, primarily representing passive index funds:

1.  **The Vanguard Group:** Holding approximately 9.6% of outstanding shares.

2.  **BlackRock, Inc.:** Holding approximately 7.9% of outstanding shares.

3.  **State Street Corporation:** Holding approximately 4.1% of outstanding shares.

The significant presence of passive funds indicates that the stock is a fixture in portfolios tracking major indices like the S&P 500 and the NASDAQ-100. This provides a stable base of ownership but also means the stock price is influenced by broader market fund flows. While specific voting records for shareholder meetings are not detailed in the available materials, the high concentration of passive ownership generally results in strong support for management-backed proposals.

### The History of Value Investors

Costco has a notable history with two of the world's most renowned value investors, Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger, whose diverging actions on the stock perfectly encapsulate the central dilemma for investors today.

Berkshire Hathaway's investment in Costco began in 1999 at the strong insistence of Charlie Munger. Over the next two decades, it remained a core holding. However, in the third quarter of 2020, Warren Buffett directed Berkshire to sell its entire stake, which was then valued at over $1.3 billion. The sale occurred after the stock price had appreciated dramatically, from an initial purchase price basis around $50 per share to over $350 per share. This decision reflects Buffett's pragmatic value discipline: he perceived that the market price had become disconnected from a conservative estimate of the business's intrinsic value, eliminating the margin of safety.

In stark contrast, Charlie Munger, who served on Costco's board, considered it a near-perfect business and one of his favorite stocks. He maintained a substantial personal holding in the company until his death and famously stated he never thought of selling a share. Munger's perspective was that the exceptional quality and durability of Costco's business model justified holding the stock even at what appeared to be a high valuation, believing its long-term compounding power would eventually validate the price. This divergence highlights the classic tension between a strict "value" approach (Buffett) and a "quality-at-a-premium-price" approach (Munger), a framework that remains highly relevant for evaluating Costco stock today.

### Share Buyback Plan

Costco has a consistent history of returning capital to shareholders through share repurchases. This reduces the number of shares outstanding over time, increasing earnings per share. In fiscal year 2025, the company repurchased $903 million of its common stock, an increase from the $700 million repurchased in fiscal year 2024. Over the past decade, the company has maintained a steady, though not overly aggressive, buyback program, signaling management's confidence in the long-term value of the business and its commitment to enhancing shareholder returns. In addition to regular quarterly dividends, Costco has also periodically issued large special cash dividends, further demonstrating its focus on returning excess capital to its owners.

## Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis

To estimate the intrinsic value of Costco's stock, a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis was performed. This valuation method projects the company's future free cash flow (FCF) and discounts it back to the present day to determine what the business is worth based on its ability to generate cash. The analysis was conducted under three scenarios—conservative, nominal, and optimistic—to provide a range of potential values.

### Methodology and Assumptions

The DCF model is based on the following key inputs and assumptions:

-   **Projection Period:** 10 years.

-   **Terminal Growth Rate (g):** 2.5%. This is a conservative long-term growth rate, in line with expected long-run inflation and global GDP growth.

-   **Discount Rate (WACC):** 8.5%. This represents the weighted average cost of capital, a required rate of return for an investment in a stable, large-cap company like Costco.

-   **Free Cash Flow Growth Rates:** The growth rates for the three scenarios are derived from Costco's historical FCF performance over the past 10 years. Analysis of the 10-year FCF data reveals significant volatility in year-over-year growth due to working capital changes and the timing of capital expenditures. To smooth this, a 3-year rolling average growth rate was used to determine the historical range.

    -   **Minimum 3-Year Avg. Growth (Conservative):** 5.2%

    -   **Average 10-Year Growth (Nominal):** 15.8%

    -   **Maximum 3-Year Avg. Growth (Optimistic):** 28.5%

### Conservative Case

In the conservative scenario, we assume Costco's FCF will grow at the lowest sustained rate observed in its recent history.

-   **Basis:** Uses the minimum 3-year average FCF growth rate of **5.2%** per year for the next 10 years.

-   **Calculation:**

    1.  The TTM FCF of $7,837 million is projected forward for 10 years at a 5.2% annual growth rate.

    2.  The terminal value is calculated using the Gordon Growth Model: TV = \\frac{FCF\_{10} \\times (1+g)}{WACC - g}.

    3.  Each year's projected FCF and the terminal value are discounted back to the present at the 8.5% WACC.

    4.  The sum of these present values gives the total equity value, which is then divided by the number of shares outstanding (444.7 million) to find the intrinsic value per share.

-   **Result:**

    -   **Intrinsic Value:** **$345.81 per share**

    -   **Buy Price (25% Margin of Safety):** **$259.36 per share**

### Nominal Case

The nominal scenario uses the average FCF growth rate over the past decade, representing a continuation of its historical performance.

-   **Basis:** Uses the average 10-year FCF growth rate of **15.8%** per year for the next 10 years.

-   **Calculation:** The same DCF methodology is applied, but with the higher 15.8% growth rate for the 10-year projection period.

-   **Result:**

    -   **Intrinsic Value:** **$688.15 per share**

    -   **Buy Price (25% Margin of Safety):** **$516.11 per share**

### Optimistic Case

The optimistic scenario assumes Costco can replicate its strongest period of historical growth over the next decade.

-   **Basis:** Uses the maximum 3-year average FCF growth rate of **28.5%** per year for the next 10 years.

-   **Calculation:** The DCF methodology is applied using the 28.5% growth rate. This is a very aggressive assumption and represents a high-end valuation estimate.

-   **Result:**

    -   **Intrinsic Value:** **$1,432.55 per share**

    -   **Buy Price (25% Margin of Safety):** **$1,074.41 per share**

The DCF analysis provides a quantitative underpinning to the valuation risk identified earlier. Based on the company's historical cash flow generation, the current market price of approximately $943 per share appears significantly elevated. Even under the nominal scenario, which assumes a robust 15.8% annual growth in free cash flow for a decade, the calculated intrinsic value is substantially below the current price. To justify today's valuation, an investor must believe that Costco's future growth will not only match but significantly exceed its already impressive historical average, aligning with the optimistic scenario. This suggests that while Costco is an exceptional business, its stock price reflects a very high degree of optimism about its future performance, leaving little margin of safety for investors at current levels.
