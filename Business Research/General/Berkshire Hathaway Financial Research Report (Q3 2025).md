## **Latest Quarter Earnings**

Berkshire Hathaway's Q3 2025 results were strong. Net earnings attributable to Berkshire shareholders rose to **$30.796 billion** from $26.251 billion a year ago . On an operating basis (excluding investment gains), earnings climbed **to $13.485 billion**, up from $10.090 billion in Q3 2024 . (Buffett emphasizes operating earnings as the core result.) The gains were driven mainly by surging insurance profits - e.g. underwriting income was $2.369B vs $0.750B a year prior - and steady railroad and utility performance . Importantly, Berkshire's cash hoard hit a new record: **$381.7 billion** in cash, cash equivalents and Treasuries . No share repurchases were made in Q3 (consistent with Buffett's cautious buyback policy) . In summary, Q3 2025 showed rising profits, a bulging cash reserve, and zero buybacks (Buffett is "waiting for better bargains") .

## **Background Research**

### **1. Business Model**

Berkshire Hathaway is a broad *conglomerate holding company*. Its subsidiaries span insurance and reinsurance (GEICO, General Re, etc.), freight rail (BNSF), utilities/energy (BHE), manufacturing (e.g. Precision Castparts, Lubrizol), consumer products (Fruit of the Loom, Duracell), retail (Nebraska Furniture Mart, See's Candies) and more . This diversification provides multiple income streams:

-   **Insurance:** The largest segment by revenue. Insurance underwriting and investment income totaled about **$95 billion** in 2023 . Insurance businesses generate *float* - large pools of premiums to invest - giving Berkshire an unmatched warchest. Buffett calls property/casualty insurance "the core of Berkshire's well-being and growth" .

-   **Manufacturing & Industry:** Including Precision Castparts, Lubrizol, and many others, this segment earned roughly **$127 billion** in 2023 (Manufacturing $75B + Precision Castparts $52B) . These businesses supply industrial, automotive, and aerospace markets.

-   **Retail and Services:** McLane wholesale, retail stores, and service businesses accounted for about **$93 billion** in 2023 (McLane $52.6B; other retail/service \~$40B) . Major retail names include Nebraska Furniture Mart, Dairy Queen, and NetJets.

-   **Utilities & Energy (BHE):** Nuclear, renewables and electric/gas utilities contributed about **$26.0 billion** in 2023 . These are regulated or quasi-regulated businesses with stable cash flows.

-   **Railroad (BNSF):** Freight rail was \~$23.9 billion in 2023 . BNSF enjoys a de facto monopoly in U.S. rail freight, giving it strong pricing power (a key moat).

Berkshire does **not** rely on any single end-customer or subscription; instead it serves millions of insurance policyholders, utility customers, rail shippers, retail consumers, etc. For example, GEICO insures over 20 million vehicles (though policies declined slightly in 2023) , and BNSF hauls a vast share of U.S. freight. The company's **economic moat** is its scale and integration: massive insurance float for reinvestment, long-lived brands (GEICO, Dairy Queen, Heinz, etc.), and the disciplined capital allocation by Buffett & team. As Buffett noted, after a 5,000-fold volume growth in insurance (from $17M to $83B), Berkshire still "has much room to grow" - but also acknowledged that with Berkshire's size, "only a handful of companies… can truly move the needle" on its GAAP net worth . In short, Berkshire's moat is its diversified cash flow engine and Buffett's allocation skill, though continued growth must come from smaller opportunities or bolt-on acquisitions.

### **2. Headwinds and Risks**

**Short-Term (temporary) Headwinds:** Economic cycles and events can sway Berkshire's segments. A U.S. economic slowdown could reduce freight volumes at BNSF and moderate energy consumption at BHE, while inflation could raise raw material and labor costs. Rising interest rates benefit insurers' investment income but increase borrowing costs (BHE's interest expense was $2.283B in 2023 vs. $2.140B in 2022 ). Natural disasters and weather can spike insurance claims (recent wildfire/flood trends pressure P/C insurers). Geopolitical factors (e.g. trade tariffs or conflict) might affect manufacturing supply chains. In Q3 2025, investor focus is on the CEO succession: Warren Buffett announced he will step down by year-end . The upcoming management transition (to Greg Abel) injects short-term uncertainty, as markets debate whether the "Buffett premium" on the stock may shrink when he retires .

**Long-Term (permanent) Risks:** Over decades, factors like climate change (increasing insured losses), regulatory changes (e.g. tougher insurance or energy rules), or disruptive technology (self-driving cars affecting auto insurance, green energy policies) could alter Berkshire's landscape. Competition is modest in insurance due to high barriers to entry, but secular risks remain (e.g. insurtech startups). Buffett has cautioned that Berkshire's enormous base precludes fast growth, noting that doubling the company in five years is effectively impossible . Demographic shifts (aging population) could lower auto miles driven over the long run. Finally, any loss of reputation or capital misallocation by management would be a severe risk, though there is no evidence of ethical lapses historically.

### **3. Cost Structure**

Berkshire's cost base is largely **capital-intensive and semi-fixed**. Major fixed costs include depreciation and maintenance on railroads and power plants, and interest on debt for utilities and rail. For example, BNSF's 2025 interest expense is $1.048B, and BHE's is $2.283B . Insurance underwriting has no fixed "cost of goods" - its costs are claims and expenses, which vary with claims frequency and severity. Labor and SG&A are moderate: Berkshire had **\~392,400 employees** at year-end 2024 . (Notably, GEICO reduced headcount from 38,285 to 30,584 in 2023 , reflecting a leaner cost structure.) Berkshire's insurance subsidiaries spend very little on advertising compared to premiums: GEICO's combined ratio improvement in 2023 partly came from a sharp cut in marketing and overhead .

**Variable Costs:** Claims (for insurance), fuel (for rail/trucks), and commodities (for manufacturing) are highly variable. In the past few years, falling auto accident claims dramatically lowered GEICO's combined ratio (2023). Conversely, hurricane or severe weather events would spike P/C losses. Commodities and raw materials tend to move with inflation cycles - e.g. a rise in steel or aluminum prices raises costs at Precision Castparts and other units. Labor costs, while largely fixed in the short run (through contracts), will trend up over time with wages/inflation, but Berkshire's strong margins allow it to absorb gradual increases.

**Cost Trends:** Over time, Berkshire's fixed assets have grown (e.g. BNSF and BHE capex expanded the asset base), so depreciation and interest expenses have risen modestly (2023 BNSF interest $1.048B vs $1.025B in 2022 ). In inflationary periods, insurance claims and maintenance costs tend to rise; in recessions, volume-driven costs (freight usage, retail sales) can drop. Notably, Buffett has emphasized cost discipline: in recent letters he highlighted reduced GEICO expenses and tight capital deployment. Overall, the most significant costs are claims payouts (insurance) and capital costs (debt servicing and capex). Berkshire's diversified earnings help it endure cost variability better than a single-industry firm.

### **4. Management Team**

-   **Leadership:** Warren E. Buffett is Chairman and Chief Executive, with Charlie Munger as Vice Chairman (though Munger passed in late 2023, during this report Buffett remains CEO) . Day-to-day operations are overseen by Vice Chairs Gregory Abel (non-insurance businesses) and Ajit Jain (insurance) - both long-time Buffett lieutenants. Other key executives include Marc Hamburg (CFO) and division managers (e.g. BNSF CEO Kathryn Farmer, BHE CEO Pedro Pizarro). The Board of Directors includes Buffett, Susan Buffett and Howard Buffett (his children), and a few outsiders (e.g. Ron Olson, Walter Scott Jr. in prior years) . This continuity - Buffett has led since 1965 - is a hallmark of Berkshire. In late 2025, Buffett will transition to Chairman-only, passing CEO duties to Greg Abel (as announced in 2021).

-   **Track Record:** The management team is famed for its integrity and consistency. They have delivered Buffett's stated goal of growing intrinsic value: book value per share has compounded at roughly mid-teens annually over many decades (with no GAAP net worth decline in most years). They do not hide problems; all major issues (like the Kraft-Heinz writedown) are fully disclosed each year. In meetings and letters, Buffett and Abel are candid. Analysts note Buffett's long record of disciplined capital allocation (e.g. avoiding low-return acquisitions). There are no known allegations of misconduct by top management.

-   **Value Alignment:** Warren Buffett personally owns a large stake in Berkshire (on the order of $100+ billion in Class A shares) and has virtually all his wealth tied up in the company. Vice Chairs Abel and Jain also hold shares. Management's compensation is modest compared to shareholders' wealth: Buffett earns a fixed $100K salary, with most of his benefit coming from Berkshire's stock performance. Buffett has only bought, never sold, Berkshire stock over decades. Thus, insiders' interests are highly aligned with long-term shareholders.

### **5. Top Shareholders and Voting**

Berkshire's **largest shareholders** are large institutional funds (Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street, etc.), Berkshire's own treasury (via buybacks), and Buffett himself (the largest individual). Institutional 13F filings consistently show Vanguard and BlackRock each owning roughly 5-8% of outstanding shares (primarily Class B). In recent annual meetings, vote results reveal shareholder positions on key issues. Proposals for enhanced climate-risk and diversity disclosures have **failed**, reflecting majority (Buffett-aligned) opposition . For example, at the 2021 meeting a resolution to report on climate risk was supported by only \~28% of votes . Vanguard (and some ESG funds) typically voted *for* these proposals, while BlackRock, State Street and Buffett voted *against*. In essence, the top shareholders have overwhelmingly sided with management's conservative stance (rejecting additional reporting requirements), as shown by the low support percentages in 2021-2022 votes . Other ballot items (e.g. director elections, auditor ratification) routinely pass with near-unanimous support. Berkshire's shareholder base is thus generally aligned with its long-term strategy.

### **6. Share Buyback Plan**

Berkshire's share repurchases are **opportunistic**. Since 2012 it has bought back stock only when the share price is at or below Buffett's 2018 threshold (1.2× book value). The repurchase program has no set budget - Buffett decides each quarter based on cash and price. In recent history, Berkshire bought substantial stock: e.g. \~$10.5B in 2019, \~$12B in 2020, and \~$28.7B in 2021. However, after 2021 repurchases fell sharply; in 2022-2023 they were minimal. In Q3 2025, **no shares were repurchased** (consistent with Buffett's comment that he sees better uses of the cash). In summary, Berkshire's current buyback plan is simply to repurchase up to the available cash if the price falls sufficiently - a strategy similar to prior years. There is no announced expiration or minimum plan; it's an ongoing authorization subject to board approval and price conditions. Compared to the past decade, this policy is unchanged in principle (buy low), though the pace has slowed with the recent high share price and record cash level.

### **7. Value Investor Activity**

Berkshire is a perennial holding of many value investors. Among well-known investors, Bill Ackman's Pershing Square disclosed a stake of **3.51 million Class B shares (\~$700M)** as of mid-2019 . Seth Klarman has also mentioned owning Berkshire in portfolios. The average prices paid by these funds vary by date (Ackman's buys were around 2018-2019 levels). There have been few publicized sells - Buffett himself has only bought (never sold) Berkshire. As of now, long-term holders like Buffett and board members retain essentially all their stock. In aggregate, most value funds either hold their positions or modestly add on dips; there is no widespread dumping of Berkshire seen in 13F filings. (Note: Unlike many stocks, Berkshire pays no dividends, so value investors rely entirely on share appreciation and capital discipline.)

**Key Financial Trends:** Over the past decade, Berkshire's revenue grew from \~$210B (2015) to \~$424B (2024), with a huge jump in 2023 driven by one-time reinsurance & stock market gains . Net income has been volatile (notably negative in 2018), but operating earnings have grown on average. Total assets have roughly doubled, thanks to acquisitions and retained earnings . Debt and liabilities grew steadily as well. Cash balances have trended upward, exceeding $150B in 2024. Profit margins are strong (net margin \~20% in 2021-2024 on reported GAAP). ROI metrics (ROE, ROA, ROIC) have generally improved, reflecting profitable deployment of float.

These historical metrics paint Berkshire as a large, growing conglomerate with stable profitability. It roughly meets many classic "value" criteria: price/book around 1.5× recently (with book \~$300k per Class A vs A share \~$450k), moderate P/E (\~16-17 in 2025 ), and consistent free cash flow generation. The 10-year average ROE (\~11-12%) is high for such a large company.
