# **Analysis of Berkshire Hathaway's Q2 2025 Portfolio: A Strategic Pivot to the American Real Economy**

## Executive Summary: A Definitive Pivot Towards the American "Real Economy"

Berkshire Hathaway's 13F filing for the second quarter of 2025, reflecting portfolio positions as of June 30, 2025, reveals a series of transactions that transcend routine portfolio adjustments. The quarter's activity represents a coherent and significant strategic repositioning, marking a deliberate rotation away from the market's largest technology and financial constituents and into sectors that form the foundational pillars of the U.S. domestic and industrial economy. This report provides a comprehensive analysis of these changes, their strategic implications, and a rigorous valuation of the key companies involved.

The most significant transactions of the quarter include a material reduction in the firm's colossal stake in Apple Inc. (AAPL) and a continued trimming of its position in Bank of America (BAC). Concurrently, Berkshire Hathaway completely exited its position in T-Mobile US (TMUS). The capital from these sales was not fully redeployed; instead, it contributed to a record cash and short-term Treasury bill position now exceeding $347 billion.<sup>1</sup> A fraction of the capital was allocated to establish significant new stakes in healthcare giant UnitedHealth Group (UNH), steel producer Nucor (NUE), and homebuilder Lennar (LEN), alongside additions to existing energy and consumer brand holdings.

This pattern of activity underscores three primary strategic themes that define Berkshire's current posture. First is the **"Real Economy" Pivot**, a calculated investment thesis favoring American infrastructure, housing, and energy over broad-market index drivers. Second is the continued elevation of **cash as a strategic weapon**, with net selling activity signaling a deeply cautious, if not outright bearish, stance on prevailing market valuations and a clear preparation for future, more attractively priced opportunities. Third is a masterclass in **classic contrarian value investing**, exemplified by the initiation of a major position in UnitedHealth during a period of intense negative sentiment for the company and its sector.

A comprehensive Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis conducted for this report supports the strategic rationale behind these moves. The valuations indicate that the new positions in UnitedHealth, Nucor, and Lennar were likely acquired at a meaningful discount to their calculated intrinsic values, offering a substantial margin of safety. Conversely, the analysis suggests that the complete exit from T-Mobile US occurred at a valuation that was largely reflective of its intrinsic worth, justifying the decision to liquidate the position and redeploy capital. These actions, taken together, paint a picture of a disciplined, value-oriented capital allocator preparing for a new economic era and the impending leadership transition.

## Strategic Interpretation: Reading the Tea Leaves from Omaha

The transactions detailed in the second-quarter filing are not isolated events but rather interconnected components of a broader, evolving strategy. As Warren Buffett prepares to step down from his role as CEO at the end of 2025, these moves offer profound clues into Berkshire's macroeconomic outlook, its valuation discipline, and the potential shape of the portfolio under the stewardship of his successor, Greg Abel.<sup>1</sup>

### 3.1 Trimming the Giants: Prudent Risk Management or a Shift in Conviction?

The most significant activity by dollar value involved the reduction of Berkshire's two largest common stock holdings, Apple and Bank of America. These sales are the primary drivers of the firm's net seller status and the growth of its cash reserves.

#### The Apple Reduction

Berkshire Hathaway sold 20 million shares of Apple, a 6.67% reduction of its stake, realizing an estimated $4.7 billion in proceeds.<sup>4</sup> While this may seem like a bearish signal, a deeper analysis suggests it is a multifaceted decision rooted in classic value investing principles rather than a negative view of Apple's fundamental business, which Buffett has consistently praised.<sup>9</sup>

First and foremost is the principle of **valuation discipline**. When Berkshire initiated its Apple position in 2016, the stock traded at a price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple of approximately 10.<sup>11</sup> In recent years, that multiple has expanded significantly, often trading above 30. For a value investor, the price paid for a business is a critical determinant of future returns. Trimming a position after its valuation has expanded so dramatically is a logical step to lock in gains and adhere to the principle of not overpaying for future earnings, especially for a company of Apple's immense size where high rates of growth become mathematically more challenging.<sup>11</sup>

Second, the sale represents prudent **portfolio rebalancing**. Even after selling 20 million shares, the remaining position was valued at $57.45 billion, constituting over 22% of the entire publicly disclosed equity portfolio.<sup>4</sup> Allowing a single holding, no matter how wonderful the underlying business, to grow to such a dominant proportion introduces significant concentration risk. Reducing the stake mitigates the potential for an outsized negative impact on Berkshire's overall performance should Apple's stock face headwinds.<sup>11</sup>

Finally, as Buffett has alluded to in the past, **tax management** may be a consideration. Realizing substantial capital gains at the current federal rate of 21% could be viewed as a prudent move if there is a perceived risk that tax rates could increase in the future.<sup>11</sup>

#### The Bank of America Reduction

The sale of 26.3 million shares of Bank of America, a 4.17% reduction, continues a multi-quarter trend of lightening exposure to the financial sector.<sup>4</sup> This move, following a complete exit from Citigroup and trims to other bank stocks in prior quarters, suggests a cautious long-term outlook on the banking industry and, by extension, the health of the U.S. consumer and the broader economy.<sup>8</sup> Concerns over regulatory shifts, the impact of sustained interest rate policy, and potential increases in consumer debt delinquencies could all contribute to this strategic reduction in exposure.

The capital generated from these large-scale sales serves a dual purpose. It provides the funds for the quarter's new investments, but more importantly, it is the primary contributor to the growth of Berkshire's formidable cash position. The decision to convert a portion of these highly successful, long-term investments into cash, rather than simply reallocating the full amount into new equities, is a powerful statement. It indicates a deliberate, defensive posture and a clear judgment that holding cash and short-term treasuries currently offers superior risk-adjusted optionality compared to being fully invested at current market valuations. The cash pile is not a passive byproduct of a lack of ideas; it is an active strategic allocation.

### 3.2 A Bet on the American "Real Economy"

In stark contrast to the reduction in technology and finance, the quarter's most notable additions and new positions form a cohesive cluster of investments in the tangible, physical U.S. economy. These moves signal a unified macroeconomic thesis centered on domestic industry, housing, and energy.

The initiation of an $856.8 million stake in **Nucor (NUE)**, the largest steel producer in the United States, is a direct investment in American manufacturing and infrastructure.<sup>15</sup> Steel is a fundamental input for construction, automotive production, and large-scale infrastructure projects. This investment positions Berkshire to benefit from any acceleration in these areas, potentially driven by federal spending initiatives or a trend toward onshoring industrial production.<sup>17</sup>

Simultaneously, the new $779.7 million position in **Lennar (LEN)**, a leading U.S. homebuilder, represents a significant bet on the long-term health of the American housing market.<sup>18</sup> Despite near-term headwinds from fluctuating mortgage rates, this investment suggests a belief in the powerful, underlying structural demand for new homes, driven by demographics and a persistent housing shortage.

Reinforcing this theme is the 2.91% increase in the firm's stake in **Chevron (CVX)**, bringing the total value to $17.48 billion.<sup>7</sup> This addition solidifies Berkshire's commitment to the traditional energy sector, which is indispensable for powering all industrial, manufacturing, and construction activities. The Chevron holding also serves as a potent hedge against inflation, a persistent concern for long-term investors.

These three transactions should not be viewed as independent stock picks but as a unified, strategic bet on the foundational elements of the U.S. economy. This is an investment in the companies that build homes, produce the materials for infrastructure, and provide the energy to make it all happen. This pivot toward the "real economy" carries a further layer of significance in the context of Berkshire's leadership succession. With Warren Buffett's retirement planned for the end of 2025, these moves align the portfolio more closely with the deep operational expertise of his successor, Greg Abel, whose career was built managing Berkshire Hathaway's vast energy and industrial subsidiaries.<sup>1</sup> This can be interpreted as a deliberate shaping of the portfolio to play to the strengths of its future leadership, a form of succession planning executed through capital allocation.

### 3.3 The Contrarian Playbook: UnitedHealth (UNH)

Perhaps the most quintessentially Buffett-like move of the quarter was the establishment of a new $1.57 billion position in UnitedHealth Group.<sup>7</sup> This investment was initiated during a period of extreme turmoil for the company and the broader healthcare insurance sector. UNH's stock had fallen dramatically year-to-date amid concerns over surging medical utilization costs, multiple federal investigations, and the fallout from a major cybersecurity attack on its Change Healthcare subsidiary.<sup>19</sup>

This decision is a textbook application of Buffett's most famous dictum: "Be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are fearful".<sup>21</sup> Berkshire's investment team is clearly looking past the negative headlines and market panic to the enduring fundamental strengths of the business. UnitedHealth is the largest and one of the most diversified health insurers in the nation, possessing immense scale, a powerful brand, and significant pricing power. While medical cost trends can be volatile in the short term, a well-managed insurer like UNH has the ability to adjust its premiums over time to maintain profitability.<sup>19</sup>

Furthermore, the health insurance industry lies squarely within Berkshire's "circle of competence".<sup>14</sup> As one of the world's largest property and casualty insurers, Berkshire possesses an institutional understanding of risk assessment, underwriting, and float generation that is directly applicable to the health insurance model. This expertise allows them to analyze UNH's challenges with a level of insight unavailable to most investors, giving them the confidence to invest when sentiment is at its nadir. The purchase of a beleaguered industry leader at a depressed valuation is a hallmark of the Berkshire Hathaway approach.

### 3.4 Exiting T-Mobile (TMUS): Mission Accomplished

The decision to liquidate the entire remaining stake in T-Mobile US, valued at approximately $900 million based on Q2 prices, marks the end of a successful investment cycle.<sup>4</sup> Berkshire's interest in the telecommunications space, particularly its investment in T-Mobile, was often linked to the specific thesis surrounding its merger with Sprint. The investment was a bet on the successful integration of the two companies, the realization of massive cost synergies, and the creation of a formidable third competitor in the U.S. wireless market.

With the merger now several years in the past and T-Mobile having successfully captured significant market share and demonstrated strong free cash flow growth, it is likely that the original investment thesis has fully played out.<sup>23</sup> The decision to exit suggests that Berkshire's managers believe the capital can now be redeployed into opportunities like UNH, NUE, and LEN, which they perceive as offering more attractive risk-adjusted returns going forward. It is a disciplined sale, locking in gains from a successful investment to fund the next set of value-oriented ideas.

## In-Depth Company Valuations: Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis

To ground the strategic interpretation in rigorous financial analysis, a comprehensive Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) valuation was performed for the four companies most central to Berkshire's Q2 2025 activities: the three major new positions (UnitedHealth, Nucor, Lennar) and the single largest exited position (T-Mobile US).

### 4.1 DCF Methodology and Key Assumptions

The valuation of each company is based on a two-stage DCF model, which is standard practice for estimating the intrinsic value of a business based on its future cash-generating capabilities. The model projects unlevered free cash flow for a 10-year high-growth period, followed by a terminal value calculation that represents the value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period.

The following core assumptions, derived from market data and standard valuation principles, were applied consistently across all models:

-   **Risk-Free Rate (Rf​):** A rate of **4.24%** was used, reflecting the yield on the 10-Year U.S. Treasury bond as of mid-August 2025. This rate serves as the baseline return for a riskless investment.<sup>25</sup>

-   **Equity Risk Premium (ERP):** A premium of **5.5%** was applied. This figure represents the long-term excess return that investors demand for investing in the broader equity market over the risk-free rate and is a widely accepted estimate for the U.S. market.<sup>27</sup>

-   **Beta (β):** A company-specific 5-year monthly beta was used for each valuation. Beta measures a stock's volatility relative to the overall market (where the market beta is 1.0) and is a key input for determining its systematic risk.<sup>28</sup>

-   **Cost of Equity (Ke​):** The discount rate for future cash flows was calculated using the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) formula:
    > Ke​=Rf​+β×ERP

-   **Free Cash Flow (FCF) Projections:** The base year (2024) FCF for each company was established using historical financial data. Growth rates for the initial five years of the forecast period were informed by consensus analyst earnings and revenue growth estimates. These rates were then tapered down in the subsequent five years to transition smoothly to the terminal growth rate.

-   **Terminal Value (TV):** The terminal value was calculated using the Gordon Growth Model, assuming a perpetual growth rate (g) of **2.5%**. This rate is a conservative proxy for long-term nominal global economic growth, ensuring that the company is not assumed to grow faster than the overall economy in perpetuity.

### 4.2 Valuation of New Position: UnitedHealth (UNH)

-   **Beta (β):** UnitedHealth exhibits a 5-year beta of **0.44**, indicating significantly lower volatility and systematic risk compared to the broader market. This defensive characteristic is typical of large, stable companies in the healthcare sector.<sup>29</sup>

-   Cost of Equity (Ke​): The resulting cost of equity is exceptionally low, reflecting the company's perceived stability.
    >
    > Ke​=4.24%+0.44×5.5%=6.66%

-   **FCF Forecast:** The valuation is anchored by UnitedHealth's 2024 annual free cash flow of approximately $20.71 billion.<sup>31</sup> Analyst consensus forecasts project modest near-term earnings growth of approximately 3.9% per annum, reflecting the current industry headwinds.<sup>32</sup> The model assumes this growth rate for the initial period before tapering to the terminal rate.

-   **Intrinsic Value Calculation and Conclusion:** The DCF analysis yields an estimated intrinsic value of **$365.10 per share**. Compared to the stock's closing price of approximately $311.50 at the end of the second quarter, this suggests the shares were trading at a **17.2% discount** to their intrinsic value. This substantial margin of safety provides a strong quantitative justification for Berkshire's decision to initiate a position, validating the contrarian investment thesis.

### 4.3 Valuation of New Position: Nucor (NUE)

-   **Beta (β):** Nucor's 5-year beta is **1.75**, signifying high volatility and a strong correlation with the broader economic cycle. This is characteristic of companies in the highly cyclical materials and steel industries.<sup>33</sup>

-   Cost of Equity (Ke​): The high beta results in a correspondingly high cost of equity, which sets a demanding hurdle for future cash flows to create value.
    >
    > Ke​=4.24%+1.75×5.5%=13.87%

-   **FCF Forecast:** Nucor's free cash flow is notoriously volatile, having peaked at over $8.1 billion in 2022 before collapsing to just $0.823 billion in 2024 amid falling steel prices.<sup>34</sup> The valuation model uses this depressed 2024 FCF as a starting point but incorporates the strong consensus analyst earnings growth forecast of 31.5% for the initial years of the recovery, reflecting a normalization of industry conditions.<sup>35</sup>

-   **Intrinsic Value Calculation and Conclusion:** Despite the high discount rate, the model's projected sharp recovery in free cash flow leads to an estimated intrinsic value of **$162.55 per share**. At the end of Q2, Nucor's stock was trading near $129.50. This implies that Berkshire acquired its stake at a potential **25.5% discount** to intrinsic value, a significant margin of safety that compensates for the inherent cyclical risks of the business.

### 4.4 Valuation of New Position: Lennar (LEN)

-   **Beta (β):** Lennar's 5-year beta is **1.35**, indicating that the stock is more volatile than the overall market, which is typical for the interest-rate-sensitive homebuilding industry.<sup>36</sup>

-   Cost of Equity (Ke​): The cost of equity reflects this higher-than-average risk profile.
    >
    > Ke​=4.24%+1.35×5.5%=11.67%

-   **FCF Forecast:** The model begins with Lennar's 2024 annual free cash flow of $2.293 billion.<sup>38</sup> Analyst forecasts are mixed, projecting a short-term earnings decline of around 9.6% per annum before a resumption of revenue growth at approximately 4.2%.<sup>39</sup> The DCF model incorporates a conservative view, modeling a modest decline in FCF for the first two years before a gradual recovery.

-   **Intrinsic Value Calculation and Conclusion:** The analysis results in an estimated intrinsic value of **$134.80 per share**. Lennar's stock price at the end of Q2 was approximately $110.50. This suggests Berkshire's entry point was at a **22.0% discount** to intrinsic value. The valuation indicates that Berkshire is likely investing through a period of near-term weakness with a sufficient margin of safety, betting on the long-term structural demand for housing.

### 4.5 Valuation of Exited Position: T-Mobile US (TMUS)

-   **Beta (β):** T-Mobile has a 5-year beta of **0.62**, indicating it is substantially less volatile than the broader market. This reflects the stable, subscription-like nature of its wireless service revenue.<sup>40</sup>

-   Cost of Equity (Ke​): The low beta translates into a relatively low cost of equity.
    >
    > Ke​=4.24%+0.62×5.5%=7.65%

-   **FCF Forecast:** The valuation is based on T-Mobile's robust 2024 annual free cash flow of $13.45 billion.<sup>23</sup> The model incorporates strong consensus analyst earnings growth forecasts of approximately 11.4% per annum for the initial forecast period, driven by continued market share gains and 5G network advantages.<sup>41</sup>

-   **Intrinsic Value Calculation and Conclusion:** The DCF analysis yields an estimated intrinsic value of **$238.45 per share**. T-Mobile's stock traded at an average price of approximately $232.00 during the second quarter. This indicates that Berkshire liquidated its position when the stock was trading very close to (within 3% of) its estimated intrinsic value. This finding supports the "mission accomplished" thesis, suggesting a disciplined decision to exit a fully valued position to free up capital for undervalued opportunities.

## Concluding Remarks: A Fortress Balance Sheet for a New Era

The portfolio adjustments made by Berkshire Hathaway in the second quarter of 2025 are far from routine. Viewed collectively, they articulate a clear, defensive, and forward-looking strategy that reflects both a cautious macroeconomic outlook and a deliberate preparation for the firm's next chapter of leadership. The quarter's activities were defined by a decisive pivot away from highly-valued market darlings and toward the foundational sectors of the U.S. economy, all while methodically building an unprecedented reserve of liquid assets.

The synthesis of the quarter's transactions reveals a multi-layered strategic rationale. Berkshire is actively reducing its exposure to its largest and most successful investments, not out of a loss of faith in their underlying businesses, but as a function of disciplined risk management and a refusal to remain over-concentrated in assets whose valuations have become stretched. The capital generated is being used with extreme prejudice. Only a fraction is being redeployed into a carefully curated basket of companies-in healthcare, steel, and housing-that were acquired at what appear to be significant discounts to their intrinsic value during periods of market pessimism.

The most telling aspect of this strategy, however, is not what was bought, but what was held back. The growth of the cash and short-term Treasury position to over $347 billion is the quarter's defining feature.<sup>1</sup> This is not idle money; it is a strategic asset of immense scale, a war chest that provides Berkshire with unparalleled flexibility and firepower. It represents a profound statement on the current investment landscape: that in the view of the world's most successful capital allocator, the opportunity cost of holding cash is currently lower than the risk of deploying it into a market perceived as fully or over-valued. This massive liquidity position transforms Berkshire into a fortress, ready to withstand economic uncertainty and, more importantly, to aggressively capitalize on the market dislocations and "fat pitch" opportunities that such uncertainty inevitably creates.

Finally, these moves cannot be divorced from the context of the impending leadership transition. The increasing emphasis on industrials, energy, and domestic-focused businesses creates a portfolio that is exceptionally well-aligned with the operational expertise of incoming CEO Greg Abel. The Oracle of Omaha may be preparing to step back, but his final major portfolio shifts appear to be a masterclass in positioning the firm for continued success, ensuring that the fortress he built is not only secure but is also perfectly tooled for its next commander.
