# **Microsoft (MSFT) Financial Research Report**

## **1. Background Research**

**Business Model:** Microsoft is a diversified technology conglomerate that **primarily earns revenue by developing and licensing software, providing cloud services, and selling hardware and related services**. In one sentence: Microsoft's model centers on **software ecosystems (Windows & Office), cloud infrastructure (Azure), and other platforms (LinkedIn, Xbox, etc.) that drive recurring revenue**.

**Main Income Sources:** Microsoft segments its operations into Productivity & Business Processes, Intelligent Cloud, and More Personal Computing. Key income streams include Office 365 (productivity software subscriptions), Azure (cloud services), and Windows licensing. A revenue breakdown for FY2024 shows the largest segment is **Server Products & Cloud (Azure)** at \~39.9% of revenue, followed by **Microsoft Office** (\~22.4%), and then **Windows** (\~9.5%). Smaller contributors are LinkedIn (\~6.7%), Gaming (\~8.8%), Search Ads (\~5.1%), etc.. The pie chart below illustrates the proportion of each segment's revenue in FY2024:

*Share of Microsoft FY2024 revenue by segment. "Server Products & Cloud" (Azure and related server software) is by far the largest contributor.*

These revenue sources reflect Microsoft's transition toward cloud and subscription services. **The distribution reveals Microsoft's heavy reliance on cloud and enterprise software** - nearly 2/3 of revenue comes from server/cloud and Office productivity lines, indicating strength in recurring enterprise demand. Meanwhile, traditional PC-based revenue (Windows, devices) is less than 12%, highlighting Microsoft's successful shift away from PC-centric business. This diversified mix (productivity software, cloud, and other services) provides resilience - growth in cloud and Office can offset declines in legacy segments like Windows.

**Latest Headwinds:** Microsoft faces several current challenges: (1) **Macroeconomic and currency pressures** - a strong USD and cautious IT spending environment have modestly slowed growth in some segments. (2) **Regulatory scrutiny and antitrust** - authorities in the U.S. and EU are examining Microsoft's dominance in cloud and software (e.g. the Activision-Blizzard acquisition drew antitrust reviews). (3) **Competition** - rivals like Amazon (AWS) and Google in cloud, or Zoom and Google Workspace in productivity, require continuous innovation. (4) **Tech transitions** - the rise of AI is both an opportunity and a threat; Microsoft must invest heavily (e.g. in OpenAI partnership) to remain a leader while managing costs. Despite these headwinds, Microsoft's recent performance has been strong - FY2024 revenue grew \~15% and net income \~22%, but **investors remain watchful of economic uncertainty and Big Tech regulatory risks**.

**Intrinsic Valuation (Buffett Methodology):** Warren Buffett's approach to intrinsic value involves calculating **owner earnings** (true free cash flow available to shareholders) and discounting them at a reasonable rate. For Microsoft, we estimate owner earnings by starting with net income and adding back non-cash charges and subtracting sustaining capital expenditures. In 2024, net income was $88.1 B. Microsoft's depreciation plus amortization in 2024 was about $15 B (estimated), and capital expenditures were about $32 B (significantly investing in data centers). Thus, **owner earnings** are roughly \~$71 B annually. Assuming a discount rate around 8% (reflecting a long-term equity return expectation) and a long-term growth rate of \~5-6%, a simplified discounted cash flow yields an intrinsic market capitalization on the order of **$2.3-2.5 trillion**, or roughly **$300 per share**. This aligns with independent estimates - for example, one analysis pegs Microsoft's intrinsic value near **$303 per share**. With Microsoft's stock trading around $370-$440 recently, it appears **above intrinsic value** by Buffett's standards (implying a thin margin of safety). In Buffett's terms, Microsoft is a fantastic company but was a much better bargain in years past (e.g. in 2015 when it traded at single-digit multiples) than at today's higher valuation. Buffett himself has *never purchased MSFT for Berkshire*, citing concerns about perceived insider advantage due to his friendship with Bill Gates .

**Value Investor Buy/Sell History:** Notably, **Warren Buffett, Charlie Munger, Howard Marks, and Li Lu have largely avoided Microsoft stock** despite its strong performance. Buffett has explained that **he will never buy Microsoft** for Berkshire to avoid any appearance of insider knowledge from Bill Gates . Charlie Munger, Buffett's partner, likewise refrained - in recent years Munger has favored other tech names (like Apple) but not MSFT. **Howard Marks** (Oaktree Capital) is known for distressed debt and has not made significant Microsoft stock purchases - in fact, Oaktree's portfolio tilts toward energy, finance, and industrial sectors, with no MSFT in recent reports. **Li Lu** (Himalaya Capital) also did not hold MSFT in his US portfolio; his focus has been on financials (Bank of America) and Chinese tech, but not Microsoft. In summary, these value investors **held no meaningful stake in Microsoft** - hence no notable buy/sell transactions or average cost basis to report. Buffett and Munger *admire* Microsoft's business (Munger once remarked that one must own dominant tech companies in a modern portfolio) yet chose not to own it, primarily due to **circle-of-competence concerns and conflict of interest** (in Buffett's case). As of 2025, none of these investors' firms hold Microsoft, *illustrating how even an exceptional company can lie outside a particular value investor's mandate or comfort zone*.

## **2. Board and Shareholders**

**Board of Directors:** Microsoft's board consists of 12 members with diverse professional portfolios. Key directors include:

-   **Satya Nadella** - Chairman & CEO of Microsoft, driving the company's cloud-focused transformation (no familial ties of note; no criminal history).

-   **Sandra "Sandi" Peterson** - Lead Independent Director (Operating Partner at CD&R; former Johnson & Johnson executive). She brings healthcare industry expertise; no known familial conflicts or legal issues.

-   **Reid Hoffman** - Co-founder of LinkedIn and partner at Greylock Partners. His presence reinforces Microsoft's commitment to social networking and AI. (Hoffman is a tech veteran with no fraud history; he is known to be well-connected in Silicon Valley.)

-   **Hugh Johnston** - CFO of PepsiCo, contributing financial acumen; no family ties to Microsoft insiders.

-   **Teri List** - Former CFO of Gap, Inc., providing retail and finance expertise.

-   **Penny Pritzker** - Former U.S. Commerce Secretary and founder of PSP Partners. She hails from the prominent Pritzker family (Hyatt Hotels owners) but has no familial connection to Microsoft's founders.

-   **Carlos Rodriguez** - Executive Chair of ADP, experienced in human capital management solutions.

-   **Charlie Scharf** - CEO of Wells Fargo, offering banking and governance expertise. (Wells Fargo's past account fraud scandal predates Scharf's tenure; he has no personal implication in wrongdoing.)

-   **John W. Stanton** - Pioneer in wireless communications (Trilogy Partnerships), lending telecom industry insight.

-   **John Thompson** - Former Microsoft chairman and CEO of Symantec. He led the board through the CEO transition; no known criminal issues (though Symantec faced some controversies under his watch, Thompson himself has a clean record).

-   **Emma Walmsley** - CEO of GlaxoSmithKline (GSK), adding global healthcare perspective; no familial ties, no legal troubles.

-   **Padmasree Warrior** - Former CTO of Cisco and Motorola, expertise in technology and engineering; no known controversies.

Overall, **Microsoft's directors are seasoned leaders across tech, finance, and government**. There are no obvious nepotistic relationships on the board (e.g. no Gates or Ballmer family members). The board's reputation is clean - none of the current directors have known fraudulent or criminal history. This professional and independent board structure aligns with good governance practices, especially with an independent lead director in Sandi Peterson to balance Nadella's combined CEO/Chair role.

**Top Shareholders and Voting History:** Microsoft's ownership is dominated by large institutional investors. The **top shareholders** as of 2025 are **The Vanguard Group (\~9.1% of outstanding shares)**, **BlackRock (\~7.6%)**, and **State Street (\~3.9%)**. Collectively, these three passive fund giants control over 20% of Microsoft's stock. Among individuals, **former CEO Steve Ballmer holds \~4.5%** (the largest individual stake) and **co-founder Bill Gates about 1.3%**. (Gates has steadily sold or donated shares over time - in 1990s he owned \~20%; by 2023 he was down to just over 1%.) CEO Satya Nadella and President Brad Smith also own shares, but each well under 1% of the company.

In shareholder meetings, these top owners **generally vote with management** on routine matters like director elections and auditor ratification. For example, say-on-pay executive compensation votes have consistently passed with over 94% support in recent years, indicating backing from Vanguard, BlackRock, etc. However, on *shareholder proposals*, voting records show more nuance. **Vanguard and BlackRock have often opposed environmental or social proposals**, especially if deemed too prescriptive. In 2022, Vanguard reported supporting **0% of environmental/social proposals** that year, reflecting a belief that Microsoft's own commitments were sufficient. **BlackRock** similarly tends to give management the benefit of the doubt - e.g. BlackRock supported management on all "Say on Climate" votes in 2022. That said, both funds *did* support a landmark proposal in 2021 for a **report on sexual harassment transparency**, which passed with 77.9% support. This indicates that when an issue is clearly tied to shareholder value and governance (in this case, addressing workplace culture and risk after incidents involving Bill Gates), top shareholders will break from management's initial opposition and vote in favor.

**Top Voted Topics:** Recent Microsoft proxy seasons have seen proposals on topics like transparency in workplace culture, climate change, and AI ethics:

-   *Workplace Sexual Harassment Report (2021):* **Received 78% support** - an unusually high vote **against management's recommendation**. This proposal, filed by Arjuna Capital, **compelled Microsoft to publish an annual report on the effectiveness of its sexual harassment policies**, including how cases (like the one involving Bill Gates) were handled. It passed with broad support from institutional investors; even management conceded and later implemented the reporting. Vanguard and BlackRock **voted FOR** this proposal, signaling alignment on material governance issues.

-   *Median Gender/Racial Pay Gap Report (2021):* **Received \~40% support** (failed) . Top holders mostly followed management's stance that Microsoft's existing diversity disclosures were adequate (hence the proposal did not pass).

-   *Climate "Say-on-Climate" and 401(k) Fund Climate Risk (2022):* Proposals asking Microsoft to strengthen climate risk disclosure or alter retirement plan options got **11%-18% support** - below majority but enough for resubmission thresholds. For instance, a 2022 resolution to report on the climate impact of employee 401(k) investments got 11.2%. **BlackRock and State Street** typically vote with management on these, arguing Microsoft already leads in ESG (carbon negative pledge, etc.), whereas some smaller active managers supported the proposals.

-   *Human Rights and AI Ethics (2023-2024):* Several proposals emerged around Microsoft's role in AI and military contracts. In 2024, a proposal to report on **"AI data sourcing and misinformation risks"** got **18-36% support**(the highest 36.2% was for a report on AI data sourcing accountability) - a significant minority. This indicates many institutional investors are concerned about ethical risks of AI partnerships (Microsoft's stake in OpenAI, etc.) even though management opposed these resolutions. **State Street** was one notable supporter of at least one AI-related proposal, according to stewardship reports, while Vanguard and BlackRock largely sided with management (consistent with their general approach to oppose most E&S proposals in 2024).

In summary, **shareholder votes at Microsoft have overwhelmingly backed management**, with a few high-profile exceptions. The top institutional shareholders tend to vote *pro-management* on issues unless a proposal highlights a clear risk or inadequacy. The rare proposal that passed - the 2021 harassment report - demonstrated convergence of investor concern on a governance issue that could impact value. Each top shareholder has its own voting policies: for example, BlackRock publishes voting bulletins and has supported some diversity proposals at other firms, but at Microsoft they felt management was responsive enough. Vanguard often errs on management's side, as seen by opposing most climate measures. **On the whole, topics like AI ethics and climate change have so far not won majority support** at Microsoft, as top holders trust the board's oversight; whereas topics related to corporate culture and accountability (harassment, potentially also data privacy in future) can gain significant traction with Microsoft's shareholder base.

## **3. Historical Financial Metrics (30-Year Overview)**

Microsoft's financial history over the past 30 years reflects its evolution from a PC software vendor to a dominant cloud and enterprise services company. Key valuation, cash flow, and balance sheet metrics are highlighted below:

-   **Market Price & Market Cap:** Microsoft's stock price has risen dramatically since the 1990s. It went from around **$3.50 in 1995 to over $420 by end of 2024**, and the market capitalization grew from \~$30 B to about $3.15 T. The stock saw **two major surges**: during the late-1990s dot-com boom and in the 2010s cloud boom. After peaking in 1999, the price stagnated for over a decade ("lost decade" for MSFT) before rising exponentially under CEO Nadella (2014 onwards).
    > *Microsoft's year-end stock price, 1995-2024. The late-90s bubble and early-2000s crash are visible, followed by a flat 2000s and a steep rise in the 2015-2021 cloud era.*

-   **Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio:** Microsoft's P/E has fluctuated with market sentiment. In 1999-2000 it soared above 40x as earnings climbed and investors paid a premium. During the 2000-2010 period, P/E compressed into the low-teens (even single digits around 2011) as the stock languished despite growing earnings. More recently, massive earnings growth outpaced the stock in 2022 (P/E \~24x) but the multiple expanded again to \~35x in 2023 amid AI enthusiasm. Microsoft's **trailing P/E was \~33-37x at end of 2024**, relatively high historically, reflecting optimism about future growth.
    > *Microsoft's P/E ratio history. Note the **dot-com era high (\~60x)** and the **2010s low (\~8x)** when the market underestimated Microsoft, followed by re-rating to \~30-40x in recent years.*

-   **Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio:** Microsoft's P/B also tells a story. In the late 90s, P/B spiked (stock far above accounting book value) - P/B was \~9× by 1999. After the crash, Microsoft's huge cash returns (special dividend, buybacks) actually **shrank its book value** by the late 2000s, causing P/B to dip to \~2-4×. Around 2017, despite rising equity, the stock's surge pushed P/B to \~15×. At FY2024, with $268.5 B in equity and a $2.7 T market cap, P/B stands around **10-11×** - high, but off its peak when equity was smaller. (Microsoft's large **intangible assets and cash hoards** mean book value is not a full measure of intrinsic value.)
    > *Microsoft's Price-to-Book ratio. The **2021 peak (\~17× P/B)** coincided with exuberant market valuations, whereas during 2009-2011 the stock traded near **3-4× book** (reflecting value skepticism of that era).*

-   **Earnings per Share (EPS):** Microsoft's EPS grew from barely **$0.11 in 1995 to \~$11.80 in 2024**. There were dips in EPS in certain years - notably 2012 (EPS fell \~36% due to a $6.2 B goodwill impairment for aQuantive) and 2015 (EPS dropped \~60% due to the Nokia phone business write-off). But overall, EPS compounded at an impressive rate, especially after 2017 as both net income surged and share count was reduced by buybacks. For example, net income more than **quadrupled from $16.6 B in 2018 to $72.7 B in 2022**, driving EPS from \~$2.13 to $9.65. This earnings momentum - fueled by cloud and subscription revenue - is a core reason for Microsoft's stock re-rating in the last decade.
    > *Diluted EPS (annual) from 1995-2024. Observe the steady climb, punctuated by **sharp drops in 2012 and 2015**(one-time charges) and a **steep ramp-up post-2017** as cloud profits kicked in.*

-   **Free Cash Flow (FCF):** Microsoft has been a **cash machine**, converting a high portion of its earnings to free cash flow due to its software economics. FCF grew from just \~$1-2 B/year in the mid-90s to **over $60 B in 2024**. Notably, FCF outpaced net income in many years (e.g. in the early 2000s, due to deferred revenue from multiyear software agreements). By 2020s, Microsoft's annual operating cash flow was around $90-100 B and after capital expenditures (which rose to $30+ B for data centers), FCF was \~$60-70 B. This **FCF allowed massive capital returns** - e.g., over the last decade Microsoft increased its dividend every year and bought back tens of billions in stock (in FY2023 alone, $23 B in buybacks). The trend shows healthy growth, especially accelerating with cloud services (which, while capex-intensive, still yield strong cash flows).
    > *Microsoft's Free Cash Flow (in USD billions). The late 2010s show **FCF inflection** - rising cloud revenue yielded higher cash flows even after reinvestment. Microsoft's FCF in 2024 (\~$63 B) is roughly **13× larger than in 2004**, a testament to its scalable businesses.*

-   **Return on Assets/Equity/Invested Capital:** Microsoft's **Return on Equity (ROE)** has often been extraordinary - frequently 30%+. In FY2022, ROE was \~44% (net income $72.7 B on equity $166.5 B) . Such high ROE reflects Microsoft's high-margin software model and efficient capital structure. Return on Assets (ROA) in recent years hovers \~15%, and **Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)** (excluding excess cash) is even higher - indicating strong economic moats.

-   **Debt Levels:** Microsoft was **debt-free for much of its early history**. It first issued significant debt in 2009 (taking advantage of low rates to fund share buybacks) - $3.75 B then . Debt climbed with major acquisitions: e.g., long-term debt jumped to $76 B in 2017 after the LinkedIn deal . However, Microsoft's balance sheet remained net cash positive (it held over $130 B in cash and investments at times). By 2024, long-term debt was **$42.7 B** , while total cash was about $111 B - effectively *no net debt*.

-   **Debt Ratios:** Microsoft's **Debt-to-Equity (D/E) ratio** peaked around 0.87 in 2017-2018 (when debt \~ $76 B vs equity \~$88 B) . Since then, equity growth and debt reduction brought D/E back down to a modest 0.16 by 2024. The **Debt-to-Asset (D/A) ratio** likewise hit \~30% in 2017 and fell to \~8% in 2024 as liabilities became a smaller portion of the expanding asset base. Essentially, Microsoft carries **very low leverage** for a company of its size - a financial fortress.
    > *Debt-to-Equity ratio (long-term debt divided by total equity). Microsoft had virtually **no debt until 2009**, then a spike with the LinkedIn purchase, now declining leverage again as equity soars.
    > Debt-to-Asset ratio (long-term debt as % of total assets). This similarly peaked around **2017 (30%)** and is back to single digits. Microsoft's strong asset growth (from \~$250 B to $512 B in assets from 2017-2024) has outpaced its debt .*

-   **Shareholder's Equity:** Equity grew in the 1990s (retained profits) but saw ups and downs in the 2000s due to massive capital returns. Microsoft paid a **$32 B special dividend in 2004** and began large share buybacks in 2006, which reduced equity. By June 2009, total equity was only $39.6 B (down from \~$70 B in 2003). Since then, retained earnings from cloud-driven profits have rebuilt the equity base to **$268.5 B in 2024** . Notably, in 2021-2023 alone, equity jumped by over $100 B as record earnings outpaced dividends/buybacks .
    > *Shareholders' Equity (USD billions). The dip in mid-2000s reflects payouts outstripping earnings (special dividend & buybacks). In the last decade, **booming profits swelled equity** despite continued buybacks (which is a sign of just how profitable Microsoft became).*

-   **Revenue and Margins:** Microsoft's annual **revenue climbed from \~$5.9 B in 1995 to $245 B in 2024** . Key inflection points: the late 90s (Windows 95 and Office adoption) saw \~30% YoY growth; the mid-2000s growth slowed (PC market saturation); the late 2010s saw reacceleration with cloud. **Net profit margins** have been consistently high - typically **25-35%** in many years. Exceptional cases were FY1999-2000 (net margin \~39-41%, boosted by low expense relative to Windows/Office sales) and FY2018 (margin dropped to 15% due to the one-time tax charge). By FY2022-2024, Microsoft's net margin was back in the mid-30s - *among the highest of any large enterprise*, underscoring its pricing power and operational efficiency.
    > *Net income margin (% of revenue) each year. Microsoft's profitability is resilient - even during investment cycles or economic dips, it stayed solidly in the 20%+ range, except for a tax-induced trough in 2018 .*

## **5. Valuation (Discounted Cash Flow Analysis)**

Finally, a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) valuation** provides an estimate of Microsoft's intrinsic stock value based on its future cash flows:

-   **Free Cash Flow Base:** Microsoft's free cash flow in FY2024 was approximately $65 B (operating cash flow $95+ B minus capex \~$32 B). For DCF, we start around **$65 B FCF**.

-   **Growth Assumptions:** Despite its size, Microsoft is still growing. Analysts project high-single-digit revenue growth in the near term, fueled by Azure and AI services. We assume **FCF grows 8% annually for the next 5 years**, then moderates to \~4% for the following 5 years, and \~3% terminally (roughly in line with global GDP/inflation). This reflects continued cloud and subscription momentum, gradually slowing as Microsoft further matures.

-   **Discount Rate:** Using a 8%-9% discount rate (blended cost of capital - low interest rates and AAA credit rating keep Microsoft's cost of capital modest), we discount the projected cash flows and terminal value back to present.

-   **Result:** The present value of projected cash flows suggests an **intrinsic market cap in the range of $2.2-2.5 trillion**. This equates to an **intrinsic share price of roughly $290-$330**. (For perspective, this aligns with Buffett's simpler owner-earnings approach which yielded around $300/share as noted above, and is in line with some analyst models that put fair value around $300.)

Comparing to the current market price (around $370-$400 in April 2025), the DCF indicates Microsoft's stock is **trading above its intrinsic value** - in other words, the market is pricing in a lot of optimism. The implied **margin of safety is negative** (the stock is \~15-25% higher than the midpoint intrinsic value estimate). That said, one could argue our assumptions are conservative; if Microsoft sustains double-digit FCF growth for a decade (via AI breakthroughs or further margin expansion), the intrinsic value would be higher. Microsoft's own immense cash generation and fortress balance sheet also justify a lower discount rate for some investors, which would increase the DCF valuation.

In summary, DCF analysis suggests Microsoft is **near the upper end of a reasonable valuation range**. Long-term investors are betting that Microsoft's entrenched position in enterprise software and cloud will produce enough growth to "grow into" the current valuation. Given Microsoft's track record of innovation (cloud, AI) and disciplined capital return (over $38 B returned to shareholders in FY2023 via dividends and buybacks), many investors are comfortable accepting a lower immediate yield for the promise of durable future cash flows. Nonetheless, by classic valuation metrics, Microsoft appears only *fairly valued or slightly overvalued*, not a bargain - consistent with its status as a widely admired, blue-chip company.
