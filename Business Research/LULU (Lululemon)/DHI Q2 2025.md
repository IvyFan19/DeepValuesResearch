## **Business Model**

**D.R. Horton's Core Business**: D.R. Horton is the largest homebuilder in the U.S., primarily building and selling single-family homes under multiple brands (D.R. Horton, Emerald for luxury, Express for entry-level, Freedom for active adult) . Homebuilding - the construction and sale of homes and lots - is by far the primary revenue source, accounting for over 90% of revenue . In FY2024, homebuilding contributed **92%** of consolidated revenue . The company also has smaller segments: a **financial services** arm (DHI Mortgage and title services) providing financing to \~70-80% of its homebuyers ; a majority-owned **Forestar** subsidiary engaged in land development (selling lots to D.R. Horton and others); and a growing **rental housing** portfolio (developing single-family rental communities). These ancillary businesses are relatively minor - e.g. in FY2024, rental operations were \~4-5% of revenues, Forestar \~4%, and financial services \~2% . No single buyer accounts for a significant share of sales due to the retail nature - it's a very diversified customer base by default. Horton's use of **DHI Mortgage** to finance \~76% of its buyers in 2023 helps ensure customers can obtain loans and close on purchases . This integration smooths the customer experience (and brings Horton fee income). Horton's ability to deliver affordable homes is key - in 2023, it reported that **"affordable product offerings"** helped it capture demand even as interest rates rose .

## **Headwinds & Risks**

**Short-Term Headwinds (1-2 years)**: The primary short-term challenge is the **high mortgage rate environment** and related dip in housing affordability. As the Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply to combat inflation, 30-year mortgage rates climbed above 7%, pricing out many buyers . Horton notes that demand **"moderated"** in late 2022 and early 2023 as rates spiked and consumer sentiment became cautious . In response, builders (including Horton) increased buyer incentives - e.g. temporary rate buydowns, closing cost assistance, and price discounts - to spur sales . These incentives, while necessary to maintain volume, are a short-term drag on margins and profits . Another short-term risk is a potential **economic downturn or recession**. If the Fed's rate hikes slow the economy or raise unemployment, housing demand could weaken further in the near term . Supply-chain disruptions and building cost inflation (lumber, materials, labor) were headwinds in 2021-2022; those pressures have eased somewhat, but any resurgence could squeeze margins temporarily. Horton is also dealing with **cost inflation** (materials and labor rose \~5-10% in 2022), though in 2023 costs began stabilizing . On the political front, there are short-term risks like the potential for changes in government programs (e.g. FHA loan limits, mortgage interest tax deductions) that could temporarily affect buyer ability or incentives . Overall, the next year or two remain challenging due to **affordability constraints**, cautious consumer sentiment, and the possibility of an economic slowdown.

**Long-Term Risks (Permanent or Structural)**: One structural concern is the **interest rate regime** - if the era of ultra-low rates is over and we sustain higher mortgage rates long-term, homeownership may be permanently less affordable for marginal buyers. Horton acknowledges that **"prolonged periods of elevated mortgage rates"** would adversely impact its business . Another long-term factor is **demographics**. Favorably, the U.S. has a large cohort of millennials now entering prime homebuying age, which supports housing demand. However, if birth rates remain low, future generations of first-time buyers could shrink. Additionally, **geographic shifts** (e.g. out-migration from high-cost states to Horton's Sunbelt markets) could alter regional demand - Horton's broad footprint gives it flexibility here . Regulatory and environmental trends pose risks too. Increasing **environmental regulations** on land development or building codes (for energy efficiency, etc.) could raise costs permanently . "No-growth" local zoning initiatives or lengthy permitting processes can restrict lot supply in some markets . Climate change may also introduce new building requirements (for resilience) or render some land (coastal, wildfire-prone) less viable for development long-term. Another long-term risk is the **cyclicality of housing** - historically, housing booms are followed by overbuilding and painful corrections. Horton tries to mitigate this with its optioned-land strategy (to avoid heavy land carrying costs), but an extreme downturn (like 2008's crash) is an ever-present risk in this industry. Finally, secular shifts in consumer preferences - for instance, if future generations delay homeownership or prefer renting indefinitely - could reduce structural demand for single-family homes. However, current evidence (undersupply of homes, strong desire for ownership among millennials) suggests the **long-term outlook for housing remains solid**, with analysts noting the U.S. is *underbuilt* relative to demographics . In sum, while cyclical swings are inevitable, Horton's biggest permanent risks would be anything that severely erodes housing affordability or fundamentally changes the American homeownership model.

## **Cost Structure**

D.R. Horton's cost structure is dominated by the **direct costs of home construction** - land, labor, materials, and subcontracted services - which are accounted in "Cost of Sales" on the income statement. In 2023, Horton's cost of home sales was about 77-78% of homebuilding revenue, leaving a gross margin around 22-23% . These direct costs are largely **variable costs**: Horton generally acquires land (or lot options) for each community, then incurs material and subcontract labor costs as it builds each house . Major variable cost components include: **land acquisition and development**(typically \~20-25% of home sale price), **construction materials** (lumber, concrete, drywall, etc., \~30-35%), **labor/subcontractors** (framing crews, electricians, etc., \~25-30%), and **interest expense** which Horton capitalizes into inventory during construction .

Horton's **fixed costs** are relatively low for its size, mainly corporate overhead and regional management. As of September 2023, the company had 13,450 employees , including \~9,190 in homebuilding operations (3,782 in construction supervision, 2,352 in sales, 3,056 office staff) and \~2,895 in financial services, with the rest at corporate, rental, and Forestar . Payroll for these staff and related administrative costs are part of **SG&A (Selling, General & Administrative) expense**, which was $2.24 billion in 2023 (about 7.1% of revenues) . SG&A includes semi-fixed costs like management salaries, sales office expenses, model home upkeep, and advertising. Notably, Horton's **advertising**expense was only $64.7 million in 2023 - a small fraction of revenue (0.18%), as the company relies more on website listings, local signage, and brokers than heavy ad campaigns . Another semi-fixed cost is **warranty and customer service**for completed homes (Horton provides a 10-year structural warranty), which is built into overhead and cost of sales estimates .

**Cost per Home**: In 2023, the average selling price of a Horton home was \~$377k , and the average cost of construction and land was roughly $290k (yielding \~23% gross margin). This varies by market - e.g. Horton's average cost per home might be higher in high-cost states and lower in the South Central region. Land cost as a percent of selling price can range from \~20% in low-cost areas up to 30% in land-constrained markets. Vertical construction cost (materials & subcontracted labor) is the other major chunk; Horton benefits from national procurement (volume discounts on lumber, appliances, etc.) to keep unit costs down.

**Significant Costs & Trends**: The largest cost buckets - land and building costs - tend to move with the housing cycle. During boom periods (e.g. 2021), raw material prices and land values climbed rapidly, pressuring builders' margins. For example, surging lumber and material costs in 2021-2022 contributed to Horton's average home cost rising faster than selling prices, compressing gross margin in 2023 by \~820 basis points . In lean times, Horton can negotiate better prices from suppliers/subs or purchase land at lower prices (often via Forestar or option contracts), which can improve margins when demand recovers. Labor cost is another key factor - Horton noted labor shortages in some markets during the 2018-2021 upcycle, which drove wages up. Now, with the housing market cooler, trade labor availability has improved, tempering labor cost inflation. **SG&A costs** are managed carefully relative to volume - Horton's SG&A as a % of revenue was 6.8-7.1% during 2022-2023 . It has a relatively lean overhead structure; during downturns, it has historically cut SG&A (through headcount and salary reductions or closing divisions) to preserve profitability. For instance, in the 2008-2011 downturn, Horton significantly reduced land spend and overhead, allowing it to remain mostly profitable. Another cost, **interest**, has been rising as rates increase - Horton's interest incurred was $151 million in 2023 (though all was capitalized to inventory since active construction exceeded debt) . If Horton's home inventory ever drops below debt levels, interest not capitalized would hit the P&L, but at present interest expense in earnings is minimal.

Overall, **land and construction are the most significant costs**, comprising around 80% of home sales revenue . These costs are variable and move with volume and market conditions. In strong housing markets, Horton's higher volumes drive operating leverage (spreading fixed SG&A over more homes) and often better subcontractor productivity, expanding margins. In weak markets, it may face inventory impairments or need to offer price incentives that effectively raise cost of sales as a percentage of revenue. The company's long-term cost trend has been positive: gross margin averaged \~25-28% in the past decade, dipping to \~22-23% in 2023 amid heavy incentives , but management expects margins to normalize as conditions stabilize. The ability to adjust construction starts quickly and the use of optioned land (65%+ of lots controlled via options) gives Horton flexibility to manage costs in different economic conditions - it can slow land development and home starts to avoid accumulating high-cost inventory when demand softens . In summary, Horton's cost structure is largely variable and scalable, with land & construction costs dominating and relatively modest fixed overhead, which helped the company stay agile and profitable through cycles.

## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

We now evaluate D.R. Horton against Warren Buffett's criteria for a "great company" and value investment, using the historical metrics above:

**1. Valuation Discipline:** Buffett typically likes companies with low valuations relative to earnings and assets. D.R. Horton currently trades around 10-12× earnings (P/E) - **below Buffett's P/E ≤ 15 benchmark** . In fact, DHI's average P/E over the past 10 years is \~11 (ranged \~5-17). It was as low as 5.3× in 2022's trough when earnings peaked and stock lagged - a very cheap valuation. The P/B ratio is about **2.0**, above the classic ≤1.5 hurdle, but that doesn't fully account for Horton's strong ROE. Also, Horton's P/B was ≈1.5 or lower during parts of 2018 and 2022 , meeting that criterion then. Regarding **Margin of Safety**, Buffett seeks buying at ≤75% of intrinsic value. Horton's intrinsic value (using a DCF or justified P/E approach) appears well above the current price in nominal and optimistic cases (see DCF analysis below), implying a &gt;25% margin of safety was present, especially in 2022-2023 when the stock was under $100 (Buffett indeed bought around $110 ). Thus, on valuation, D.R. Horton largely **passes** - it has often traded at a single-digit P/E (due to cyclical fears), providing a margin of safety for value investors .

**2. Cash Generation & Allocation:** A great company must generate consistent free cash flow (FCF) and use retained earnings effectively. D.R. Horton's **Free Cash Flow** can be choppy year-to-year due to working capital swings (it invests in land and home inventory heavily during growth spurts). For example, FCF was slightly positive in 2015-2019, then dipped near breakeven in 2021-2022 as the company built inventory (only $0.56B CFO in 2022) , then surged to $4.3B in 2023 when it harvested that inventory . Over the full cycle, however, Horton did produce positive FCF each year - it was **positive all 10 years**, albeit small in some (e.g. 2021 CFO $0.53B ). On average, FCF/Revenue was &gt;5% in most years, except when intentionally growing inventory (2021-22 had FCF margin \~2%). So it *mostly passes* the FCF consistency test, acknowledging the cyclicality. Importantly, Horton has **allocated capital prudently**. It retained earnings to fuel expansion during high-growth periods (no dividend from 2008-2013, low payout since), then returned cash aggressively when flush (initiated dividend in 2014, and repurchased \~12% of shares since 2017). We can check **Return on Retained Earnings**: from 2014 to 2024, Horton retained roughly $20 billion cumulative and net income increased \~$4.2 billion, implying \~21% return on those retained earnings (this is a rough gauge). Even a shorter horizon: between 2018 and 2023, the company retained \~$5.5B (net of dividends) and net income rose \~$3.3B - a very high incremental return. So it **exceeds the ≥12% return on retained earnings** threshold. In Buffett's terms, management has turned each dollar plowed back into &gt;$0.20 of incremental annual earnings, a strong performance.

**3. Growth & Predictability:** Buffett likes steady, upward earnings with no losses. D.R. Horton's **EPS record** has been positive every year for the past decade and generally rising (EPS grew from $1.50 in 2014 to $14.34 in 2024) . There was a slight EPS dip in 2023 (down 16% from 2022's peak) , breaking the streak of continuous increases, but EPS remained far above pre-2020 levels. Over 10 years, EPS CAGR is \~25%. Thus, Horton nearly achieves "rising every year for 10 years" - nine up years and one modest down - still a **strong pass** on earnings growth overall. **Book value per share**(shareholders' equity per share) has compounded at \~15% annually, from $15.99 in 2015 to $77.78 in 2024, with **no declines in any year** (even during 2023's profit dip, book value per share rose 20% thanks to buybacks and retained earnings ). That comfortably meets Buffett's test of 10% BVPS CAGR and no contraction. **Owner's Earnings** (net income + depreciation - maintenance capex) has been positive each year. Essentially net income itself serves as a proxy since capex is minor for a builder (Horton's depreciation was only \~$78M in 2024 ). Owner's earnings yield (OE/Market Cap) is currently around 8% ($4B/$50B), which is slightly below the ≥10% Buffett likes. However, at 2022's low share price it was \~15% - so an investor buying during market pessimism would have gotten &gt;10% owner earnings yield (indeed one reason Buffett likely bought then). On consistency, owner's earnings have grown over time with net income, though as noted, reported FCF swings with inventory cycles. Overall, Horton shows **strong growth and relatively predictable performance for a cyclical business**, with only a mild earnings dip in a tough 2023 - a resilient result that suggests it possesses some durable advantages.

**4. Balance-Sheet Safety:** Buffett avoids highly leveraged companies. D.R. Horton's balance sheet is very solid. **Debt-to-Equity** is \~0.4 (or Debt/Asset \~28%), well below the 0.5 threshold . Horton carries about $5.9B long-term debt vs $25.8B equity in 2024 . Its **interest coverage** is enormous because EBIT was $5.9B in 2024 while interest incurred was \~$150M (if it weren't capitalized) - over 35× coverage. Using the given standard, **Interest Exp/Op Income** was effectively \~0% in 2023-24 (since interest was fully capitalized) . Even if we include all interest, $150M is only 2.5% of $5.936B op income in 2024, easily passing the ≤15% test. Horton's **Current Ratio** is exceptionally high at \~7.3× in 2024 , far above the &gt;1.5 benchmark - the company maintains plenty of liquidity (over $7B cash on hand and a large revolver available). Horton deliberately keeps a low debt profile; its debt-to-capital was \~18% in 2023 , and it has no significant near-term maturities (next senior note due 2026). This conservative balance sheet contributed to its investment-grade credit rating. Buffett would likely approve of Horton's financial fortress: it could pay all short-term liabilities 7× over, and long-term debt is modest relative to assets and equity. The **one caveat**: much of Horton's "assets" are inventories (land and homes), which in a severe downturn can lose value, but Horton has a strong history of managing inventory risk (e.g. using option contracts to avoid over-leveraging on land ). In sum, Horton **passes all balance sheet safety checks** with flying colors.

**5. Profitability & Efficiency:** Buffett favors companies with high margins and returns on capital. D.R. Horton's margins are good for its industry, though not as high as some elite non-cyclical businesses. Its **Gross Margin** was \~25.9% in 2024 - below the &gt;40% Buffett ideal (builders are asset-heavy, lower-margin businesses). Even at peak, Horton's gross margin \~31% (2022) didn't approach 40% . So by that strict &gt;40% yardstick, Horton **does not pass gross margin** - but it's the nature of the industry; importantly, Horton's gross margin is among the highest in the homebuilding sector (industry median \~22% ), indicating it has some competitive advantage in cost or pricing. **Net Profit Margin** reached \~17% in 2022, but in normal times is \~12-13% , below the suggested 20%. Thus, by that &gt;20% net margin rule, Horton **falls short**. However, its net margin is consistently higher than most peers (e.g. Lennar's was 14% in 2022). Where Horton shines is **Return on Equity**, averaging \~22% the past 5 years and \~18% over 10 years . Buffett looks for ≥15% ROE long-term, and Horton clearly achieves that (10-year avg \~18%, and it was &gt;30% in the boom) . This indicates efficient use of capital. Similarly, **ROIC** (return on invested capital) for Horton has been strong recently: \~32% in 2022 and \~19-22% in 2023-24 , well above Buffett's \~12% hurdle. Even adjusting for cycles, Horton's ROIC has trended upward - it was \~12-15% in mid-2010s , rising to \~25-30% in late 2010s and 2020s. This suggests Horton has built some durable efficiencies at scale (perhaps volume purchasing power and low SG&A). **ROA** is slightly below Buffett's 6% target in some years (e.g. 2015-2016 \~5.6-7.8% ) but has been well above 6% since 2020 (13.7% in 2024 ). Horton's ROA is acceptable given homebuilding's asset intensity and easily beats peers. Lastly, **Depreciation/Gross Profit**: Buffett prefers companies with low capital intensity. Horton's depreciation is minimal - only \~$79M vs $9.53B gross profit in 2024 , i.e. \~0.8%. Even including amortization of intangibles, it's under 1% of gross profit - far below the ≤10% threshold. This indicates that Horton's earnings are real cash earnings and not being consumed by heavy maintenance capex - a *positive*for the business quality (most assets are inventory that turns over, not fixed plant that depreciates).

To summarize, D.R. Horton meets many of Buffett's quality criteria: **consistent earnings growth**, high returns on equity/capital, strong free cash flow conversion over time, shareholder-friendly management, and a solid balance sheet. It falls short on the **margin criteria** (gross and net margins are below Buffett's ideal) - but this is typical for a homebuilder, which is a more commodity-like business than Buffett's favorite consumer brands or tech firms. Despite lower margins, Horton's **efficiency (ROE/ROIC)** is high, suggesting it compensates with fast asset turnover and prudent leverage. Buffett did invest in Horton (albeit briefly), indicating he found enough to like, likely the valuation, dominance in its industry, and financial strength outweighed concerns about cyclicality. In conclusion, by Buffett's checklist D.R. Horton **scores well** on valuation, capital stewardship, growth, and balance sheet; it only modestly lags on profit margins, which are inherent to its industry but mitigated by superior scale and execution. Thus, Horton can be considered a great company in a cyclical sector - with metrics that, for the most part, **pass Buffett's standards** for quality and value.

## **DCF Analysis (Conservative, Nominal, Optimistic)**

To estimate D.R. Horton's intrinsic value, we perform a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis under three scenarios - **Conservative**, **Nominal**, and **Optimistic** - based on varying free cash flow growth assumptions. We use the company's recent free cash flow (FCF) as a starting point and growth rates informed by its past 10-year FCF history (which has been volatile but strongly upward overall):

-   **FCF Baseline**: We take $4.3 billion as the current annual free cash flow (approximately DHI's operating cash flow in FY2023 minus minimal capex). This roughly equates to recent net income (\~$4.76B in 2024 , noting Horton's net income and cash flow converge over time since inventory changes net out).

-   **Growth Rates**: Over the last decade, D.R. Horton's FCF growth has ranged from negative in some years to extremely high in others (e.g. +666% from 2022 to 2023 when cash flow rebounded post-inventory build ). To ground assumptions:

    -   *Conservative*: We use **0% FCF growth**, assuming earnings and cash flow simply flatline at current levels forever. This captures a scenario of no growth - perhaps if high rates permanently cap housing demand.

    -   *Nominal/Base*: We use **+10% annual FCF growth for 10 years**, reflecting a moderate average growth. Horton's net income grew \~15% CAGR in the past 10 years, and revenue \~14% . However, to be cautious (given cycles), we choose 10%, which is the low end of its historical range (and assumes slower growth as the company is larger now).

    -   *Optimistic*: We assume **+20% annual FCF growth for 10 years**. This is closer to Horton's exceptional growth phase (EPS grew \~25% CAGR last decade). 20% is aggressive but within reason if conditions turn very favorable (e.g. sustained undersupply driving volume and pricing). We use this as a best-case to see upside.

-   **Terminal Value**: After 10 years, we assume FCF growth flattens to 0% (no growth perpetuity). This is conservative (Buffett might instead use a terminal multiple). We choose a **10% discount rate** (typical for equity - implies about a 7% equity risk premium over risk-free rates). This is aligned with Buffett's hurdle (he often uses \~10% as a discount rate for valuation).

Using these inputs, we calculate the present value of 10 years of cash flows plus a terminal value at year 10 (FCF\_Year10 ÷ 10% discount rate, discounted back).

**Conservative Case (0% growth)**:

FCF stays at \~$4.3B each year. The present value of a flat $4.3B annuity for 10 years at 10% is $26.5B. Terminal value (year 10 FCF $4.3B / 0.10 = $43.0B) discounted back is $16.6B. Sum = **$43.1 billion intrinsic equity value**. With 305M shares, that is about **$141 per share**. Applying a 25% margin of safety, the **buy price** would be **≤$106**. *Result:* Even with zero growth assumed indefinitely, DHI's intrinsic value is around $140/share, slightly below the current market price ($164) - indicating the stock isn't a bargain in a no-growth scenario, but also not dramatically overpriced.

**Nominal Case (10% growth)**:

FCF grows to $11.2B by year 10. The 10-year cash flow PV sums to $27.0B. Terminal value = $11.2B/0.10 = $112B, PV = $43.2B. Total = **$70.2 billion** equity value, or \*\*$232 per share\*\*. With a 25% margin, a **buy price** would be **&lt;$174**. *Result:* Under reasonable growth (10% annually, which is slower than past performance), DHI's estimated intrinsic value is about $230/share. The current stock price ($164) is about 30% below this intrinsic value, suggesting a margin of safety exists. In fact, at $164 the market is roughly pricing in only \~8% growth (since that's the breakeven growth between 0% and 10% scenarios). This nominal case implies DHI is **undervalued** for a long-term investor expecting continued growth.

**Optimistic Case (20% growth)**:

FCF grows explosively, reaching $26.6B by year 10 (reflecting perhaps a prolonged housing boom). The PV of cash flows = $34.8B. Terminal value = $26.6B/0.10 = $266B, PV = $102.7B. Sum = **$137.5 billion**, or \*\*$451 per share\*\*. 25% margin buy price = **&lt;$338**. *Result:* If one believes D.R. Horton can sustain 20% growth for a decade (similar to its last 10-year EPS trajectory) and then level off, the stock's intrinsic value would be enormous - nearly 3× the current price. This highlights the **upside potential** if housing demand stays robust and Horton continues to dominate. Of course, 20% annual growth over 10 years is a lofty scenario (it would imply perhaps doubling home deliveries to \~180k/year and some price increases). But given Horton's past ability to double revenue roughly every 5 years in expansions, it isn't entirely outlandish. Still, a prudent investor might not assume this best-case, but it shows DHI's **intrinsic value could be much higher** if the optimistic story plays out.

These DCF figures should be taken with caution due to the cyclicality of housing. D.R. Horton's cash flows are not as smooth as a typical Buffett consumer stock - in downturns, FCF could drop significantly (even be negative if Horton chooses to reinvest heavily at a bottom). That said, using normalized or average cycle earnings in a DCF would likely yield a similar conclusion to our nominal case. At present, DHI's stock at \~$164 is **below our base-case intrinsic value ($232)**, suggesting 30-40% upside. It is above the no-growth value ($141), indicating the market is pricing in some growth but arguably not the full historical growth potential.

In sum, **Conservative DCF** (zero growth) shows limited downside (value \~$140 vs price $164, a mild overvaluation if growth truly stalled). **Nominal DCF** (10% growth) shows the stock is undervalued by about 25-30%. **Optimistic DCF**shows huge upside (the stock could triple in a very strong scenario). Therefore, under reasonable long-term assumptions, D.R. Horton appears to offer a **margin of safety** for investors - its strong cash generation and moderate valuation combine to make it an attractive DCF-based investment, as evidenced by Buffett's own interest in 2023. An investor demanding a 25% margin of safety would aim to buy DHI below \~$174 (nominal scenario) - the stock spent much of 2023 under that level, which is likely why astute investors were accumulating .
