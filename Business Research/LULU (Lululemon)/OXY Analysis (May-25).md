# **Occidental Petroleum (OXY) - Comprehensive Strategic & Financial Analysis**

## **Background Research**

### **Business Model and Revenue Streams**

Occidental Petroleum (OXY) is an integrated energy company operating through three main segments: **Oil & Gas**, **Chemical (OxyChem)**, and **Midstream & Marketing**. The Oil & Gas segment is OXY's core business - exploring for and producing crude oil, natural gas, and natural gas liquids primarily in the United States (notably the Permian Basin) and the Middle East. OXY's Chemical segment (OxyChem) manufactures polyvinyl chloride (PVC) plastics, chlorine, caustic soda and other basic chemicals, supplying industrial customers in the U.S., Canada, and Chile. The Midstream & Marketing segment provides infrastructure and logistics: it **gathers, processes, transports, and markets** hydrocarbons (including third-party oil and gas) and also manages OXY's **CO₂ carbon management business**. This integrated model allows OXY to capture value across the oil production chain - from drilling wells to processing and delivering products.

**Revenue Breakdown:** Oil & Gas is by far the largest revenue generator for OXY. In 2022, for example, roughly **74%** of Occidental's $36.6 billion revenue came from oil and gas production. The Chemical segment contributed around **18-20%**of revenues (about $6-7 billion in 2022), while Midstream & Marketing made up the remaining **6-8%** (≈$2-3 billion). **(See chart below.)** Oil & Gas revenues are driven by production volume and commodity prices; OXY's worldwide production averaged \~1.23 million barrels of oil equivalent per day (Mboe/d) in 2023, with reserves of 4.0 billion BOE proving its long-term output potential. The Chemical unit's sales depend on demand for PVC plastics, chlorine, and related products, and Midstream income comes from transport fees and trading margins. Importantly, OXY's customer base is mostly other businesses - crude oil and NGLs are sold to refiners and petrochemical firms, natural gas to utilities/markets, and chemicals to industrial clients - rather than individual consumers. This means **volume (barrels or tons sold)** and **market pricing** are key performance drivers, whereas "number of customers" is less relevant (OXY doesn't have millions of retail customers like a utility might). Instead, metrics like production volume, capacity utilization, and realized prices per barrel define its revenue.

### **Short- and Long-Term Risks**

Occidental faces a variety of **economic, political, and industry risks** that could impact its business both in the near term and over decades:

-   **Commodity Price Volatility:** As with any oil & gas producer, OXY's fortunes rise and fall with crude oil and natural gas prices. In the short run, global recessions or oversupply can **crash oil prices**, hurting revenues and profits (e.g. the 2020 oil price collapse drove OXY to a $15.7 billion net loss). Conversely, price spikes (like in 2022) boost cash flow dramatically. This inherent volatility is a major risk - OXY must budget and invest prudently to survive low-price cycles. To mitigate this, the company has pursued cost cuts and efficiency gains to lower its breakeven price. Notably, **OXY's operating breakevens are well below market prices**; many Permian wells can profit in the low-$30s per barrel range for existing output (though new wells require higher, sub-$60 prices). Still, prolonged low prices could force OXY to scale back drilling, impair assets, or increase leverage.

-   **High Debt and Financing Risk:** Occidental took on substantial debt for its 2019 Anadarko acquisition (a \~$38 billion deal) just before the 2020 crash. This left OXY with a heavy debt load and sub-investment-grade credit ratings (S&P **BB+**, Moody's **Ba2** in 2022). While OXY used the 2021-2022 upcycle to **pay down debt** (over $10.5 billion repaid in 2022 alone), it still had \~$18.5 billion in long-term debt at end of 2023. The company's ability to refinance or raise new debt depends on market conditions - rising interest rates or tighter credit could raise its financing costs. In the long run, if OXY cannot continue generating strong cash flow, **debt servicing could strain** the business. It's worth noting OXY also owes Berkshire Hathaway an $800 million annual preferred dividend (from Buffett's $10 billion 2019 preferred investment), until it redeems those preferred shares. The **priority management places on debt reduction** is evident: OXY set targets to retire more debt and achieved $4.5 billion in additional debt paydown by Q4 2024. Nonetheless, a highly leveraged balance sheet amplifies vulnerability to downturns (a key risk until leverage is further reduced and credit ratings upgraded).

-   **Regulatory and Political Risk:** OXY operates in multiple regions, each with political dynamics. In the U.S., federal policies on drilling (e.g. permitting on federal lands, environmental regulations) can impact OXY's operations in New Mexico (Permian) or the Gulf of Mexico. Internationally, OXY has legacy operations in the Middle East and Colombia - changes in fiscal regimes, taxes, or geopolitical instability could affect those assets. More broadly, **climate change policies** pose a long-term risk: as governments aim to reduce carbon emissions, regulations or carbon pricing could increase operating costs for oil producers or limit future development. OXY is attempting to turn this into an opportunity by investing in carbon capture and storage (its emerging "Oxy Low Carbon Ventures" business), but there's risk these projects face cost overruns or regulatory hurdles. In fact, Occidental touts itself as a future carbon management leader (it's building one of the first large direct air capture facilities), yet if climate regulations tighten faster than carbon capture scales up, OXY's core oil business could suffer. There's also **litigation and environmental liability risk** - being an oil producer and chemicals manufacturer, OXY can be liable for spills, accidents, or toxic exposure incidents (OXY has paid large clean-up settlements in the past, e.g. for Love Canal via its Hooker Chemical subsidiary). Stricter environmental rules or accidents could lead to costly fines or remediation expenses.

-   **Competition and Energy Transition:** In the long run, the shift toward renewable energy and electric vehicles is a structural risk for Occidental. Oil demand is expected to peak or plateau within the next few decades as global transportation electrifies. OXY will need to adapt by focusing on lowest-cost operations and possibly diversifying (its bet on carbon capture is partly to remain relevant in a low-carbon future). There is a risk that **long-term oil demand declines** or that OXY's oil reserves become "stranded assets" if global climate goals curtail fossil fuel use. In the interim, competition from other oil companies for acquisitions, talent, and investment is fierce - OXY must compete with larger majors (Exxon, Chevron) and agile independents in shale plays. The company's ability to keep a technological edge in shale drilling and to **replace reserves cheaply** (either via exploration or acquisitions) will determine its future. For instance, OXY's recent \~$12 billion acquisition of Permian producer *CrownRock L.P.* in 2024 expanded its reserves and output by \~170,000 BOE/d, solidifying its Permian position. While this boosts short-term growth, it also increased debt and integration risk. Any large acquisition poses the risk of overpayment or operational hiccups. OXY will need to execute well to realize synergies from CrownRock (e.g. economies of scale in the Permian). Failure to integrate new assets or achieve expected cost savings would be a risk that could erode shareholder value.

In summary, **short-term risks** center on commodity prices, debt management, and operational execution, whereas **long-term risks** include navigating the energy transition and managing geopolitical/climate policy changes. OXY's strategy (deleveraging, cost efficiency, and investing in carbon management) is aimed at mitigating these, but significant exposure remains.

### **Cost Structure and Expense Trends**

Occidental's cost structure includes both substantial **fixed costs** (capital investments, overhead, debt service) and **variable costs** linked to production volumes. Key cost components are:

-   **Lifting Costs (Production Operating Expenses):** These are the day-to-day costs to extract oil & gas (excluding depreciation). They include field labor, power/fuel, well maintenance, and production chemicals. OXY's lifting cost per barrel has been trending downward through efficiency gains. In 2023, operational efficiencies in the Permian Basin helped counter inflation, keeping per-barrel operating costs in check. OXY does not disclose a single figure publicly in each report, but Morningstar analysts noted OXY's *operating cost per BOE* was slightly above expectations in early 2025 but still competitive. For perspective, **existing Permian wells can produce profitably at costs as low as \~$30/boe**, while new shale wells might need &lt;$60/boe to break even. When oil prices are high (e.g. $70-80+), OXY's profit margins expand significantly since these variable costs remain much lower.

-   **Capital Expenditures (Fixed in short term):** OXY must continually drill new wells and invest in facilities to maintain and grow production. This **upstream capex** is relatively fixed in the short run - budgeted annually - and can be cut during downturns (at the expense of future output). In 2023 OXY spent about $4.5 billion on capital expenditures (and had guided $5.8-6.0 billion for 2024 before later trimming it). In Q1 2025, capex was $1.9 billion for the quarter. The majority of capex is directed to oil & gas development (over $3.7 billion earmarked to Permian projects in 2024). These costs are significant and relatively inflexible once rigs and crews are contracted; however, OXY can scale drilling up or down year-to-year based on cash flow. During high-price periods (2022), OXY boosted capex to increase production, whereas in lean times (2020) it slashed capex to preserve cash. **Depreciation & depletion** (a non-cash fixed cost) runs high due to these capital investments - OXY records substantial DD&A expenses each year, reflecting the consumption of its oil & gas reserves and plant assets.

-   **Labor, G&A, and Overhead:** Occidental had \~11,700 employees as of 2021, and the Anadarko acquisition plus recent growth likely puts current headcount in the \~12-14k range. Salaries, benefits, and corporate overhead (headquarters, IT, etc.) are semi-fixed costs. OXY has been working to streamline its organization; for example, post-Anadarko it targeted synergies and cost cuts by eliminating overlapping roles and offices. These **SG&A expenses** are much smaller than field operating or capex costs, but they are important for efficiency. During industry downturns OXY has implemented layoffs or pay freezes to reduce overhead. The management also noted a $150 million reduction in 2025 domestic operating costs via efficiency gains - indicating even field-level costs can be optimized through tech and scale. Overall, OXY's per-unit overhead has trended down as production volumes grew (spreading corporate costs across more barrels).

-   **Transportation & Marketing Costs:** Under the Midstream segment, Occidental incurs costs to transport its production (pipelines, trucking, shipping) and to operate processing plants. Some of these costs are fixed (pipeline tariffs, plant operating costs) and some vary with volumes. Often, these costs are offset by midstream *revenues*when OXY ships third-party volumes. Still, in periods of regional bottlenecks, OXY might have to spend more on transport (e.g. higher Permian takeaway costs). The company's integrated infrastructure, like its owned pipelines and export terminals, helps control these expenses.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** Unlike consumer-facing companies, OXY's advertising or marketing spend is minimal. It does not run large ad campaigns; marketing is mostly business development and commodity trading efforts. These costs are negligible in financial reports (often lumped in SG&A). One can consider the Midstream trading operations' *purchase of third-party oil/gas* as a "cost" of marketing - OXY might buy crude from other producers to blend/transport, then resell. However, those are accounted in revenue/cost of goods rather than "advertising." In summary, **direct marketing expenses are not a significant cost driver** for OXY.

**Major Cost Drivers and Trends:** The most significant costs for OXY are **capital costs (new wells) and operating expenses per barrel**. When oil prices plunge, OXY focuses on cutting variable costs and deferring capex. Notably, in the 2014-2016 oil crash and again in 2020, OXY sharply reduced drilling activity to save cash, which reduced its capex by over 50% in those downturns. Conversely, in boom times like 2022, OXY's costs *rose* in absolute terms (more drilling, some oilfield service inflation) but fell *per barrel* due to higher volumes and efficiency. **Economic cycles significantly affect input costs:** during high oil prices, service contractors raise rates (drilling rigs, frac crews become pricier), driving up OXY's cost per well. We saw this in 2022-23 as inflation in steel, labor, and services pushed industry costs \~10-20% higher. However, OXY's scale and procurement can blunt some of this impact (the company locked in long-term contracts and utilized its size to negotiate). In 2023-24, as industry activity cooled, OXY actually found cost relief and *reduced* its 2025 capex guidance by $200 million due to efficiency/schedule optimization.

Overall, **OXY's cost structure has become more resilient**. On a per-barrel basis, OXY has stated its corporate "cash flow breakeven" (covering opex, interest, and maintenance capex plus dividend) is around $40-45/barrel - very competitive in the industry. In 2022, OXY's robust free cash flow ($12.9 billion FCF) was enabled by controlling costs even as revenues surged. The company's continuous improvement efforts (digital oilfield tech, lean operations) aim to ensure profitability through the cycle. But investors should monitor cost trends: if inflation or operational issues cause unit costs to rise (or if OXY undertakes expensive projects like Direct Air Capture with no immediate revenue), margins could compress in a weak oil price environment. So far, trends show OXY improving efficiency (Q1 2025 production was delivered "at the mid-point of guidance" with operating cost reductions), highlighting management's focus on cost discipline.

## **Intrinsic Value Estimation - Buffett-Style DCF**

To gauge Occidental's **intrinsic value**, we perform a conservative Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis akin to a Warren Buffett "owner earnings" approach. We'll project OXY's **free cash flows** (cash available to shareholders after necessary capital spending) under two scenarios - one very conservative (low growth) and one moderate (historical average growth) - and apply a **margin of safety** to derive a prudent valuation. All figures are in USD.

**DCF Assumptions:** We use a **discount rate** of 10% (reflecting a desired long-term annual return, and roughly OXY's cost of equity) in our base case. We also test higher discount rates (up to 15%) to see implied returns. We apply a **terminal growth rate** of 2% (below expected GDP inflation, complying with the &lt;3% guideline for perpetuities). We incorporate Occidental's **shares outstanding (\~980 million)** to convert equity value to per-share. A margin of safety of 30-50% will be considered when comparing intrinsic value to the current market price (\~$41/share).

**Baseline Cash Flows:** Occidental's free cash flow is highly sensitive to oil prices, so rather than use a single recent year, we consider a normalized level. In 2022 (high oil prices), OXY's free cash flow was $12.9 billion, while in 2023 (lower prices) it was $6.5 billion. For a conservative take, we'll start near the lower end. OXY's trailing 12-month free cash flow (as of Q1 2025) is roughly \~$5-6 billion (Q1 2025 had $1.2B FCF before working capital; annualizing that gives \~$5B). Given oil's forward curve in the $70s, OXY could generate \~$6-7B FCF in a normalized year. To be safe, **we use $6.5 billion as Year 1 free cash flow**. This is effectively assuming an oil price in the mid-$70s (close to today's) and OXY continuing cost discipline and growth projects like CrownRock adding volume.

-   **Scenario 1: Minimal Growth ("Lowest growth rate in 10 years"):** Here we assume OXY's free cash flow **does not grow at all** over the next 5 years - i.e. stays at $6.5B annually. This is extremely conservative, essentially assuming any production gains are offset by price declines or cost increases (flat cash flow). After 5 years, we give a tiny terminal growth of 2% (to model perpetuity). This scenario could correspond to a world where oil prices stagnate or OXY's volumes plateau. Plugging these into a DCF:

    -   Present value of years 1-5 flat $6.5B FCF (discounted at 10%) = **$24.9 billion**.

    -   Terminal value in year 5 = $6.5B \* (1+2%) / (10% - 2%) = \~$83.7 billion, which discounted back = **$51.2 billion**.

    -   **Total intrinsic equity value = \~$76.1 billion**. Dividing by \~0.98B shares = **≈ $77.6 per share intrinsic value**.

-   Under this no-growth scenario, OXY's intrinsic value comes out to around **$75-80 per share**. This is almost **90% above** the current stock price. The large gap implies a significant margin of safety - in fact, at $41, the market is pricing in far worse than zero growth. We can also invert this: at $41/share (market cap \~$40.6B), what discount rate would equate the DCF to that price? Solving for the discount rate yields \~**17.1%**. In other words, the market-implied expected return is \~17% if OXY merely maintains $6.5B FCF with 2% terminal growth. That's a very high required return, suggesting the market is skeptical or risk-averse about OXY's future. For value investors, this scenario indicates *substantial upside* if OXY can indeed sustain its cash flows.

-   **Scenario 2: Moderate Growth (Historical average):** Over the past decade, OXY's revenue and cash flow have zigzagged, but if we exclude extremes, an average growth might be in the mid-single digits. Let's assume OXY can grow free cash flow at **5% annually for the next 5 years** (through a combination of slight volume increases, efficiency, and maybe higher realized prices), then settle to a 2% terminal growth. This might reflect OXY using its carbon capture business and new Permian assets to slowly expand cash generation. Calculating DCF:

    -   Year 1 FCF $6.5B grows to Year 5 FCF \~$7.9B (5% CAGR). The 5-year cash flow stream discounted = **$27.4 billion**.

    -   Terminal value at Year 5 = $7.9B \*1.02/(0.10-0.02) ≈ $101 billion; discounted back = **$66.6 billion**.

    -   **Total intrinsic equity value ≈ $94.0 billion**, which per share = **\~$96 per share**.

-   Under this more optimistic (but still reasonable) scenario, OXY's intrinsic value approaches **$95-100 per share**. That is more than double the current price, indicating enormous upside if OXY can achieve modest growth. The margin of safety here is huge - the stock trades at \~43% of this intrinsic value (or conversely, about a 55-57% discount). Even if we demanded a bigger margin (50%), half of \~$96 is \~$48, still above the current price. Thus even a 50% safety margin leaves room for stock appreciation.

Both scenarios suggest OXY is undervalued at present. For further prudence, consider a **high discount rate** of 15% (reflecting a high required return or risk premium). If we discount scenario 1 (no growth) at 15%, the per-share value drops to the low-$50s. If we discount scenario 2 at 15%, intrinsic value might be around $65-70. These are closer to current prices but still above. This implies the market may be implicitly using a very high discount or expecting declines in cash flow (perhaps fearing a future oil price drop or large capex for carbon projects).

However, given OXY's strong free cash flow generation in a $70 oil world and Buffett's endorsement, many analysts use \~10% as appropriate cost of equity. At 10%, clearly, **the stock appears materially undervalued**.

**Expected Return at Current Price:** Another way to look at it - if one buys OXY at \~$41 and OXY performs as in Scenario 1 (flat FCF, no growth), the IRR would be about 17% as calculated. If OXY achieves Scenario 2 (5% growth), the IRR at $41 would be even higher (\~20%+). These are excellent returns - indicating why Buffett might be interested. In fact, Buffett likely sees OXY as yielding a high "shareholder earnings" yield: in 2023 OXY had \~$6.5B FCF, which is an **FCF yield of \~16% on the $40.6B market cap**. Even after funding growth and paying dividends, that implies a mid-teens return potential.

**Margin of Safety Analysis:** If we take a middle ground intrinsic value around $80 (between our scenarios), a 30% margin of safety would mean a buy price of $56; a 50% margin implies $40. Right now, at $41, the stock is essentially trading at about a 50% discount to conservative value - which for a value investor is attractive. This suggests OXY is in the "strong buy" zone if one trusts these cash flow projections. The key question is durability of cash flows: our DCF assumes OXY can continue generating on the order of $6-7B/year in free cash. That, in turn, assumes oil prices don't collapse long-term and OXY's capex yields enough new production to offset declines. Given OXY's 4 billion BOE reserves and Permian growth, those assumptions are reasonable. OXY could even exceed them in high-price years or via new businesses (if direct air capture yields new income streams by mid-2020s).

It's also prudent to test a stress scenario: say oil falls and OXY's FCF shrinks to $4B for a few years then recovers. The intrinsic value would drop but likely still around $60+. OXY's current price appears to reflect an overly pessimistic view - possibly market concerns about recession or the CrownRock deal temporarily weighing.

In Buffett's own methodology, he might consider OXY's **"owner earnings"** (net income + non-cash charges - maintenance capex) and then project with a margin of safety. OXY's 2023 owner earnings (roughly net $3.7B plus $5B depreciation minus $2-3B sustaining capex) would be in the $5-6B range - quite close to the FCF we used. Buffett likely looks at OXY and sees a **double-digit earnings yield with stable assets** - which is why he's comfortable buying more.

**Intrinsic Value Summary:** Using a Buffett-style DCF with conservative inputs, Occidental's intrinsic value is estimated around **$75-95 per share**, significantly above the current market price. Even accounting for a hefty margin of safety (30-50%), the stock appears undervalued. This suggests an investor like Buffett could expect a \~15-20% annual return buying OXY at current levels - aligning with his actions. Of course, commodity companies merit a margin of safety due to cyclicality. But OXY's financial reset (lower debt) and buybacks actually reduce downside risk compared to a few years ago. Therefore, from an intrinsic value perspective, **Occidental Petroleum offers considerable upside potential relative to its price**, with the market seemingly underestimating the company's cash flow resilience and shareholder return strategy. This gap between price and value is precisely what has attracted value investors and underpins the bullish thesis on OXY.

-   **Return on Equity (ROE):** ROE has been all over: it was **high in good years** (2013 ROE \~13.8%, 2018 \~19.6%), **negative in loss years** (2015 ROE -25.7%, 2020 ROE -67%), and recently robust again (2022 ROE a huge **44.5%**due to $12.4B profit on $30B equity). 2023 ROE was \~12.7%. The 10-year average ROE is low-single-digits because of multiple loss years. But OXY's **normalized ROE** (on mid-cycle earnings) might be \~8-12%. Importantly, since 2021 OXY's ROE turned positive and healthy, reflecting improved profitability on a slimmed equity base.

-   **ROIC (Return on Invested Capital):** Using ROI from macrotrends as a proxy (which roughly equates to after-tax ROIC using net income and debt+equity as capital), OXY's ROIC was excellent in 2018 (\~17.9%) and stellar in 2022 (\~27.7%), but deeply negative in 2015 (-15 to -25%) and 2020 (-25.8%). The **10-year average ROIC** is around 5-6%, but excluding crisis years it improves. In 2023 ROIC was \~13%, indicating solid efficiency. This is above OXY's cost of capital (\~7% WACC), so value is being created now, whereas in 2015-2020 OXY was destroying capital with poor returns.

-   **Dividends per Share:** Occidental was known for a generous dividend until 2020. It paid **$2.88 per share in 2014**, steadily increasing each year by a penny per quarter: $3.02 in 2016, $3.14 in 2019. The quarterly dividend reached $0.79 by 2019 (annual $3.16). In 2020, OXY famously slashed the dividend by \~99% amid the crisis - paying only **$0.82 total in 2020** (after maintaining the $0.79 in Q1, then just $0.01 for Q2-Q4). 2021's dividend was a token **$0.04** (one cent a quarter). In 2022, with recovery, OXY raised it to $0.13 in Q2; total paid was **$0.52** for 2022. In 2023, they increased it to $0.18 from Q1 onward, totaling **$0.72**. For 2024, they further increased to $0.22 (from Q1) and then to $0.24 in Q2; if we annualize Q4 2023's $0.18 and 2024's $0.22, it's **$0.88** for full-year 2024. The drastic cut in 2020 broke OXY's long dividend growth streak, but it was necessary to conserve cash. Now dividends are rising rapidly (2022 +1200%, 2023 +38%, 2024 +22% growth rates per Fidelity data). Even so, the 2024 dividend ($0.88) remains far below pre-2020 levels, highlighting that OXY has prioritized debt reduction and buybacks over restoring the old payout.

The 10-year EPS average is skewed by outlier losses; excluding 2015 & 2020, OXY's average EPS is around $3-4. Notably, 2022's EPS of $12.40 was an all-time high. It has since normalized downward.

The 10-year FCF trend is negative in mid-2010s, turning positive after 2017, then booming in 2021-22. **Owners' Earnings** (Buffett's measure akin to FCF after maintenance) would average around the FCF figures in profitable years. OXY's FCF margins were thin or negative until 2018; by 2022 FCF margin was \~35%.

### **Financial Analysis (2014-2024 Trends)**

Occidental's financial performance over the past decade can be characterized in **distinct phases**: a high-profit early 2010s, a severe downturn mid-2010s, a leveraged expansion in 2019 followed by a near-crisis in 2020, and finally a dramatic recovery by 2022-2023. Let's analyze key trends and the company's financial health:

**Profitability and Margins:** OXY's profitability is highly correlated with oil prices. In the strong oil price environment of 2011-2014, OXY enjoyed healthy margins (2013 net margin \~29%). The oil price crash of 2015 crushed margins - OXY had negative net and operating margins in 2015-2016, as fixed costs couldn't be cut fast enough to avoid losses. Net loss in 2015 was $-7.8B (largely asset impairments, indicating previous investments were written down). 2016 was a smaller loss ($-0.6B). By 2017-2018, oil's partial rebound lifted OXY back to respectable profits: 2018's \~$4.1B net (23% net margin) showed that OXY's core business is quite profitable at \~$65 oil.

The **Anadarko acquisition in 2019** temporarily depressed profitability (due to interest costs, integration expenses, and unfortunately coinciding with a price dip). 2019 saw a slight operating loss and a net loss of $0.98B, despite higher production, highlighting the burden of deal financing (Buffett's preferred dividends, etc.). Then **2020** hit: the pandemic-driven price collapse forced massive impairments. OXY's $15.7B net loss in 2020 is one of the largest in its history - essentially wiping out a decade of prior profits on paper. This included writing down oil & gas reserves' book values. The fact that equity fell from $34B to $18.6B in one year shows how destructive 2020 was financially.

However, **2021-2022 marked a renaissance**. OXY not only returned to profitability but to *record* profit in 2022. Net income of $12.4B in 2022 was Occidental's highest ever, translating to an ROE of \~45%. This dramatic swing underscores OXY's operating leverage: once fixed costs (depreciation, interest) are covered, additional revenue flows heavily to the bottom line. We see that in 2022, operating margin &gt;37% and net margin 34% - extraordinarily high due to a combination of strong commodity prices and OXY's cost discipline.

**Expense control**: OXY took significant steps to trim costs in the downturn. From 2014 to 2020, OXY's SG&A and operating costs were reduced (e.g., OXY cut overhead by consolidating offices, and lowered Permian unit operating costs through technology). By 2021, OXY's per-barrel operating expenses were down nearly 20% from 2014 levels. This meant that when revenue surged in 2022, much of it translated to cash flow instead of being eaten by costs. The reduction in interest expense with debt paydown also helped - interest expense peaked in 2020 ($1.6B) and dropped by 2023 (under $1B).

**Liquidity and Solvency:** A critical aspect of OXY's financials is how it managed its balance sheet. The Anadarko acquisition in 2019 loaded the company with debt and new assets. Debt-to-equity skyrocketed from \~0.5 to \~2.0, and interest coverage became tight by 2020 (EBIT was negative, so coverage was negative - a bad sign). In 2020, there were genuine bankruptcy concerns in the market, reflected by bond downgrades to junk. However, Occidental **staved off insolvency** through a combination of asset sales (over $2B sold in 2020), drastic capex cuts (-60% in 2020), and an emergency dividend cut (saving $2.6B per year). Buffett's $10B preferred investment also provided a financial lifeline (though at the cost of a hefty 8% dividend). These measures pulled OXY back from the brink - by late 2020, OXY maintained adequate liquidity ($2B cash, and it didn't breach covenants).

The quick recovery in oil prices from late 2020 onward then allowed OXY to **rapidly improve solvency metrics**. Debt/EBITDA which was infinite in 2020 fell to \~2x in 2021 and \~0.8x in 2022 (EBITDA \~$18B vs net debt \~$15B). By 2023, OXY's **debt ratio** (debt/assets \~0.60) was still a bit high in absolute terms, but net debt/EBITDA remained around 1x, very manageable. The company's **interest coverage** in 2022 was \~11× (EBIT of $13.7B vs interest \~$1.2B). Even in 2023, interest coverage \~7×, robust.

The **trend in leverage** is important: From 2019 to 2023, OXY reduced long-term debt from \~$38.5B to \~$18.5B. That's a 52% reduction. This was funded by windfall cash flows in 2021-22. One can clearly see the deleveraging in the D/E ratio declining each year after 2020. OXY also chipped away at the preferred stock: in 2022-23 it redeemed $1.4B of the $10B Buffett preferred (they disclosed redeeming $475M in Q4 2022, and $582M in 2023), which improves future cash flow by reducing the preferred dividend burden.

**Asset Quality and Write-downs:** The large impairments in 2015 and 2020 suggest OXY wrote off lower-value fields (likely natural gas assets, given gas price declines, and some high-cost oil properties). While painful, these write-downs "cleaned up" the balance sheet. By 2022, OXY's asset base (especially after impairments) was more reflective of current values (book value per BOE in reserves is now conservative). That's partly why OXY's ROE shot up in 2022 - the denominator (equity) had been reduced by prior impairments, so the assets left are highly productive ones.

**Segment performance:** Over the decade, Oil & Gas segment earnings dominated in good years and saw losses in bad years. The Chemical segment provided stable, if smaller, profits ($500M-$1.5B EBIT most years). OxyChem actually acted as a buffer in downturns: in 2015-2016, OxyChem remained profitable ($0.7B EBIT each year) even as E&P lost money, giving OXY a bit of cash flow to offset upstream losses. In 2021-2022, OxyChem reached record earnings (in 2022, OxyChem pre-tax income \~$2.5B, riding high commodity chemical margins). This diversification helped smooth consolidated results somewhat. The Midstream segment has been lumpy: sometimes contributing positive earnings (especially when it monetizes assets or trades well), other times incurring losses (e.g., in 2020 OXY had derivative losses). But midstream's financial weight is much smaller than upstream.

**Efficiency Ratios:** OXY's **ROIC improvement** from negative in 2016 to nearly 27% in 2022 is striking. That indicates how dramatically asset productivity improved when prices normalized. It also indicates management's better capital allocation - they cut marginal projects and sold non-core assets (OXY sold its non-core Africa assets to Total in 2019 for $8.8B and its Permian minerals in 2021 for \~$0.5B, etc.), meaning the capital employed is more concentrated on high-return assets.

One can note **production growth vs capex**: OXY's production in 2024 (\~1.2-1.3 MMboe/d) is up about 60% from 2014 (\~0.75 MMboe/d). Yet the share count is up \~25%. This suggests that, despite dilution, production-per-share has grown. That is a positive from an efficiency standpoint - OXY ultimately got value from Anadarko (in production terms) that, with higher prices, is finally paying off.

**Dividend and Buyback Sustainability:** The data shows OXY historically paid more in dividends than it sometimes earned (especially 2015-2019). Its payout ratio was well over 100% during down years (hence debt increased). The dividend cut in 2020 reset that. Now, with a token dividend, OXY's payout ratio in 2022 was \~4% of earnings and \~7% of FCF - extremely low, meaning plenty of room for raises. Even after raising to $0.72/year in 2023, that was only \~18% of 2023 earnings. This conservative dividend is sustainable under quite low oil prices (management indicated they can sustain the dividend at $40-$50 oil). Meanwhile, buybacks have emerged as the main return mechanism - OXY repurchased \~5% of shares in 2022-23. These were fully covered by free cash flow. For instance, 2022's $3B buyback was roughly one-third of $8.9B FCF; 2023's $1.8B repurchases were also well within the $5-6B FCF. So OXY's shareholder returns are now grounded on actual cash generation, unlike pre-2020 when they borrowed at times to fund dividends. This marks a shift to a much **healthier capital return model**.

**Trendline Outlook:** Looking at 2014-2024 trends, OXY emerged larger (in production/assets) but also more leveraged by 2019, then restructured to become financially leaner by 2024. Key improvements: net debt halved, break-even costs lowered, and share count stabilized (starting to decline via buybacks). Profitability metrics like ROE/ROIC, while volatile, show an upward trajectory post-2020. The big question is whether OXY can maintain solid earnings in a "normal" oil price environment (say $60-$70 Brent). The 2023 results (net $3.75B at \~$80 Brent average) prove OXY can still earn &gt;10% ROE in such conditions. If oil averages lower, say $50, OXY's margins would compress but likely remain positive (unlike 2016 when costs were higher).

**Financial Health Summary:** As of the latest year (2024), Occidental is in **far better financial shape** than it was five years prior. Debt ratios are back to moderate levels (Debt/Equity \~0.7, interest cover strong). Liquidity is sufficient with positive free cash flow even at moderate oil prices. The company has returned to investment-grade credit, reflecting that rating agencies see the risk of default as low now. From a shareholder perspective, OXY's earlier over-extension has been substantially mitigated by disciplined use of the 2021-22 windfall. The company's *ability to withstand future downturns is greatly improved* - lower debt and a smaller dividend burden give OXY flexibility that it lacked in 2020. This sets the stage for a more balanced approach where both defense (balance sheet) and offense (growth and returns) are feasible.

Finally, **market valuation perspective:** OXY's P/E \~15× (2023) and P/CF \~4-5× (2022-23) is cheap relative to the S&P 500, but not unusual for cyclicals during earnings peaks. However, given OXY's deleveraging, the market may re-rate it to a higher multiple if it demonstrates stable earnings through cycles (akin to a more utility-like profile with carbon capture). Already, OXY's current EV/EBITDA around 4× for 2023 is low historically (it was 8-10× in 2013-14). This suggests investors haven't fully recognized OXY's transformation and earnings power. If OXY hits its stride (mid-cycle EPS \~$5 perhaps), a 10× multiple would justify a \~$50 stock, and in bull cases much more. Our intrinsic value in the prior section ($75-95) underscores that *if* one assumes through-cycle average cash flows, OXY is materially undervalued.

In conclusion, Occidental's 10-year financial journey shows resilience and adaptation. The company took a bold risk in 2019, survived a black swan in 2020 by sacrificing dividends and restructuring, and then capitalized on a favorable turn of the cycle to emerge stronger. Its financial statements now evidence prudent management: high profits in good times, improved cost structure for bad times, and a clear dedication to creating shareholder value (via buybacks and debt elimination). The prudent investor will note that while OXY's past was erratic, its **present financial footing is solid**. Going forward, maintaining capital discipline (avoiding overpriced acquisitions, keeping costs low, continuing moderate capex) will be key to realizing the intrinsic value indicated by these fundamentals. So far, management is walking that path, and the financial trends support a cautiously optimistic outlook for Occidental Petroleum's future.

### **Conservative DCF Analysis Scenarios**

To complement our intrinsic value discussion above, we double-check valuation under specific growth scenarios drawn from historical performance:

1.  **Scenario A - Lowest 10-Year Growth:** Occidental's worst 10-year growth stretch included serious declines (e.g., revenues fell \~35% in 2015). However, for a forward-looking DCF, we interpret "lowest growth" as essentially **no growth** to slightly negative. Thus, Scenario A assumes **0% revenue and cash flow growth** for the next 5 years (i.e., flat production and oil prices), and perhaps even a slight decline thereafter. Using 2023 as a base: revenue \~$28B, FCF \~$6B, we project these flat. Terminal growth set to a minimal 1%. Discounting at, say, 12% (more conservative than 10%) to add margin: The DCF value comes out roughly around **$50-$55 per share**. This aligns with the idea that even if OXY stagnates, it's worth more than current $41 (because it would still generate a lot of cash each year and has finite reserves not expiring quickly). The **expected return in this scenario** at $41 would be \~12% (matching the discount, since we equated value around $50). That's a solid return for zero growth, indicating downside protection.
