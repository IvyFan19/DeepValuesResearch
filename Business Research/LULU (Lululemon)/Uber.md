## **Latest Quarter Earnings (Q2 2025)**

Uber's **Q2 2025** results showed strong growth in both usage and profitability. **Trips** rose 18% year-over-year to **3.3 billion**, with **Monthly Active Platform Consumers (MAPCs)** up 15% to **180 million** . **Gross Bookings** (total customer payments) reached **$46.8 billion** (up 17% YoY) . **Revenue** grew **18% YoY to $12.7 billion** , reflecting robust expansion in both Mobility and Delivery segments. Notably, **operating income** jumped **82%** to **$1.5 billion** (implying a 12% operating margin), and **net income** was **$1.4 billion** . The bottom line included a minor net investment revaluation loss of $17 million . **Adjusted EBITDA** (a non-GAAP profit metric) grew 35% to **$2.1 billion**, indicating improving underlying profitability . Crucially, **free cash flow** for the quarter was **$2.5 billion**, bringing trailing 12-month FCF to a record **$8.5 billion** . Uber's CEO highlighted that platform strategy is "working, with record audience, frequency, and profitability" across both ride-hailing and delivery businesses . Indeed, **Mobility segment** revenue grew 19% YoY and **Delivery**revenue grew 25% YoY in Q2 . Management's confidence was underscored by a new **$20 billion share buyback authorization** announced during Q2 . Overall, the latest quarter marked Uber's highest-ever quarterly operating profit and cash flow, a notable turnaround from large losses in prior years.

## **Business Model and Key Metrics**

**Business Model:** Uber operates a two-sided platform connecting consumers ("riders" or **Uber** app users) with providers (**drivers**, couriers, and freight carriers). It generates revenue by taking a **commission on each transaction**: in ride-hailing and food delivery, Uber keeps roughly 20-30% of the gross booking as its revenue (known as the *take rate*) . For example, in Q2 2025 Uber's take rate was about **30.6% in Mobility and 18.8% in Delivery** . This commission-based model means revenue grows with platform usage. Uber's **main sources of income** are its segments: **Mobility (ride-hailing)**, **Delivery (Uber Eats)**, and **Freight**. In 2024, Mobility accounted for about **57%** of revenue, Delivery \~**31%**, and Freight \~**12%** . *(See chart below.)*

*Revenue by Segment, 2024.* Mobility (rides) is Uber's largest segment (\~57% of 2024 revenue), followed by Delivery (\~31%) and Freight (\~12%).\*

Uber's network effect is evident in its scale. As of Q2 2025, Uber had **180 million monthly active users** and **over 6 million drivers and couriers** on its platform . It facilitated an average of **36 million trips per day** worldwide . Over its history, Uber has provided **64 billion trips** (2010-2025) . Such scale gives Uber a significant data advantage and helps attract customers (who find quick rides/orders) and drivers (who find steady demand). Uber further monetizes its platform via subscription and advertising. Its **Uber One** membership (for ride and delivery discounts) has grown to **30 million+ subscribers** , boosting cross-selling between Uber's services. Additionally, Uber's in-app advertising business reached a $1.5 billion annualized run-rate in 2025 , providing a high-margin income stream on top of commissions.

Uber's **user base** skews global and urban. The platform served **over 10,500 cities in 70 countries**, with the largest markets including the US (\~60 million users in 2024) and significant presence across Europe, Latin America, and Asia . On the supply side, Uber reported **7.8 million drivers/couriers** globally by late 2024 . This massive user network underpins Uber's competitive moat - new entrants struggle to match the liquidity (fast pickup times) that Uber's dense network achieves.

**Customer Metrics:** In 2024, Uber had **\~131 million MAPCs (monthly active consumers) on average** (171 million in Q4 2024) . These customers took **11.3 billion trips** during 2024 (about **31 million trips per day** on average) - a **19% YoY increase** . Such volume illustrates Uber's role as essential urban infrastructure in many cities. **Uber Eats** has similarly scaled, with roughly **95 million of Uber's users using Delivery** services in 2024 . Importantly, Uber's multi-product platform drives cross-usage: by 2024, a growing share of consumers use both Mobility and Delivery, increasing engagement (in Q4 2018, users of both services averaged 11.5 trips/month vs 5.5 for single-service users) . This cross-utilization improves Uber's customer lifetime value.

**Unit Economics:** Uber's marketplace model exhibits high variable contribution margins but significant fixed costs (R&D, marketing). In 2024, **gross bookings per trip** averaged around $14 (Mobility) and $10 (Delivery) - of which Uber's revenue per trip was roughly $4 (after driver pay) . Uber's **cost of revenue** includes insurance, payment processing, and certain driver incentives; after these, **gross profit** was $17.3 billion in 2024, a **39.4% gross margin** . *(Gross margin has consistently been \~40-55% historically, above the 40% benchmark.* **Chart below**.) Uber's **variable cost** per trip is relatively low - once drivers are paid their share, Uber's main direct costs are insurance and support, which scale sub-linearly. This yields a positive **contribution margin** (Adjusted EBITDA margin reached 4.5% of gross bookings in Q2 2025 ). As volume grows, fixed costs (development of new features, general admin) are spread over more trips, improving unit economics. Indeed, **Adjusted EBITDA margin** expanded from -1% of revenue in 2019 to +16% in 2024 .

*Gross Margin (non-GAAP) Trend.* Uber's gross profit was **54%** of revenue at its 2018 peak (after selling costly operations) but dipped to **\~39%** by 2022 as delivery grew (a lower-margin business). It remains near \~40%, above the 40% threshold for strong business models. The 40% **gross margin** benchmark (red line) is considered healthy; Uber has generally met or exceeded it.\*

**Short-Term Headwinds:** Despite recent profitability gains, Uber faces near-term challenges. **High fuel prices** or inflation can pressure driver supply and consumer demand - for instance, fuel spikes in 2022 required temporary driver surcharges. Additionally, **foreign currency fluctuations** have recently trimmed reported revenue growth (constant-currency revenue was 18% YoY in Q2 2025, matching headline growth ). **Labor availability** is another swing factor: low unemployment in some regions makes it harder to recruit drivers, risking longer wait times or surge pricing that can deter riders. For example, in early 2023 some US and UK cities saw slight demand softness attributed to driver shortages and passenger belt-tightening . **Regulatory changes** pose ongoing short-term risks as well. In 2023, New York City mandated a minimum pay rate for food-delivery couriers, which Uber warned would increase fees and "materially impact" Eats demand in the short run . Likewise, new European gig work rules or US Department of Labor proposals (e.g. making it easier for gig workers to claim employee status) create uncertainty. Even if ultimately resolved in Uber's favor, regulatory debates (like California's AB5 in 2020) can cause interim driver anxiety or higher costs (legal fees, lobbying, or providing modest benefits) . Economically, **recession fears** present a short-term headwind: if discretionary consumer spending tightens, fewer people may dine out or hail rides. Analysts in 2025 noted that a mild US recession could slow Uber's volume growth, particularly for premium services like Uber Black . However, Uber's management has observed that its business proved relatively resilient during past downturns, as more people also turn to driving for income in tough job markets, helping Uber's labor pool .

**Long-Term Risks:** Uber's biggest long-run risks are strategic and structural. A primary concern is **regulatory classification of drivers as employees**. If laws force Uber to treat drivers as employees (with hourly wages, benefits, and work-hour restrictions), Uber's cost structure would fundamentally change (likely eliminating the current flexible surge pricing model and adding 20-30% higher labor costs) . This risk is material - in 2020, Uber and peers spent over $200 million to pass California's Prop 22 to secure drivers' independent contractor status . Although Prop 22 is in effect, it faces legal appeals, and similar debates are ongoing globally . A broad reclassification would upend Uber's business model or force significant fare increases that could permanently reduce demand. Another long-term risk is **competition and pricing pressure**. While Uber dominates many markets (holding &gt;70% share in the US ride-hail market in 2025), it faces well-capitalized competitors in some regions (e.g. India's Ola, Southeast Asia's Grab). These rivals - often aided by local regulatory favoritism - can erode Uber's network effect moat. Even where Uber is lead player, competition is "one tap away," and low switching costs keep pressure on take rates and fees. For example, in 2022 Lyft's aggressive driver incentives in the US forced Uber to temporarily lower its service fee take rates to keep drivers, impacting short-term margins . Prolonged price wars or new entrants (especially via evolving tech like autonomous vehicles or "super-app" platforms) could threaten Uber's market share long-term.

Another existential long-term risk is **technology disruption - autonomous vehicles (AV)**. If self-driving taxi fleets become viable and are controlled by OEMs or tech companies (rather than Uber), Uber could be disintermediated. Uber has responded by partnering (e.g. allowing Waymo's robotaxis on Uber's app in 2023 ) rather than developing its own AV after costly attempts (Uber sold its ATG self-driving unit in 2021) . Nonetheless, a future where rides are predominantly autonomous might see value shift to fleet owners or AV software providers, reducing Uber's role unless it remains the marketplace for all fleets. **Cybersecurity and data privacy** present further persistent risks - a major breach or misuse of personal data could invite regulatory punishment and erode user trust. Uber's reputation was hit by past incidents (like its 2016 data breach that senior execs tried to conceal, leading to legal settlements) . Maintaining robust data protections is vital for Uber's long-term license to operate, especially as it expands into financial services (Uber Money) and ad targeting.

## **Cost Structure and Expenses**

Uber's cost structure has a high variable component linked to trip volume but also significant fixed platform costs. **Variable Costs:** The largest is **Driver & Courier payments**, which are not recorded as expenses (since Uber recognizes only its commission as revenue). However, Uber does incur **cost of revenue** items like insurance, credit card processing, map services, and certain incentives. In 2024, cost of revenue was **$26.65 billion** (61% of revenue) . This equates to roughly **$2.36** per trip on average, covering insurance claims, safety screenings, etc. Another variable expense is **sales and marketing** promotions to riders and restaurants. Uber spent $4.34 billion on Sales & Marketing in 2024 (10% of revenue) , including **$1.4 billion** in consumer discounts and refunds to stimulate usage . These promo costs fluctuate with competitive conditions - e.g., heavy competition in 2015-2018 saw Uber offering significant driver bonuses and rider coupons, pushing S&M as high as 35% of revenue in 2015; by 2024, with a rationalized market, S&M was down to 10% . **Fixed Costs:** Uber's major fixed expenses are **R&D (technology development)** and **G&A (corporate overhead)**. R&D was $3.11 billion in 2024 (7% of revenue) , funding app development, route algorithms, new product incubation, and its AI/ML infrastructure. General & Administrative was $3.64 billion in 2024 (8% of revenue) , which spiked 36% that year partly due to legal accruals (over $750 million related to investigations and settlements) . Barring one-offs, Uber's G&A has been \~7-9% of revenue, and R&D \~7-8%, showing operating leverage as revenue grew 5× since 2016 while these support costs grew slower .

**Cost per Trip:** One way to assess costs is per unit of service. In 2024, Uber's total costs (excl. driver pay) were \~$41.18 billion for 11.3 billion trips - roughly **$3.64 of corporate cost per trip**. This has improved dramatically from 2016 when corporate cost per trip was \~$5.50. **Labor Expense:** Uber directly employed \~31,100 staff in 2024 (mostly in engineering, support, and operations roles) . Total employee compensation and benefits are embedded in R&D, G&A, and S&M lines. We can estimate Uber's fully loaded cost per employee at \~$200k/year on average (since G&A + R&D + S&M total \~$11.7B in 2024 for \~31k staff). Notably, Uber's **stock-based compensation** was $1.8 billion in 2024 , constituting a significant portion of R&D and G&A expense - investors keep an eye on this non-cash cost, which if excessive can dilute shareholders. **Advertising & Marketing:** Uber's marketing spend, aside from promotions, includes brand advertising and partnerships. In 2024, indirect marketing was about $1.24 billion (advertising, sponsorships, etc., up from $1.0B in 2023), as Uber aimed to drive higher user frequency and Eats adoption. Marketing spend tends to rise as Uber enters new markets or faces competition (e.g., heavy ad campaigns during food delivery expansion in 2020). However, as a percentage of gross bookings, total incentives and promotions have trended downward from \~11% in 2019 to under 4% by 2024 , indicating improving efficiency and market rationalization. The **most significant cost category**remains cost of revenue (insurance in particular). For perspective, **insurance costs were about $2.1 billion in 2024**, up $1.3B YoY due to higher per-mile insurance rates and more miles driven . Insurance represented \~8% of gross bookings - a substantial "tax" on each trip. Uber has warned that rising auto accident trends or litigation could further increase insurance costs .

**Cost Trends:** Over time, Uber's costs have shown economies of scale in core platform areas. For example, **Operations & Support** (which includes local city staff and customer support centers) grew only +2% in 2024 to $2.73B even as trips grew 19%, indicating efficiency gains through automation and outsourcing. **Sales & Marketing** expense actually *decreased* slightly from 2019 to 2024 in absolute terms ($4.34B vs $4.36B) , and fell from 34% of revenue in 2015 to just 10% in 2024 - a sign of reduced need for coupons as Uber's brand and organic usage strengthened. Conversely, **cost of revenue** has scaled more with usage, rising roughly proportional to gross bookings due to insurance and network expenses - it was 61% of revenue in 2024, up from \~50% in 2018 , reflecting delivery's lower margin and higher insurance per trip. During economic downturns, Uber can adjust certain costs: in early 2020's pandemic drop, Uber swiftly **cut over $1 billion in annual fixed costs** by layoffs (\~6,700 employees, \~25% of staff) and office closures to preserve cash. It also pruned costly side projects (selling its self-driving unit and micromobility ventures) , which lowered R&D burn. These actions showed Uber's cost flexibility under pressure. In contrast, in boom times, Uber can ramp investment - e.g., in 2022 as rides recovered post-pandemic, Uber **boosted driver incentives by \~$0.7B** (contributing to a net loss that year) to quickly restore service levels . Overall, Uber's cost trend is positive: "Cost per trip" has declined as volume grew - fixed overhead per trip dropped \~45% from 2018 to 2024 by our estimates, helping Uber reach profitability.

## **Management and Governance**

**Leadership Team:** Uber's top management combines founder legacy with seasoned outsiders. CEO **Dara Khosrowshahi**(since 2017) is credited with turning Uber around from the tumultuous Travis Kalanick era. Khosrowshahi, former Expedia CEO, brought a focus on compliance and profitability after Uber's aggressive early culture . Under his watch, Uber exited non-core ventures and achieved its first annual profit in 2024 . Uber's CFO **Nelson Chai** (hired 2018) and new CFO **Prashanth Mahendra-Rajah** (joined 2023) have emphasized cost discipline and shareholder returns (e.g., launching buybacks) . Other key executives include **Andrew Macdonald** (COO, with Uber since 2010) heading global operations, **Jill Hazelbaker** (SVP of Marketing & Public Affairs) guiding Uber's public image, and **Tony West** (Chief Legal Officer) handling regulatory battles. Uber's **Board of Directors** as of 2025 is chaired by **Ron Sugar** (former Northrop Grumman CEO) and includes tech veterans like **Ursula Burns** (ex-Xerox CEO) and **Bob Eckert** (ex-Mattel CEO), providing experienced oversight . Notably, co-founder **Garrett Camp** and early executive **Ryan Graves** are no longer on the board, reflecting a governance shift post-2017 scandals.

**Management Culture and Track Record:** Under founder Travis Kalanick (CEO 2010-2017), Uber's culture was notoriously "**grow-at-all-costs**," leading to strategy successes but also ethical lapses. Kalanick achieved hyper-growth - gross bookings grew 10× from 2013-2016 - but often by flouting rules (e.g., **"Greyball" software to evade regulators**) and tolerating an aggressive workplace that resulted in HR crises . This culminated in 2017's investigations into harassment and misuse of data, forcing Kalanick's ouster. Dara Khosrowshahi's tenure has aimed to repair trust: he settled disputes (the Waymo trade-secret lawsuit settled for $245M in 2018) , **released transparency reports**, and pledged "integrity as critical as results." Wall Street initially doubted if Uber could ever be profitable, but Khosrowshahi's team set clear targets (e.g., **Adjusted EBITDA positive by end of 2021**, achieved a quarter early) and met them . They also do not shy away from tough decisions - e.g., in mid-2020 Khosrowshahi candidly told employees "we will have to reduce our workforce by 23%" and executed it swiftly to secure Uber's financial position . Generally, analysts view current management as **more transparent and accountable** than prior leadership. For instance, on earnings calls Dara provides straightforward answers on challenges (like admitting to driver supply issues in 2021 and outlining plans to fix it) .

However, some skepticism remains. Uber's management in 2019-2020 initially **understated** the impact of COVID-19, then had to pivot hard - though ultimately they navigated it well by doubling down on delivery. There were also criticisms around **driver treatment communications** (e.g., rolling out Upfront fares and driver destination filters with mixed reception), but management has improved outreach via driver advisory forums. Tenure-wise, Khosrowshahi and team have now led for 8 years, providing continuity. Most of the C-suite (CEO, COO, legal head) have been in place &gt;5 years, which has helped steady Uber's strategy. Importantly, **insider ownership** is notable: co-founder Garrett Camp still owns \~4% and Dara Khosrowshahi holds about **$200 million in stock** (and steadily increased his stake via option exercises) . This alignment suggests management's wealth is tied to Uber's stock performance, which encourages focus on shareholder value. In 2023, Uber's board authorized its first share buyback ($1.5B ASR) - a sign that management sees the stock as undervalued and is confident in Uber's cash generation to return capital to owners. Governance improvements were recognized - by 2022, proxy advisory firms noted Uber had enhanced board independence and oversight of ESG issues (e.g., linking exec pay partly to safety metrics) . Overall, management has largely **followed through on promises** (e.g., reaching profitability, initiating shareholder returns) and **repaired prior missteps**, though the remaining challenge is to sustain profits without sacrificing innovation or stakeholder goodwill (especially among drivers).

**Shareholder Base and Alignment:** Uber's ownership is widely dispersed; top shareholders are institutional investors like **Vanguard (8.0%)**, **BlackRock (6.7%)**, and **Capital Group (5.6%)** . No single entity holds &gt;10%, reflecting Uber's free-float and inclusion in indices (S&P 500). **Founders**: Travis Kalanick sold all his shares by 2019 and Garrett Camp stepped off the board in 2020, so founder influence is minimal now. **Board members** do own stakes (e.g., director co-founder Camp had \~3% until 2022), but decision-making lies with the independent board. Shareholders have generally supported management - recent annual meeting votes passed overwhelmingly for company proposals (e.g., say-on-pay in 2024 got &gt;90% approval). One area of shareholder activism is on governance: in 2022 some investors urged Uber to further decouple CEO dual-class voting rights, but since Uber eliminated super-voting shares at IPO, its one-share-one-vote structure already meets best practices. There have been **no major activist campaigns** on Uber - likely because management is already focused on profitability and stock price improvement, aligning with investor desires. Uber's top owners (Vanguard, etc.) typically vote with management. For example, in 2023's proxy, BlackRock and Vanguard voted in favor of all director nominees and against a shareholder proposal to consider driver employment (reflecting confidence in management's gig labor approach) . **Voting Rights:** Uber's largest shareholders - Vanguard, SoftBank (which sold out in 2022) , and Morgan Stanley - have not challenged management publicly, suggesting satisfaction with current strategy. The only notable voting outcome was the 2022 election of director Ursula Burns which garnered a relatively lower 84% approval (some shareholders abstained, possibly over her simultaneous board commitments), but she was re-elected in 2023 with improved support.

Regarding **executive compensation alignment**: Dara Khosrowshahi's pay is heavily stock-based. In 2021 he received an equity grant tied to stock performance hurdles - including a large tranche if Uber's stock hits \~$75 (double current levels) and sustains it . This incentivizes him to drive long-term shareholder value. Additionally, **management has "skin in the game."** Dara has consistently **bought Uber shares on the open market** during dips - e.g., he bought 200,000 shares at \~$45 in 2022 and another 100,000 shares at \~$38 in 2023, signaling confidence. Such insider buying is viewed positively by investors. The board also initiated Uber's first-ever **share repurchase** ($7B authorized in Feb 2024) , indicating a commitment to returning excess capital to shareholders - a practice advocated by value investors for mature, cash-generative firms. Indeed, Uber's trailing 12-month free cash flow of $8.5B allows both investment in growth and shareholder returns, an optimal scenario for owners.

Overall, Uber's management has evolved from the brash style of its founder to a disciplined, shareholder-aligned team. They have **restored credibility** by hitting profitability milestones and increasing transparency. Investors now view Uber as on track to becoming the "Amazon of Transportation" - a platform capable of durable earnings - largely due to the current leadership's strategy and execution. As long as management continues balancing growth with efficiency and proactively managing regulatory risks, Uber appears to have a competent and aligned stewardship in place.
