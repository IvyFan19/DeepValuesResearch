## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

ASML's latest earnings (Q3 2025) showed stable results with net sales of €7.516 billion (virtually flat from €7.467 billion in Q3 2024) and a gross margin of 51.6% (up from 50.8% a year ago). Net income came in at €2.125 billion, roughly unchanged from €2.1 billion in the prior-year quarter, with EPS of €5.49 (vs €5.28 in Q3 2024). Importantly, new system sales volume was lower (66 new lithography systems sold in Q3 2025 vs 106 in Q3 2024), but this was offset by a richer product mix (EUV tools contributed strongly) such that revenue held steady. Net bookings - a key indicator of future demand - were €5.4 billion, more than double the year-ago bookings of €2.6 billion and slightly above analyst expectations, signaling robust forward demand.

ASML's outlook remains cautiously optimistic. The company reiterated full-year 2025 guidance for \~15% sales growth (to around €32.5 billion) with \~52% gross margin. For Q4 2025, ASML expects a seasonal revenue uptick to €9.2-9.8 billion with gross margin 51-53%. Notably, management warned that **China sales are expected to drop significantly in 2026** after very strong contributions in 2024-2025 (China was \~46% of sales in 2024 and \~33% so far in 2025). This decline reflects tightening export controls and a normalization after a demand pull-in, but ASML still does not foresee 2026 total revenue falling below 2025 levels. Meanwhile, demand related to AI and advanced logic remains a tailwind - ASML is seeing "continued positive momentum" from investments in AI chips, benefiting both leading-edge logic (e.g. TSMC for GPUs) and advanced memory (e.g. HBM for AI). ASML's CEO highlighted that EUV adoption is progressing well (including shipments of the first High-NA EUV prototype and advanced packaging lithography tools). Overall, the quarter was in line with guidance, and the company maintains a confident long-term outlook despite near-term China headwinds.

ASML also updated its capital returns: it declared an interim dividend of €1.60 per share to be paid in Nov 2025, and it continued share buybacks with €148 million repurchased in Q3 (cumulative €5.9 billion repurchased under the 2022-2025 program). Management noted it will likely **not complete** the full €12 billion buyback authorization by end-2025 and intends to announce a new program in January 2026. This ongoing return of cash underscores ASML's strong free cash flow generation and shareholder-friendly policy.

## **Business Model**

**Core Business & Revenue Streams:** ASML is the world's leading supplier of lithography equipment - the machines used to imprint circuit patterns onto silicon wafers in chip manufacturing. Its flagship products are photolithography scanners, notably its exclusive Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) lithography systems (critical for sub-7nm logic chips) and its Deep Ultraviolet (DUV) systems (used for a wide range of semiconductor nodes). ASML's revenue comes from two main sources: **(1) New lithography system sales**, and **(2) Installed Base Management (services & upgrades)**. In 2024, new system sales accounted for roughly 77% of revenue (€21.77 billion out of €28.26 billion), while service and field option sales (maintenance contracts, spare parts, and performance upgrades on existing machines) contributed about 23% (€6.49 billion). Within new system sales, ASML earns income from different product lines: EUV scanners (the most expensive at \~$150 million+ each) have grown to \~40-50% of new system revenue in recent years, while the remainder comes from DUV immersion scanners, DUV dry scanners, and special metrology/inspection systems. For example, in 2020 ASML sold 31 EUV systems generating €4.5 billion (\~32% of 2020 sales), and this mix has increased as more chipmakers adopt EUV for advanced nodes. Services revenue (Installed Base Management) is a stable, high-margin source, growing with the cumulative installed base of machines - in 2024 it reached €6.5 billion, up \~15.5% YoY, as customers continued to purchase software enhancements, maintenance, and productivity upgrades to extend the life and output of their expensive tools.

**Customer Base and Volume:** Unlike consumer-facing companies, ASML has a **small number of very large customers**- essentially all the major semiconductor manufacturers. Its top customers include leading logic foundries and IDMs such as **TSMC**, **Samsung Electronics**, **Intel**, and **SMIC**, as well as memory chipmakers like **SK Hynix**, **Micron**, and **Samsung** (for DRAM/NAND). In total, ASML's customer count is on the order of a few dozen fabs worldwide - it sells to every chipmaker that requires advanced lithography, which might be \~60-70 companies globally (including smaller fabs), but a handful of big players drive the bulk of orders. For instance, in 2022, TSMC, Samsung, and Intel collectively accounted for a significant portion of ASML's revenue (TSMC alone has been estimated around \~30% in some years). China's share surged in 2024 as multiple Chinese fabs (like SMIC and YMTC) bought older-generation tools in bulk.

ASML ships a limited number of machines each year due to their complexity and cost. For perspective, in 2024 it sold **380 new lithography systems** (44 of which were EUV, 336 DUV/other) and 38 used/refurbished systems. This volume (418 total systems) was actually *lower* than 2023's 449 systems, reflecting a slight industry downturn, but the **average selling price (ASP)** per system has risen with a richer mix of EUV. A decade ago, ASML's ASP for new systems was around €25 million; in recent quarters it's substantially higher - for example, in 2023 the blended ASP was roughly €52 million (total new system sales €21.8B/421 units) as EUV tools (priced &gt;€150 M each) made up a larger share. The **installed base** is also sizable: ASML has thousands of systems in the field (by 2020, over 1,200 DUV scanners and \~100 EUV units cumulatively) which generate recurring service revenue. Each EUV machine comes with a service contract and ongoing upgrade cycle (e.g. source power improvements) - a strong annuity-like stream.

**Competitive Moat:** ASML enjoys a formidable **moat rooted in technological monopoly and ecosystem lock-in**. It is literally the *only* company in the world that can produce cutting-edge EUV lithography tools - a result of decades of R&D investment (\~€6 billion spent to develop EUV) and a complex supplier network (e.g. Zeiss for the ultra-precise mirrors) that would be nearly impossible for a new entrant to replicate. In DUV lithography, ASML also holds the largest market share (\~60-70%), with only two much smaller competitors (Nikon and Canon) focused on older technology. Nikon, the former rival in high-end lithography, has essentially fallen behind - it no longer produces cutting-edge scanners for leading-edge nodes after the 193nm immersion era, ceding that market entirely to ASML. This near-monopoly in leading lithography gives ASML significant pricing power and deep integration with customers. ASML's tools are mission-critical - chipmakers **cannot produce advanced chips without ASML's machines**, making ASML an indispensable supplier. Furthermore, ASML's know-how spans hardware, specialized light sources, resist chemistry optimization, and software (its computational lithography and metrology products enhance yield). The *ecosystem effect* is strong: ASML collaborates closely with customers and materials suppliers to continuously improve yields, creating high switching costs. A customer designing a 3nm process with ASML's EUV tools, Twinscan platforms, and YieldStar metrology is deeply invested in ASML's ecosystem. The company also fiercely protects its IP (e.g. it pursued and settled a patent dispute with Nikon in 2018, and it closely guards against any technology leakage, such as a recent incident of an ex-employee allegedly stealing data for a Chinese firm). All these factors form a **formidable moat**. In short, ASML is often described as a *"one-of-a-kind monopoly"* in a critical segment of the semiconductor supply chain, which is why its profit margins and returns are extraordinarily high.

*Key takeaway:* ASML's business model is selling essential "picks and shovels" to chipmakers, specifically lithography systems that have no close substitutes. Its revenue is diversified across product lines (EUV vs DUV) and supplemented by recurring service income. With a near-monopoly in EUV and a dominant position in DUV, ASML's moat is secure - it would take any rival **many years and tens of billions in R&D** to even attempt to catch up, as evidenced by the lack of any viable competitor in EUV lithography.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

**Short-Term/Cyclical Headwinds:** In the near term (next 1-2 years), ASML faces cyclical and macroeconomic pressures that could **temporarily** impact its business:

-   **Semiconductor Demand Cycles:** The chip industry is notoriously cyclical. Downturns in end-market demand (PCs, smartphones, etc.) or inventory corrections can lead chipmakers to delay capacity expansion, reducing orders for ASML's tools. For instance, in late 2022 into 2023, a slump in memory chip prices and smartphone demand caused some ASML customers to push out tool deliveries. ASML's 2024 revenue was almost flat (+2.5%) after years of growth, reflecting a broader chip downturn. Such cycles are *temporary*, but ASML's quarterly orders and revenue can fluctuate significantly with the capex cycles of a few large customers.

-   **Export Controls & Geopolitics:** Geopolitical tensions - particularly the U.S.-China tech war - present short-term volatility. The Dutch government (under pressure from the U.S.) has imposed export restrictions on ASML's most advanced tools to China. Since 2019, ASML has been barred from selling EUV scanners to China, and in 2023 the Netherlands expanded curbs to certain advanced DUV models. ASML can still sell older DUV to Chinese fabs, which led to a surge of orders in 2024-25 as Chinese customers rushed to stockpile tools. However, as noted, **China sales are expected to drop sharply in 2026** due to these restrictions and the prior pull-forward of demand. Politically-driven swings in access to China's market act as a short-term headwind (China was \~€8.5B of ASML's €28.3B sales in 2024, so a decline will create a growth gap in 2026). That said, ASML management believes this is a *normalization* rather than a permanent loss - China represented unusually high demand in 2024-25 that will "normalize" lower. Also, ASML's backlog from other regions (Taiwan, Korea, U.S., Europe) is strong enough that they expect **2026 sales at least flat vs 2025** despite the China fall-off.

-   **Supply Chain Constraints:** ASML's ability to ship tools on time can be hampered by supply bottlenecks. Its machines involve thousands of components (optics, electronics, precision mechatronics), so shortages in any critical component (like high-end lasers or lens materials) could delay production. In early 2022, a fire at one of ASML's suppliers (Berlin factory of a component supplier) threatened to impact tool shipments by a few hundred million euros (though ASML managed to mitigate most of it). Any similar disruptions, or general supply chain tightness, are short-term risks that can shift quarterly revenue from one quarter to the next.

-   **Economic Slowdowns:** A broader macroeconomic slowdown or recession can indirectly reduce demand for electronics (and hence chips and fab equipment). For example, rising interest rates and inflation have tempered consumer electronics spending recently, which filtered through to more cautious capex by chipmakers in 2023. If global GDP weakens significantly, chip companies may defer expansion plans, hitting ASML's near-term growth. However, such slowdowns typically *delay* orders rather than cancel them - the secular demand for semiconductors tends to resume once the economy improves.

In summary, short-term headwinds like **cyclical downturns** and **export restrictions** may cause some lumpy results or modest growth pauses (as seen in 2019 and 2024), but ASML has so far weathered these with only temporary impacts. The company's robust order backlog and broad customer base across logic and memory help cushion cyclical swings.

**Long-Term/Secular Risks:** Over the longer term (5-10+ years), ASML faces several strategic risks that could **permanently** influence its business:

-   **Technological Disruption or Alternatives:** ASML's dominance relies on optical lithography remaining the key process for chipmaking. A radical new technology that supplants photolithography could erode ASML's core business. Potential alternatives often discussed include *next-generation lithography methods* like **electron-beam direct-write**, **nanoimprint lithography**, or even *completely new computing paradigms* (like **quantum computing**or **3D chip architectures** that reduce reliance on 2D litho scaling). So far, none of these have proven viable at scale - optical lithography (193nm immersion and now EUV 13.5nm) continues to be extended with ASML's innovations. ASML is already developing **High-NA EUV** (a next-gen EUV tool with a higher numerical aperture lens to further shrink features) expected by 2025-2026 to enable &lt;2nm nodes. This suggests ASML can likely maintain lithography leadership through the foreseeable future. However, if a non-optical method eventually achieves high-volume manufacturing at smaller geometries, it could threaten ASML's monopoly. This is a *distant but existential* risk. (Notably, ASML is actively involved in exploring beyond-EUV options and has a track record of pivoting - e.g., decades ago it researched X-ray lithography before EUV became viable).

-   **Geopolitical Decoupling & Loss of Markets:** A more permanent splitting of the semiconductor supply chain into geopolitical blocs could constrain ASML's full market potential. If China (the world's largest chip consumer) cannot purchase ASML's top tools indefinitely, ASML loses a growth avenue (it can still sell older tools there, but those are lower-margin and eventually even DUV may be restricted further). In a worst-case scenario, China might **succeed in developing its own lithography equipment** for mature nodes - China's SMEE has primitive 90nm lithography tools today, far behind ASML, but over a decade they could potentially narrow the gap for less advanced processes. While it's unlikely China can replicate EUV in any reasonable timeframe (ASML's CEO has suggested it would take *many* years and international cooperation for anyone else to build an EUV scanner), a self-sufficient Chinese tool industry is a long-term competitive threat. Also, prolonged trade restrictions might push China to invest heavily in alternatives (like multi-patterning older tech or other techniques) reducing reliance on ASML. Outside China, geopolitical factors could cause ASML to shift supply chains or manufacturing (e.g., the U.S. pushing for more tool production domestically) which could add cost or complexity.

-   **Customer Consolidation or Insourcing:** The semiconductor industry has consolidated - e.g., memory has just a few players, logic foundry is dominated by TSMC. If one of ASML's major customers significantly cuts back or faces trouble, ASML's sales could be permanently impacted. For instance, if Intel's market share in leading-edge logic continues to erode, its lithography orders might never recover (though TSMC or Samsung tends to pick up the slack, so industry demand overall remains). Another angle: a large customer *insourcing* lithography development - this is highly improbable given the cost and ASML's head start, but a giant like Intel once attempted more control by investing in ASML (Intel, Samsung, and TSMC each took a \~15% stake in ASML in 2012 to fund EUV R&D, rather than doing it themselves). The result only strengthened ASML's collaborative model. Thus, customer-related long-term risk is moderate.

-   **Execution & Saturation:** ASML's long-term growth also depends on continued device scaling. **Physical limits** in chip shrinking could slow lithography demand - e.g., if Moore's Law significantly decelerates, fabs might buy fewer cutting-edge tools or extend tool lifetimes. ASML's EUV is enabling the current roadmap (\~2nm), but beyond that, the industry might shift to stacking (chiplets, 3D packaging) more than shrinks. ASML is addressing this by entering **advanced packaging lithography** (they shipped their first dedicated tool for chip packaging in 2025). Still, if transistor miniaturization enters a long plateau, lithography tool sales might plateau too - not vanish, but growth could slow permanently.

-   **Cost Inflation & Complexity:** Each new generation of lithography is exorbitantly costly. EUV tool development took decades; High-NA EUV is extremely complex. If R&D costs start outweighing the market's willingness to pay, ASML's returns could diminish. For example, an EUV tool costs \~$150M; a High-NA EUV might cost well over $300M. If only a couple of customers adopt it, ASML must ensure it recoups costs. Thus far, demand is there (ASML has orders for High-NA from multiple customers for 2025+), but the **risk is that technological complexity or delays** (e.g., if High-NA faces issues) could permanently strain ASML's growth or allow an alternative solution to catch up.

In summary, **permanent risks** to ASML revolve around *technological shifts* (a new paradigm replacing optical lithography), *geopolitical realignment* (losing access to a portion of the market), and the eventual *slowdown of Moore's Law*. None of these are imminent threats - ASML's leadership position looks secure for at least the next several years (even critics acknowledge no viable alternative to ASML's tools through 2030). However, investors should monitor signs of these secular changes. ASML itself remains proactive: it continuously innovates (investing \~€3-4B in R&D annually), and it advocates on geopolitical matters (working with governments to navigate export rules).

Overall, while short-term headwinds may create bumps, ASML's long-term narrative as the *linchpin* of chip fabrication remains intact. The biggest long-run risk - a displacement of lithography - appears low probability in the next decade, given industry consensus that **"lithography will remain at the heart of customer innovation"** and ASML's own confidence in extending its roadmap.

## **Cost Structure**

ASML's cost structure combines high fixed costs (for R&D, engineering, and manufacturing overhead) with variable costs tied to machine production. Understanding its costs provides insight into margins and how they behave in different conditions:

-   **Cost per Machine (COGS per unit):** ASML's machines are extremely complex, so each unit has a high bill of materials and assembly cost. For example, an EUV scanner contains unique components (specialized lasers, ultra-flat mirrors, vacuum chambers) sourced from a network of suppliers like Zeiss. The *cost of goods sold (COGS)*includes these materials and direct labor for assembly. ASML doesn't disclose cost per unit explicitly, but we can infer margins: in 2024, gross margin was \~51.3%, meaning roughly half of the sales price is gross profit. If a new EUV sells for \~$150 million, perhaps \~$75 million is cost. Importantly, **COGS has a large fixed-cost absorption element** - ASML's factories must run close to capacity to spread overhead. When volume drops, unit cost rises (hurting gross margin), and vice versa. This is why gross margin fluctuates with volume: e.g., in the softer 2019, GM was \~44.7%, whereas in boom years like 2021, GM reached 52.7%. ASML also continuously works to reduce unit costs via learning-curve effects (especially for new products like EUV where yield improvements in manufacturing can lower cost). For instance, ASML significantly improved the source reliability and production efficiency of EUV tools over time, which helped raise EUV gross margins closer to corporate average by 2021-2022 (initially EUV tools were barely breakeven in cost). So while each machine has high variable cost, scale economies are vital - **marginal cost declines** as production volume increases and processes mature.

-   **Labor Expenses:** ASML employs over 44,000 people globally as of 2025. A large portion are highly skilled engineers (for R&D and customer support) and manufacturing specialists. Labor costs appear in both COGS (factory workers) and operating expenses (R&D engineers, SG&A staff). In 2024, ASML's total operating expenses were about €5.47 billion, of which the majority is R&D (€4.63 billion in 2024 per Yahoo Finance data) and the rest SG&A (\~€0.84 B). Much of these expenses are essentially labor (salaries, benefits) and are **relatively fixed** - ASML doesn't lay off R&D staff in a slow quarter; it views R&D as vital and tends to increase it consistently (e.g. R&D spend grew from \~€0.9B in 2015 to \~€4.6B in 2024). Thus, labor costs (especially R&D) are fixed costs that ASML carries through downturns, impacting operating margins but preserving long-term competitiveness. On the production side, ASML's workforce has grown to meet demand, but management is cautious to avoid over-hiring cyclically - they use subcontractors in manufacturing that can be adjusted as needed. Still, **skilled labor is a critical resource** for ASML and not easily variable; the company invests heavily in talent retention and recruitment (particularly in the Netherlands and in tech hubs worldwide).

-   **R&D and Fixed Overhead:** R&D is by far ASML's largest expense category (in 2021-2024, R&D was \~12-16% of sales each year). These are fixed costs aimed at future products: e.g., developing High-NA EUV, next-gen DUV improvements, new software, etc. R&D does not scale with quarterly tool shipments - it's a strategic investment. Similarly, ASML has significant capital expenditures on its own facilities (cleanrooms, demo labs) - though these are modest relative to revenue (CapEx in 2024 was €2.61B, about 9% of sales). Depreciation of these facilities (and amortization of capitalized development costs, if any) is another fixed cost. **Manufacturing overhead**(running cleanrooms, maintaining precision equipment to assemble tools) is also largely fixed. For example, ASML's Eindhoven and Veldhoven factories must be maintained and climate-controlled regardless of whether they ship 30 or 50 tools in a quarter. This fixed-cost leverage means ASML's profitability improves dramatically when volume is high - overhead per machine goes down. Conversely, in periods of low demand, ASML's gross and operating margins compress because these fixed costs get spread over fewer units. We saw this in 2019: ASML shipped 229 new systems vs 207 in 2018, but those included some lower-end models and operating profit margin fell as certain fixed costs remained (2019 op margin \~24%, down from \~29.5% in 2018, partly due to EUV R&D and overhead still ramping).

-   **Material and Component Costs:** A significant variable cost is the advanced components ASML buys. This includes precision lenses and mirrors (sourced exclusively from Carl Zeiss SMT in Germany - ASML even took a 24.9% stake in Zeiss SMT to secure this supply), lasers (from companies like Cymer, which ASML acquired in 2013 for DUV lasers, and Gigaphoton or Trumpf for EUV light sources), sensors, and ultra-clean ceramics, among others. These parts are expensive and can even fluctuate in cost if supply is tight. However, ASML often has long-term supply agreements; for instance, it co-invests with suppliers to ensure capacity (the Zeiss stake is a prime example). So while materials are a variable cost per unit, ASML has some control - it is more constrained by availability than price swings, since few alternatives exist and it effectively single-sources from aligned partners.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** ASML's marketing expenses are relatively **minimal** in the context of its financials. This is a B2B business where sales are relationship-driven and highly technical. ASML does not need mass-market advertising; their "marketing" is mainly participating in industry conferences, hosting investor days, and engaging in one-on-one negotiations with the handful of chip manufacturers. Selling expenses (part of SG&A) include things like sales engineers, customer training, and after-sales support infrastructure. In 2024, total SG&A was around €0.84B (just \~3% of sales) - indicating how low marketing/sales spend is relative to revenue (compare that to R&D at \~16% or COGS \~49%). We can infer pure advertising spend is negligible; ASML's **brand and market position** carry weight automatically in the industry (if you're a chipmaker, you already know ASML - "marketing" is built-in through technical reputation). One exception might be employer branding - ASML invests in outreach to attract engineers (for example, sponsoring tech events, local initiatives, even sports in the Brainport Eindhoven region) - but again these costs are small.

-   **Most Significant Costs:** The most significant costs for ASML are **R&D** and **manufacturing costs (materials + overhead)**. In terms of the P&L, COGS (materials, manufacturing labor, factory costs) was \~49% of revenue in 2024, and R&D was \~16%. Together, these dominate the cost structure. To break it down: Out of €28.3B revenue in 2024, COGS was \~€13.8B, R&D \~€4.6B, SG&A \~€0.8B. So materials/components (\~€7-8B) and personnel (manufacturing + R&D staff compensation) are the bulk of expenses. Another way to see significance: **Gross margin** (which reflects materials and production efficiency) is \~50%, whereas **operating margin** is \~32%. The 18-point drop is almost entirely R&D and SG&A. Thus, *material costs and R&D* are the critical cost levers for ASML's profitability.

-   **Cost Trends Over Time & in Different Environments:** Over the past decade, ASML's cost structure has scaled but also evolved with product mix. We have seen **gross margin rise** from mid-40s% to over 50% as volume grew and EUV production matured. During boom times (e.g. 2021's 33% revenue growth), ASML's fixed cost absorption was excellent - gross margin hit 52.7%, and operating margin over 30%. In slower times, margins dip (2019 GM 44.7%, op margin \~24% as noted). ASML tends to maintain R&D through cycles, which means in a downturn the operating margin compresses more (as happened in 2019 when net income was flat on higher R&D, and in 2023 when R&D rose \~20% despite slower sales). In high-demand periods, that R&D is rewarded with outsized profit growth.

In different economic conditions, **ASML's costs behave as follows**: In a downturn, it has limited ability to cut short-term costs - it won't slash R&D (doing so would jeopardize future tech leadership), and it has commitments to suppliers (for long lead-time components) that it generally upholds to preserve relationships. It may slow new hiring or use up inventory (reducing purchasing temporarily), but fundamentally costs are mostly fixed or strategic. This can lead to temporary margin compression, as seen in the slight EPS dip in 2022 despite revenue growth (\~flat net income due to heavy R&D and some one-time charges). In an upturn, those fixed costs become a boon - revenue can surge without a proportional rise in R&D or SG&A, boosting margins and returns (e.g., net income jumped \~65% in 2021 on 33% sales growth).

**Bottom line:** ASML's cost structure is characterized by *high fixed costs (R&D, overhead, highly skilled labor)* and substantial variable costs per machine (specialized components). The most influential costs are the technology investments (R&D) and the precision manufacturing costs. Over time, ASML has improved its cost per unit through learning curve and volume, which, combined with its pricing power, has steadily lifted gross margins. It spends aggressively on R&D regardless of cycles, reflecting a long-term focus. This means in lean years ASML's earnings may dip as it "bites the bullet" on maintaining costs, but in growth years it reaps the rewards of scale. This approach has been effective, as evidenced by strong profitability across cycles and sustained technological leadership.

## **Management & Governance**

**Executive Team & Board (Current and Historical):** ASML's management team blends long-term company veterans with new leadership for its next phase. As of 2025, **Christophe Fouquet** is President & CEO (he succeeded Peter Wennink in 2025) . Fouquet has been with ASML for years (he previously led EUV product marketing and corporate strategy) and was a natural internal successor when Wennink retired. **Roger Dassen** is the CFO (since 2018) - he was formerly a partner at Deloitte and has overseen ASML's finance during its recent growth spurt. The broader executive team includes Martin van den Brink (a 35+ year ASML veteran who served as CTO and was instrumental in EUV development) - van den Brink is considered a "founding father" of ASML's technology and remains on the Board of Management as Vice Chairman. Other key executives are Fredrick Schneider (Chief Operations Officer) and the heads of product sectors like EUV, DUV, etc. On the **Board of Directors (Supervisory Board)**, **Nils Andersen** serves as Chairman. The board includes industry and finance figures; for example, former executives from Dutch tech firms and international experts. Importantly, ASML's governance structure is two-tier (common in the Netherlands): the Board of Management (executives) runs day-to-day, while the Supervisory Board oversees and advises. ASML's historical leadership is notable: the company's previous CEO, **Peter Wennink** (CEO from 2013-2025), was actually ASML's CFO before taking the helm and had joined ASML in 1999. Wennink, along with van den Brink, guided ASML through the critical EUV investment years. ASML was originally founded as a joint venture by Philips and ASM International in 1984 - its early leaders came from those parent companies (e.g., co-founder and first CEO Arthur del Prado from ASM). Though no founders are actively managing the company now, the spirit of long-term innovation set by them continues.

Overall, ASML's top management has **extensive company tenure**. Many have spent decades at ASML, which provides continuity. There is also continuity in strategy: Peter Wennink was succeeded by an internal leader (Fouquet) to ensure a smooth transition - similar to how Wennink himself continued the work of his predecessor Eric Meurice in pushing EUV. The company's culture has been described as *"technology-driven and collaborative"*, and much of the senior team either grew up in that culture or were brought in to support it.

**Management Track Record & Transparency:** ASML's management has generally delivered on the promises it makes to investors. For example, several years ago ASML laid out a 2020-2025 growth scenario (at Investor Day) predicting significant revenue expansion; so far they have achieved much of it (with \~€28B sales in 2024 and targeting €30-35B in 2025). When it comes to product development goals, management has been **fairly transparent about challenges**. EUV is a case in point: it was delayed for years beyond initial hopes, but ASML kept investors informed through regular updates on source power, throughput milestones, etc. It did not hide the fact that EUV was consuming massive R&D and that initial adoption would be slow. Now, EUV is paying off, as management foresaw. Analysts often ask tough questions on ASML's earnings calls about things like order push-outs or gross margin pressure; management (Wennink and Dassen in recent years) have answered candidly. For instance, they openly discussed how in 2023 some memory customers delayed shipments and how that affected the mix and margins, rather than obscuring it. ASML also provides mid-quarter updates when necessary, like pre-announcing if a significant event (supplier fire or new export rule) might alter their outlook. There's little evidence of ASML's management hiding problems - on the contrary, they tend to address issues directly (e.g., acknowledging the IP theft incident by a China-based employee in 2023 and reinforcing data security measures).

On **analyst calls**, ASML's management has a reputation for being *straightforward and technically deep*. Longtime CEO Peter Wennink was known for detailed explanations (he himself is an accountant by background but extremely knowledgeable on the tech as well). The new CEO Christophe Fouquet, coming from the technical side, similarly can answer in-depth on product timelines. They typically give comprehensive answers rather than evasive ones - for example, detailing how many EUV layers customers are using or what utilization rates look like, which not all companies would disclose in detail.

**Management Stability and Integrity:** The core leadership (van den Brink, Wennink, etc.) has been with ASML for decades, instilling confidence in their commitment. Turnover at the top has been low. The CFO position changed in 2018 when Roger Dassen took over (the prior CFO became a board member), but that was a planned succession. ## **DCF Analysis**

To estimate ASML's intrinsic value, we performed a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** analysis under three scenarios - **Conservative**, **Nominal (base case)**, and **Optimistic** - based on differing assumptions for free cash flow (FCF) growth. We use ASML's recent free cash flow as a starting point and apply growth rates derived from its historical performance:

-   **FCF Baseline:** ASML's free cash flow in 2024 was approximately **€8.56 billion** (operating cash flow €11.17B minus capital expenditures €2.61B). For ease of analysis, we'll work in USD (assuming parity for simplicity, the figures are similar; actual conversion at $1.07/€ would adjust values, but not ratios). We take **$9 billion** as the starting annual FCF (roughly the USD equivalent of €8.56B).

-   **Growth Rates:** Over the past decade, ASML's FCF grew rapidly, but with variability. The *lowest* annual FCF growth was negative (e.g., -63% from 2022 to 2023 due to working capital). Excluding anomalous drops, a low but positive growth might be in the mid-single digits. The *average* FCF (or earnings) growth has been \~20% annually (revenue CAGR \~21%, net income \~21% CAGR as well), reflecting secular trends and EUV ramp. The *highest*sustained growth occurred in periods like 2016-2021 where FCF spiked - e.g., FCF jumped \~150% from 2019 to 2021, but that's not annualized. If we consider net income, the biggest jump was +65% in 2021. So a reasonable "optimistic" FCF growth might use something around that level for early years, though sustaining it is unrealistic. Instead, we interpret "optimistic case uses best past growth" as using the highest historical annual growth as a constant - which is aggressive. For ASML, that might be \~**25-30%** (since 2021 saw +65%, but that was extraordinary; 2017 saw +43%, 2018 +22%, 2023 +39%). We choose **25%** for optimistic scenario, which assumes ASML can sustain a very high growth akin to its best multi-year run. For the nominal scenario, we'll use roughly the 10-year average \~**15%** FCF growth (in line with long-term industry drivers, albeit a bit lower than historical to account for size). For conservative, we take a cautious figure - ASML rarely grows &lt;10% except in downturns, but to be safe, we'll use **5%**, reflecting a scenario of significant slowdown (perhaps due to cyclicality or maturity).

-   **DCF Parameters:** We assume these growth rates apply for a finite horizon (say 10 years), then revert to a stable **terminal growth** of **3%** (a bit above inflation, reflecting secular demand for chips). We use a discount rate (required return) of **10%**, consistent with a typical equity hurdle for a company of this quality (some might argue lower for ASML given stability, but 10% is standard for margin of safety in valuation). All cash flows are taken after tax and reinvestment (FCF), so this directly values the equity (we add no debt since net debt is \~zero).

Now, **Scenario 1: Conservative Case** - *Free Cash Flow grows at only 5% annually* (perhaps reflecting a worst-case of saturation or prolonged downturn):

-   Year 1 FCF: $9.0B; Year 2: $9.45B; … Year 10: \~$14.66B (5% compounding for 10 years).

-   Terminal Year 11 FCF: $14.66B \* 1.03 = $15.10B, then divided by (10%-3%) = *Terminal value* of \~$215.8B (present value in year 10).

-   We discount each year's FCF and terminal value back at 10%. Summing these yields an **intrinsic equity value** of approximately **$157 billion** in present value terms.

-   Dividing by \~0.39 billion shares gives **$403 per share** intrinsic value. In euros, around €380/share.

-   Compare to current market price (around $985/€930). In this conservative scenario, the stock is **overvalued** - intrinsic \~$403 vs market \~$985. There is *no margin of safety*; rather the market price is \~2.4× the conservative intrinsic value. A **25% margin of safety price** would be 0.75 \* $403 ≈ **$302/share** (far below current levels). So by this case, ASML would need to drop \~70% to hit a buy zone - indicating the conservative scenario is *very pessimistic* relative to market expectations.

*(Interpretation: 5% FCF growth is akin to ASML stagnating - market clearly assumes higher growth than that. This scenario might correspond to a world where semiconductor advances slow dramatically or geopolitical issues severely cap ASML's growth. It's a low-probability scenario given industry trends, but we consider it for thoroughness.)*

**Scenario 2: Nominal/Base Case** - *Free Cash Flow grows at 15% annually* (closer to ASML's past average growth, assuming continued technology transitions like High-NA EUV, but accounting for some eventual slowing):

-   Year 1 FCF: $9.0B; Year 5: $9.0 \* (1.15)^5 ≈ $18.1B; Year 10: ≈ $36.4B.

The **margin of safety** in this scenario is negative (-16%). The price would need to be 25% below $850, which is \~$638/share, to have a 25% MOS. Thus, at \~$985, the stock is \~55% higher than the "buy target" of \~$638 in this moderate scenario.

In fact, the price could rise to \~$1,316 (25% below $1,755) and still offer a 25% MOS. So at $985, one could deem ASML a bargain if one believes this high-growth scenario.

*(Interpretation: This scenario assumes ASML's revenue and cash flow explode due to perhaps multiple technology inflections - e.g., AI, 5G, automotive chips all drive massive capacity expansions, plus ASML's High-NA EUV is a huge success commanding even higher prices, etc. In such a scenario, ASML's sales might hit \~$100B by 2034 with very high margins. **Step-by-Step Calculation Explanation:**

To elaborate the calculation mechanics for one case (nominal 15%):

1.  We started with $9.0B FCF at year 0 (2024).

2.  We projected each year's FCF = prior year FCF \* 1.15. For example, year1 = 9.0*1.15 = $10.35B; year2 = 10.35*1.15 = $11.90B; … up to year10 ≈ $36.4B.

3.  Terminal value at end of year10 = FCF\_year11 / (r - g\_terminal). We took year11 FCF = year10 \* (1+3%) = \~$37.5B. With r=10%, g=3%, TV = 37.5 / 0.07 = $535.7B.

4.  We then discounted each annual FCF and the TV:

    -   Present Value (PV) of year1 FCF = 10.35 / (1.10)^1 = $9.41B.

    -   Year2 PV = 11.90 / (1.10)^2 = $9.83B.

    -   …

    -   Year10 PV = 36.4 / (1.10)^10 = $14.06B.

    -   PV of Terminal = 535.7 / (1.10)^10 = $206.7B.

5.  Summing all these PVs = total intrinsic equity value. In this case, the sum came to \~$329.6B (as computed above)【54†】. We can round to $330B.

6.  Dividing by 0.388 billion shares = $330B / 0.388B ≈ $850/share.

We repeated the process with growth=5% and 25%. For 5%, the sum PV was \~$156.9B【54†】, dividing by shares \~ $403/share intrinsic. For 25%, sum PV \~$684.7B【54†】, per share \~$1,755.
