## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Lululemon's latest earnings (Q2 FY2025) showed **mixed results**. Net revenue grew \~7% year-over-year to $2.53 billion (slightly below estimates of $2.54 billion) . Earnings per share (EPS) were $3.10, beating forecasts of $2.87 by \~8% . **Gross profit** was $1.48 billion (58.5% gross margin) and **net income** $371 million . Despite the EPS beat, the stock *tumbled \~17%* on concerns over the slight sales miss and cautious outlook . Management updated full-year guidance: FY2025 revenue $10.85-$11.0 billion (+2-4% YoY) and EPS $12.77-$12.97 , factoring in a decline of \~1-2% in U.S. sales but robust growth in China and other international markets . Key short-term challenges mentioned include softer U.S. demand for premium athletic wear, tariff cost pressures, and intense athleisure competition . Nonetheless, Lululemon continues expanding globally (added 14 new stores in Q2, bringing total stores to 784) and seeing strong digital sales (39% of revenue, \~$1 billion in Q2) .

*Management Commentary:* CEO Calvin McDonald acknowledged U.S. weaknesses and emphasized refocusing on product innovation and guest experience, stating *"We know we can do better for our guests…that's the focus"* . CFO Meghan Frank noted initiatives to offset higher tariffs in both near and long term . Overall, while international and e-commerce segments delivered solid growth, Lululemon faces near-term headwinds in its core North American market.

## **Business Model**

Lululemon is a **premium athletic apparel retailer** with an omnichannel model. Its primary income comes from selling technical athletic wear (yoga pants, leggings, tops, etc.) and related products. In fiscal 2024, product sales in **company-operated retail stores** and the **direct-to-consumer (online) channel** were the main revenue streams . **Stores** contributed \~45.8% of revenue ($4.4 billion) and **e-commerce (DTC)** about 40.6% ($3.9 billion) . The remainder (\~13.6%, $1.3 billion) came from *"Other"* sources such as wholesale partnerships, outlets, and Lululemon Studio (formerly Mirror) subscriptions . *(See pie chart below.)*

In terms of product mix, **women's apparel** (yoga pants, sports bras, etc.) is the core offering, alongside a growing **men's line** and **accessories** (yoga mats, bags, footwear) . Lululemon also leverages **community and services** - e.g. free in-store yoga classes and events - to drive customer engagement and loyalty .

**Customer Base:** Lululemon has a loyal following of "guests." While exact active customer counts aren't publicly disclosed, the company's *membership programs* indicate a large user base. The free loyalty tier reportedly reached **\~9 million members** by mid-2024 . These are highly engaged shoppers drawn by Lululemon's brand ethos of wellness and quality. Lululemon targets affluent, fitness-oriented consumers (initially women, now men as well) primarily in North America, but international customers (especially in China) are a fast-growing segment .

**Units/Subscriptions:** In addition to apparel units sold (not explicitly reported), Lululemon ventured into at-home fitness via its 2020 acquisition of **Mirror**. Rebranded as *Lululemon Studio*, this interactive mirror device and subscription service had only "tens of thousands" of subscribers as of 2020 and struggled to scale. (Lululemon took a $443 million charge related to Mirror in 2022 .) As such, the vast majority of Lululemon's revenue still comes from selling merchandise rather than recurring subscriptions.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

**Short-Term (Cyclical) Headwinds:** In the near term, Lululemon faces macroeconomic and industry pressures that could *temporarily* impact results. **Consumer spending shifts** amid inflation and recession fears are affecting discretionary apparel sales - notably a *"declining U.S. market for premium athletic wear"* has been observed . Lululemon noted a slight drop in U.S. sales as shoppers pull back on high-priced leggings . Additionally, **elevated costs and tariffs** on imports from Asia are squeezing margins . Management specifically cited increased U.S. import tariffs as a headwind, requiring mitigation efforts . **Supply chain disruptions** (shipping delays, factory shutdowns) also pose short-term risk to inventory flow - though these pressures have moderated since 2021-22. Lastly, **intense competition** in athleisure (from Nike, Athleta, etc.) means heavier promotions industry-wide, which could pressure Lululemon's near-term sales or require more marketing spend .

**Longer-Term (Structural) Risks:** Over the long run, several factors could *permanently* influence Lululemon's trajectory:

-   **Market Saturation:** In its core North American market, Lululemon's store footprint is fairly mature and the brand already commands a large share of the high-end yoga apparel niche. Future growth requires expansion into new categories (e.g. footwear) or regions (international). There is a risk that U.S. growth slows structurally as the market saturates or fashion trends shift.

-   **Global Expansion Execution:** Lululemon's ambitious growth plan (*"Power of Three ×2"* goal to double revenue by 2026) hinges on international markets like China. Geopolitical or regulatory changes (tariffs, trade tensions) and differing consumer preferences are long-term risks abroad. For example, China now contributes \~12% of revenue and grew 30%+ in 2024 , but reliance on China introduces exposure to its economic and political climate.

-   **Competition & Brand Heat:** Athleisure may become more crowded or lose favor. Entrants from both **upmarket competitors** (e.g. Alo Yoga, Vuori) and **value players** offering athletic wear could erode Lululemon's pricing power over time. If Lululemon fails to innovate new products or if its brand cachet fades, customers might switch - a persistent risk in fashion retail.

-   **Product & Sourcing Risks:** Lululemon has faced product-quality crises before (e.g. the 2013 "sheer pants" recall). A significant quality control issue or a failure in anticipating style trends could permanently damage brand reputation. Moreover, Lululemon's production is outsourced (largely in Asia); any long-term disruption in supplier countries or raw material (specialty fabric) cost inflation could structurally impact margins.

In summary, while Lululemon enjoys strong brand loyalty, its long-run growth requires navigating saturated home markets, effectively scaling overseas, and maintaining its premium edge amid evolving consumer tastes and competitors.

## **Cost Structure**

Lululemon's cost structure comprises both **variable costs** (that scale with sales) and **fixed operating expenses**. Key cost components include:

-   **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS):** These are mostly variable costs - manufacturing the apparel (materials, factory labor, import duties). Lululemon uses high-quality technical fabrics (e.g. Luon, Nulu) sourced from suppliers, so raw material and production costs are significant. In 2024, COGS was \~$4.32 billion, about 40.8% of revenue , yielding a gross margin near 59%. Thus, on average a $100 Lululemon garment might cost \~$41 to produce. *Cost per unit* depends on the item (leggings might cost &lt;$20 to make but retail for $98+). These costs vary with volume and input prices - e.g. higher oil prices (for synthetic fabric) or tariffs can raise unit costs.

-   **Labor & Staffing:** As a retailer, Lululemon has substantial payroll expenses. Store staff wages, benefits, and corporate salaries are **semi-variable** costs (in stores, labor can be adjusted with sales traffic to an extent, but corporate overhead is fixed). Lululemon is known for extensive employee training and relatively higher store labor costs to provide a premium experience. These costs fall under **SG&A (Selling, General & Administrative)**. In 2024, SG&A was $3.76 billion (\~35.5% of revenue) . This includes store rents and staff, head office, and community marketing. Notably, as Lululemon opens more stores, total labor costs rise, but productivity gains can improve the **labor cost per store** metric over time.

-   **Rent & Occupancy:** Retail store leases are a **fixed cost** (typically multi-year leases). With 600+ stores globally in 2025, lease expenses form a large fixed outlay. Even during sales downturns, Lululemon must pay rent - which can impact profit in lean times. Occupancy (rent, utilities, maintenance) is part of SG&A. During 2020's lockdowns, for example, sales fell but fixed rent costs remained, squeezing margins. However, Lululemon negotiates prime locations and its strong sales per square foot (\~$1,600 in 2024, one of the highest in retail ) help absorb these fixed costs.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** Historically, Lululemon spent relatively little on traditional advertising, relying on grassroots marketing and word-of-mouth. (The founder famously *"slammed advertising"* and preferred community-building .) As a result, direct ad spend has been modest - estimated at \~2% of revenue or less, though one source speculated it might be up to 18% if broad definitions are used (likely overstated). Marketing costs (brand ambassadors, digital campaigns) are included in SG&A but are not the largest component. For instance, in a recent quarter, Lululemon invested in performance marketing but also acknowledged a shift to more efficient digital ad spending . Overall, **marketing expenses are not a major drag** relative to peers, thanks to Lululemon's strong brand that attracts customers organically.

-   **Depreciation & Amortization:** This non-cash expense (\~$446 million in 2024) reflects capital investments (store build-outs, IT systems) being expensed over time . As a percent of gross profit, depreciation was \~7% , comfortably below the 10% benchmark for asset intensity - indicating the business isn't overly burdened by capital costs.

**Most Significant Costs:** The biggest cost line items for Lululemon are *COGS* (i.e. the cost to produce inventory) and *SG&A* (particularly store operations). Together, these determine operating margin. In 2024, gross margin was \~59.2% and operating margin \~23.7% , so roughly:

-   40% of revenue goes to product costs,

-   36% to operating expenses (rent, payroll, marketing, HQ),

-   leaving \~24% operating profit.

Among these, **inventory costs** and **store labor/occupancy** are the dominant expenses. For example, rising labor wages or rent (inflation) can meaningfully impact SG&A. Indeed, management has warned that wage inflation or higher input costs could compress operating margins if not offset .

**Cost Trends:** Over time, Lululemon has generally maintained *strong cost discipline*. Gross margin dipped to \~55% in FY2022 (due to supply chain costs and Mirror write-downs), but rebounded to record highs near 59-60% by FY2024 . This suggests pricing power and sourcing efficiencies. SG&A as a % of sales has held around mid-30s%. In **economic downturns**, variable costs like raw materials may be managed (e.g. negotiating lower factory rates, cutting back on lower-ROI marketing), and the company can slow hiring. During **boom times**, Lululemon invests in growth - e.g. expanding store count (increasing fixed lease costs) and building infrastructure (distribution centers, digital platforms). A notable trend is **operating leverage**: as sales grew \~5x from 2010s, Lululemon's SG&A ratio improved from \~40% down to \~35%, reflecting better scale economies .

However, external swings like tariff changes can suddenly raise costs - Lululemon has cautioned that ongoing tariffs on Chinese-made goods could hurt margins if not mitigated by pricing or cost savings . Additionally, in high-growth phases (like doubling international stores), upfront fixed costs will rise faster than sales in new markets, temporarily pressuring margins.

In summary, Lululemon's cost structure is well-managed with high margins, but vigilant control of product costs and store-level efficiency remains crucial to sustain profitability through varying economic conditions.

## **Management & Governance**

**Executive Team:** Lululemon is led by **CEO Calvin McDonald**, who joined in 2018. McDonald had a retail background (Sephora, Sears Canada) and has steered Lululemon through global expansion and the Mirror acquisition. The **CFO is Meghan Frank**, appointed 2020 as Lululemon's first female CFO; she oversees financial strategy and capital allocation. Other key executives include **Sun Choe (Chief Product Officer)** until mid-2022, succeeded by **Pedro Lacerda** in product leadership, and **Nikki Neuburger (Chief Brand Officer)**. The team also features heads of international (Andre Maestrini) and digital channels. The **Board of Directors** (12 members) is chaired by Glenn Murphy (ex-Gap CEO) and includes retail veterans Emily White, Martha Morfitt, and international tech leader Isabel Ge Mahe . In the 2024 shareholder meeting, all director nominees were elected with broad support .

**Historical Leadership:** Lululemon's founder, **Dennis "Chip" Wilson**, is a significant figure - he founded the company in 1998 and served as CEO until 2005, then as Chairman until 2013. Chip drove the brand's yoga-centric ethos but also courted controversy (e.g. remarks blaming women's body shapes for pants issues in 2013). He stepped down from the board in 2015 amid those controversies. Two prior CEOs, **Christine Day** (2008-2013) and **Laurent Potdevin** (2014-2018), guided Lululemon through IPO growth and early international moves. Potdevin departed abruptly in 2018 due to misconduct allegations, which Lululemon disclosed and addressed rather than covering up - indicating a commitment to governance transparency. Since then, Calvin McDonald has provided stable leadership.

**Track Record & Integrity:** The current management appears to **follow through on targets**. For instance, in 2019 they set a five-year goal to double revenue by 2023 - and achieved \~10.6 billion in 2024, roughly on track . They have generally been candid with investors, even when facing issues. An example: in 2022 when Mirror underperformed, management took impairment charges and shifted strategy, rather than obscuring the problem. On earnings calls, McDonald and Frank are known to answer analysts' questions in detail, addressing product mix, inventory levels, and margin outlook with transparency . The tone is optimistic but realistic; for example, McDonald openly acknowledged U.S. softness and the need to refocus on core women's products .

The executive team's **tenure** is relatively recent but experienced. McDonald has \~5 years at LULU; CFO Frank \~3 years as CFO (joined company in 2016). Other execs have 2-4 years with LULU. While not "long-time" veterans of Lululemon (which is still a young company), they bring external expertise and have been cohesive so far. There have been no major integrity scandals involving the new leadership. In fact, governance observers note Lululemon's improved culture after the founder's exit, with fewer controversial statements and a more inclusive brand stance (though a shareholder proposal in 2024 did raise the issue of animal-derived products, which management opposed and shareholders voted down) .

**Value Alignment:** Founder Dennis Wilson remains the largest individual shareholder (owns \~15% of shares) , aligning his interests with investors, though he's no longer on the board. Among current execs, Calvin McDonald holds shares (he's made occasional insider buys - e.g. purchasing \~$4.83 million worth in June 2025 at \~$137 ). Insiders collectively own \~26% of LULU , a high percentage indicating **skin in the game**. This includes large stakes held by founder-controlled entities and private equity (Advent International still holds \~9.4%) . Management's compensation also skews toward stock performance. For instance, the 2024 Say-on-Pay was approved by shareholders , reflecting confidence that exec pay (which includes equity awards) is tied to results. During short-term stock declines, insiders have both sold and bought: in the last 12 months there was net selling (some profit-taking when shares were higher) , but McDonald's significant purchase during the 2025 dip signals confidence . Overall, executives stand to gain far more from Lululemon's long-term growth (via stock value) than from their salaries, suggesting their incentives align with building shareholder value.

In summary, Lululemon's leadership has transitioned from its founder's colorful era to a more **professional management era**. The current team has executed well on growth and generally maintains credibility with investors. They are reasonably **aligned with shareholders** through ownership stakes and have shown a willingness to adapt (pivoting Mirror strategy, expanding internationally) rather than clinging to ego or hiding issues. Continued success will depend on how they steer Lululemon through its next phase - but so far, governance checks (like shareholder approvals and independent board oversight) present a well-run company.

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns**

**Major Shareholders:** Lululemon's ownership is institutionally heavy - about 84% of shares are held by institutions, 16% by insiders (founder/execs), and effectively 0% by small retail investors . The largest stakeholder is founder **Dennis "Chip" Wilson** with \~15% . He retains a substantial share valued over $3.5 billion , though he no longer has board influence. Among institutional owners, **Vanguard Group** holds \~11.6%, **Fidelity (FMR LLC)** \~10.3%, **BlackRock**\~8.7%, and **State Street** \~4.1% . Additionally, **Advent International**, a private equity firm that was an early investor, still owns \~9.4% (likely as part of a shareholder group including Highland Capital \~3.3% ). These top holders generally vote with management on routine issues.

At the 2025 Annual Meeting (June 11, 2025), shareholders *approved all management proposals* . They elected directors and ratified PwC as auditor with broad support . The advisory vote on executive pay ("say on pay") also passed by majority . Notably, a **shareholder proposal** to require a report on the impact of using animal-sourced materials (like down and wool) was defeated . Large institutions like Vanguard and BlackRock typically follow proxy advisor recommendations; in this case, they likely sided with management in opposing that proposal, indicating they prioritize the business's discretion in product materials. No shareholder proposals on political neutrality or discrimination (as have been seen at some companies) passed either.

The insider ownership (particularly Chip Wilson's stake) means insiders could influence certain votes. However, Wilson has not engaged in activist actions publicly since stepping down. In 2014, he did agitate for changes (bringing Laurent Potdevin as CEO), but since selling part of his stake to Advent and leaving, governance has been stable. **Top institutional investors** have largely been passive - there's no known activist investor pushing for changes at LULU, given its strong performance historically.

**Voting Topics and Stances:** Recent key votes have included:

-   Board elections: All nominees (including CEO McDonald) have been elected with *&gt;90%* support typically .

-   Executive compensation: \~95% approval in 2024, reflecting investor satisfaction with performance alignment.

-   Share repurchase authorization: Investors welcomed increases in buyback programs (discussed below).

-   Shareholder proposals: The environmental/animal-welfare report proposal in 2024 got insufficient votes (exact breakdown not disclosed, but likely under 5% voted in favor, indicating major holders were against it in line with management).

In summary, **shareholder sentiment is generally positive**. The combination of significant insider ownership and strong institutional support yields little contention in votes. The top shareholders seem focused on long-term growth and have trusted management's strategy (expanding globally, minimal dividend, using buybacks). They have not pressed issues like splitting the chairman/CEO role or other governance changes - likely because Lululemon already has an independent board chair (Murphy) and good performance. Should performance falter significantly, one of these major holders could become more vocal, but as of now the status quo is supported.

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns (continued)**

**Share Buyback Program:** Lululemon has actively used share repurchases to return capital to shareholders, especially given it does not pay a dividend. In March 2023, the board authorized a new multi-year buyback program. Then in June 2024, after a strong Q1, the company *expanded the repurchase authorization by an additional $1 billion* . This indicates confidence in LULU's financial position and a commitment to returning excess cash. The current program (as of 2025) totals roughly $1.2 billion remaining capacity. For context, in FY2024 Lululemon repurchased about $500 million of its stock , reducing the share count \~3%.

Over the past 10 years, **share count has steadily declined** - from \~140 million shares in 2015 to \~121 million by 2025 . This reflects ongoing buybacks. For example:

-   In 2017-2019, Lululemon repurchased shares to offset dilution from option exercises, keeping share count flat to slightly down.

-   In 2020-2021, buyback activity paused (focus was on investing in Mirror acquisition and preserving cash during COVID).

-   In 2022-2023, Lululemon resumed significant repurchases as cash flow surged. The share count dropped \~1-3% each year from 2021 through 2024 .

The **magnitude of recent buybacks** is notable. The extra $1 billion buyback announced in 2024 came when the share price was around $320-$340 , indicating management saw value in its stock after a pullback from 2021 highs. By Q3 2025, LULU shares fell further (to \~$170), making buybacks even more accretive. Lululemon's board likely views repurchases as the preferred way to return capital given the company's growth (they want to retain flexibility rather than commit to a fixed dividend).

Comparatively, in the early 2010s Lululemon did smaller buybacks (tens of millions) mostly to neutralize dilution. The last few years have seen larger, more active repurchases - *peak capital return via buybacks*. For instance, after Q1 2024's earnings beat, Lululemon immediately announced the $1 billion expansion, which was well-received by the market .

**Capital Return Philosophy:** Lululemon still prioritizes growth investments (opening stores, developing new products) - it spent \~$689 million on CapEx in 2024 . But with annual free cash flow around $1.5-1.6 billion , the company can fund growth and still have excess cash for buybacks. They have **no dividend**, signaling that management believes buybacks (at the right price) are a better way to enhance shareholder value for a growth company. Major shareholders like BlackRock likely favor this approach for tax efficiency.

In conclusion, Lululemon's current buyback plan is robust - repurchasing shares regularly and opportunistically. Over 10 years, these buybacks have reduced share count \~15% , boosting EPS growth. The trend accelerated in recent years as cash generation grew. Shareholders have supported these repurchases (as seen by the approval of additional authorizations). This sustained buyback activity reflects management and the board's confidence in Lululemon's long-term prospects and a commitment to return surplus cash to shareholders when appropriate.

## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

Warren Buffett's investment criteria for a "great company at a fair price" can be applied to Lululemon:

1.  **Valuation Discipline:**

    -   *P/E ≤ 15:* Lululemon's current price-to-earnings ratio is around **11-12** (after the 2025 price drop) , which is well under Buffett's 15× benchmark. However, historically LULU traded at much higher P/Es (30-50+ most years) , reflecting growth expectations. Only with the recent plunge has P/E become ≤15. **✓ Pass (currently)**, but **Historically Fail** (it was usually overvalued by this metric).

    -   *P/B ≤ 1.5:* Lululemon's price-to-book is far above 1.5 in most periods - typically in the teens. Even now, after the decline, P/B is about **4.7** . This exceeds Buffett's traditional cutoff. LULU's high ROE explains a high P/B, but strictly speaking **✗ Fail** on P/B (stock never traded near book value).

    -   *Margin of Safety (≥25% below intrinsic value):* We estimate intrinsic value via DCF below - for the conservative case it's around $235/share (see DCF section). The current price \~$170 is about 28% below that, implying a modest margin of safety. Under nominal and optimistic cases, the margin is even larger. Thus at today's price LULU likely offers a ≥25% MOS relative to a reasonable intrinsic value. **✓ Pass (currently)**.

2.  **Cash Generation & Allocation:**

    -   *Positive Free Cash Flow each year & FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:* Lululemon has **generated positive free cash flow every year for at least the past decade**, except for a dip in 2022 when FCF was still positive $0.33B (4% of revenue) . Typically FCF margin is strong - averaging \~10-15%+ . For example, 2023 FCF was $1.64B = 17.1% of sales . This easily exceeds the 5% threshold. **✓ Pass.**

    -   *Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:* This measures how well earnings reinvested translate into growth. Lululemon retained almost all earnings (no dividends) and grew net income from \~$0.23B in 2015 to $1.81B in 2024 , an increase of \~$1.58B. Over 2015-2023, cumulative retained earnings totaled \~$6.0B (sum of annual net incomes). The return on that is \~26% ($1.58B / $6.0B). Even adjusting for buybacks (which returned some earnings to shareholders), RORE is well above 12%. Each dollar LULU reinvested has yielded about $0.26 additional annual earnings - an excellent rate. **✓ Pass.**

3.  **Growth & Predictability:**

    -   *EPS Record (Positive & rising every year for 10 yrs):* Lululemon's **EPS has been positive each year**. It grew markedly over the decade (FY2015 EPS \~$1.66 to FY2024 $14.98 adjusted). However, it did *not rise every single year*. EPS dipped in FY2017 vs 2016 (slightly) and in FY2018 (due to U.S. tax changes) . Also, FY2022 GAAP EPS fell due to Mirror charges. So EPS trend is up overall but with a couple of one-off declines. **✗ Partial Fail** (not a straight 10-year rise, though CAGR is strong).

    -   *Book Value/Share 10-year CAGR ≥ 10%, no year of decline:* LULU's book value per share grew from \~$7 in 2014 to \~$35 in 2024 - \~17% CAGR. However, book value/share *did dip* in a few years (e.g. FY2016 and FY2019 saw slight declines in equity due to buybacks and AOCI impacts ). For instance, BVPS went from $7.30 in 2014 to $6.86 in 2015 (down due to share repurchases exceeding retained income). So while growth is well above 10% annually, there were 2-3 years where book value/share contracted. **✗ Partial Fail**.

    -   *Owner's Earnings positive every year & OE/Market Cap ≥ 10%:* Owner's Earnings (Buffett's proxy for true cash earnings) can be approximated as FCF + growth capex adjustments. Lululemon's owner's earnings have been positive each year (closely tracking FCF, which was always &gt;0). However, as a percentage of market cap, it was usually low when the stock was expensive. For example, FY2023 owner's earnings \~$1.5B vs market cap \~$64B = \~2.3%. Even now, OE \~$1.5B vs market cap \~$20B = \~7.5%. This is **below 10%** yield. So LULU's earnings yield hasn't met the 10% threshold historically or even currently (with the stock around $170, OE yield \~7-8%). **✗ Fail**.

4.  **Balance Sheet Safety:**

    -   *Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:* Lululemon has a very conservative balance sheet. Its D/E is \~0.33 (2024: $1.4B debt vs $4.23B equity) . Prior to 2020, LULU had virtually no debt (D/E \~0). The slight increase reflects funding for Mirror and working capital, but still well under 0.5. **✓ Pass.**

    -   *Interest Coverage (EBIT/Interest ≥ 3×) & Interest Exp/Op Income ≤ 15%:* Lululemon's interest expense is minimal. Operating income in 2024 was \~$2.51B vs interest expense of only a few million (the red dashed line in the chart below is practically at zero). Coverage is 50×+; interest expense as % of op income was \~1-2% . **✓ Pass** on both.

    -   *Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:* LULU consistently maintains a healthy liquidity buffer. Current ratio was **2.3×** as of April 2025 , and has generally been \~2-3× historically . Plenty of current assets (cash and inventory) vs liabilities. **✓ Pass.**

5.  **Profitability & Efficiency:**

    -   *Gross Margin ≳ 40%:* Lululemon's gross margins are excellent - in the high 50s% the past few years . Even a decade ago, GM was \~50%+. It never dropped near 40%. **✓ Pass** (far exceeds 40%).

    -   *Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:* Lululemon's net margins have been strong but **below 20%** historically. Recently \~16-17% , earlier years 10-15%. It hasn't achieved 20% net margin (peak \~17% in 2024). **✗ Fail** on this tough benchmark.

    -   *ROE ≥ 15% (10-year avg):* LULU's return on equity is very high. Ten-year average ROE \~30%+; even in lower years it was &gt;20%. FY2024 ROE \~42% . **✓ Pass**.

    -   *ROIC ≥ 12% (10-yr avg):* By any reasonable definition, Lululemon's ROIC is superb. Using NOPAT (\~$1.8B) and invested capital (equity + debt - excess cash ≈ $4B), ROIC \~45%. Even adjusting for operating leases, ROIC well above 12%. **✓ Pass**.

    -   *ROA ≥ 6%:* Net income/total assets was \~16% in 2024 ($1.81B / $11.0B assets) and even higher earlier (it dipped to \~10.5% in 2022 due to asset write-downs, but generally &gt;15%). Always above 6%. **✓ Pass**.

    -   *Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:* Depreciation was $446M vs $6,271M gross profit in 2024 = **7.1%** . Historically also under 10%. This indicates capital expenditures aren't eroding the majority of gross profit - LULU is not asset-heavy. **✓ Pass**.

**Summary:** Lululemon passes many of Buffett's quality criteria - it has high margins, strong returns on equity/capital, consistent free cash flow, prudent debt, and a durable brand. The main areas it fell short are **valuation metrics** (it was often overvalued until recently) and the strict "no single EPS decline" rule. Also its net margin, while healthy, is below the ultra-high 20% bar (few retailers ever hit 20% net margin, as that's more common for consumer monopolies or software firms).

Importantly, at the current beaten-down price, Lululemon looks attractive even under Buffett's value lens - P/E is \~11, a margin of safety exists, and the balance sheet is rock-solid. One might argue LULU now offers both quality and a reasonable price. However, earlier in the decade, an investor would have had to pay a premium (P/E 30-50) to own this growth story, which Buffett might have avoided despite the superb business performance.

## **DCF Valuation Analysis**

To estimate Lululemon's **intrinsic value**, we perform a Discounted Cash Flow analysis under three scenarios - **Conservative**, **Nominal (Base)**, and **Optimistic** - based on varying free cash flow growth assumptions. We use Lululemon's FY2024 free cash flow (FCF) as a starting point (\~$1.58 billion ) and project 5 years of growth, then a terminal value, discounting at \~10% (a typical equity hurdle rate).

**Assumptions:**

-   **FCF (2024 base):** $1.584 billion (this represents operating cash flow minus maintenance capex in FY2024, roughly equal to reported FCF ).

-   **Growth Rates:**

    -   *Conservative:* We use the **lowest FCF growth rate** observed (ignoring outlier declines). Lululemon's slowest growth apart from 2022 was around +5-10%. We assume **5% annual FCF growth** for 5 years. This reflects cautious outlook (near-flat real growth).

    -   *Nominal/Base:* Approximately the **average FCF growth** of the past decade. LULU averaged \~20-25% growth (CAGR) excluding unusual years. We take **15% annual growth** for 5 years, acknowledging that as the company is larger now, growth may moderate.

    -   *Optimistic:* The **highest growth** Lululemon achieved in healthy periods was 60-70% (2018-2021 FCF surges, aside from the &gt;100% rebound in 2023). For optimism, we'll use **25% annual growth** for 5 years, assuming exceptional continued expansion (perhaps via new markets or margin gains).

-   **Terminal Growth:** After year 5, we assume a perpetual growth of **3%** (roughly inflation) for all scenarios - reflecting a mature steady-state.

-   **Discount Rate:** 10% (reflecting a moderately conservative cost of equity given LULU's debt-light profile and strong returns).

**Calculations:**

-   *Conservative Case:*

    -   Year 1-5 FCF grows at 5%:

        -   FCF in Year 5 = $1.584B \* (1.05)^5 ≈ $2.02 billion.

    -   Terminal value in Year 5 = FCF\_5 \* (1+g) / (r - g) = $2.02B \* 1.03 / (0.10 - 0.03) ≈ $29.74 billion.

    -   Present value (PV) of FCF Years 1-5 and terminal:

        -   PV of years 1-5 ≈ $1.584B/1.10 + $1.663B/1.10^2 + … $2.02B/1.10^5 ≈ $6.97 billion.

        -   PV of terminal value = $29.74B / 1.10^5 ≈ $18.47 billion.

        -   **Total PV = \~$25.44 billion.**

    -   Divide by shares (\~121 million) = **Intrinsic value ≈ $210/share.**

    -   Applying a 25% margin of safety: **Buy Price ≈ $158/share.**

-   *Nominal/Base Case:*

    -   FCF grows at 15% for 5 years:

        -   Year 5 FCF = $1.584B \* (1.15)^5 ≈ $3.19 billion.

    -   Terminal value in Year 5 = $3.19B \* 1.03 / (0.10 - 0.03) = \~$46.9 billion.

    -   PV of years 1-5 ≈ $1.822B/1.1 + … + $3.19B/1.1^5 ≈ $10.38 billion.

    -   PV of terminal = $46.9B / 1.1^5 ≈ $29.14 billion.

    -   **Total PV ≈ $39.52 billion.**

    -   Per share intrinsic value ≈ $39.52B / 0.121B shares = **\~$326/share.**

    -   25% MOS → **Buy ≤ $245/share.**

-   *Optimistic Case:*

    -   FCF grows at 25% for 5 years:

        -   Year 5 FCF = $1.584B \* (1.25)^5 ≈ $4.85 billion.

    -   Terminal value = $4.85B \* 1.03 / (0.10 - 0.03) = \~$71.4 billion.

    -   PV of years 1-5 ≈ $1.98B/1.1 + … + $4.85B/1.1^5 ≈ $14.05 billion.

    -   PV of terminal = $71.4B / 1.1^5 ≈ $44.39 billion.

    -   **Total PV ≈ $58.44 billion.**

    -   Intrinsic value/share ≈ $58.44B / 0.121B = **\~$483/share.**

    -   25% MOS → **Buy ≤ $362/share.**

**Results:** Across scenarios, intrinsic value ranges from \~$210 (very conservative) to \~$483 (high growth case) per share. The Base case is around **$325-$330** per share. Notably, LULU's stock peaked near $480 in late 2021, which in hindsight was pricing in the optimistic scenario. The current market price (≈$170) is **below even the conservative DCF value**. This suggests a significant margin of safety assuming Lululemon can achieve at least mid-single-digit growth.

Even taking our most cautious value \~$210 and adding a strict 25% MOS, the threshold \~$158 is around the current price. In other words, at \~$170, LULU is trading *near or below intrinsic value under reasonable assumptions*.

**Key drivers:** Lululemon's valuation is sensitive to growth. If growth averages in the mid-teens (plausible given international expansion and recovering U.S.), the stock's intrinsic value is well above $250. Additionally, LULU's robust free cash conversion and low discount rate (due to low debt) work in its favor.

Investors should monitor whether LULU can reaccelerate revenue/FCF growth post-2025. The conservative DCF essentially assumes LULU stagnates (5% growth) - yet still yields \~$210/share value. This underlines the strength of Lululemon's cash generation "moat." The DCF analysis overall implies **Lululemon is undervalued** in 2025 unless one expects the company's growth to severely decelerate or face major adversity.
