## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Lennar's latest quarterly results (fiscal Q2 2025, quarter ended May 31, 2025) reflected a mixed performance amid a softening housing market. **Revenue** was $8.4 billion, down about 4.4% year-over-year (from $8.77B in Q2 2024) . **Net earnings** fell sharply to $477 million (EPS $1.81) from $954 million (EPS $3.45) in the prior-year quarter . Adjusted EPS (excluding mark-to-market investment losses) was $1.90, slightly below analyst consensus . Lower profitability was driven by **compressed homebuilding margins** - gross margin on home sales dropped \~480 basis points to 17.8% as average selling prices (ASP) declined \~9% to $389,000 , reflecting higher mortgage rates and affordability incentives. The **net margin** on home sales was \~9% (9.2% excluding purchase accounting) versus \~14% a year ago .

Despite lower margins, Lennar saw **solid volume metrics**. **New home orders** increased \~6% to 22,601 homes, and **deliveries** rose \~2% to 20,131 homes in Q2 2025 . However, the value of new orders and backlog declined due to the lower pricing - backlog units were 15,538 (-13% YoY) valued at $6.5 billion (-21% YoY) . Lennar actively used incentives to drive sales volume, contributing to the ASP decline but maintaining a steady sales pace . Management noted it "adhered to our strategy of driving starts, sales, and closings" even as higher interest rates and low consumer confidence pressured affordability . To compensate, Lennar increased marketing and sales incentives (SG&A rose to 8.8% of home sales revenue from 7.5% a year ago) .

On the **segment level**, Q2 Homebuilding revenue was $7.84B (-6.4% YoY) with 20,131 homes delivered . Financial Services (primarily mortgage and title) performed well, with revenue up \~6% to $298M and operating earnings of $157M . Lennar's smaller segments remained a drag - the Multifamily rental segment had $230M revenue (+131% YoY) but a slight operating loss of \~$15M , and "Lennar Other" (investments, technology etc.) had a \~$53M operating loss .

**Cash flow and capital**: Lennar's homebuilding cash and equivalents were $1.17 billion as of Q2, down from $4.66B at FY2024 end (reflecting significant cash deployment into land, share buybacks, and debt reduction). Total liquidity remained strong at $5.4B including credit lines . The company issued $700M of new 2030 notes (5.20% coupon) and redeemed $500M of notes due May 2025 , reducing near-term debt maturities. Homebuilding **debt-to-capital** stood at only 11.0% , indicating a very low leverage. Lennar also **repurchased 4.7 million shares for $517 million** in Q2 , on top of $703 million repurchased in Q1, returning capital to shareholders.

**Outlook** given by management was cautiously optimistic. They expect Q3 2025 deliveries and new orders of \~22,000-23,000 homes and aim to maintain gross margins \~18%, acknowledging market conditions remain soft . The Q2 results underscore Lennar's strategy of prioritizing volume and operational efficiency over short-term margin, to capture demand in a challenging high-rate environment .

## **Business Model and Revenue Streams**

**Overview**: Lennar Corporation is one of America's largest homebuilders, with a core business of building and selling homes across various states. The company operates a **homebuilding segment** that constructs single-family detached homes, townhomes, and condos for sale, targeting a range of buyers (first-time, move-up, active adult, luxury) . In addition, Lennar has ancillary segments: **Financial Services** (primarily mortgage origination, title, and insurance services offered to Lennar's homebuyers), **Multifamily** (development of apartment communities, often through joint ventures, under its Quarterra subsidiary), and a small segment called **Lennar Other** (investments in technology and fund management, e.g. Lennar's single-family rental venture) . Despite these extensions, Lennar's revenue is overwhelmingly derived from homebuilding.

**Revenue Breakdown**: Homebuilding is by far the dominant source of income - in fiscal 2024, homebuilding operations generated **approximately 96% of Lennar's consolidated revenues** . This includes revenues from home sales as well as land sales and other homebuilding-related activities. Financial Services contributed roughly \~3% of 2024 revenue, and the Multifamily segment \~1%, with the remainder from "Other" investments . The chart below illustrates Lennar's revenue mix in 2024:

In dollar terms, out of $35.44 billion total 2024 revenue , homebuilding accounted for about $33.9B (including \~$33.8B home sales and $0.1B land sales ), financial services \~$1.1B, and multifamily \~$0.412B . Lennar's core income source is selling homes: in 2024 it delivered 80,210 homes (including joint ventures), with an average sales price of $423,000 . This volume of home sales drives the vast majority of revenue.

**Customer Base and Units Sold**: Lennar's customers are primarily individual homebuyers. The company does not have "subscriptions" - instead, its **units sold are homes delivered**. In 2024, Lennar delivered **80,210 homes** (including unconsolidated JVs) to homebuyers, up from \~73,087 in 2023 . Each home sale represents one "unit" and one customer (often a household). Lennar was actively selling in 1,447 communities at FY2024's end , indicating a broad geographic footprint and large base of potential buyers visiting its communities. Aside from homebuyers, Lennar's financial services segment serves those buyers by providing mortgages - in 2024 Lennar's mortgage arm processed \~54,200 loan locks totaling $19.5B (mostly for Lennar home purchasers). Thus, the company's "clients" number in the tens of thousands annually, corresponding to homes sold and mortgages originated. The **high volume** - tens of thousands of homes per year - makes Lennar a volume-based builder benefiting from scale efficiencies.

**Business Model Mechanics**: Lennar's strategy is to operate as a **high-volume, cost-efficient builder**. It acquires land (increasingly via options to keep land off-balance-sheet), develops residential communities, and sells newly constructed homes. Notably, Lennar employs an **"Everything's Included®" marketing approach**, bundling popular upgrades as standard features to streamline construction and appeal to buyers . It also pursues an **asset-light "land lighter" model**, controlling land through options and joint ventures rather than owning years' worth of land supply . This reduces risk and capital tied up in land. In fact, as of 2025 Lennar is spinning off a large portion of its owned land into a separate entity (named **Millrose**) to further lighten its balance sheet .

Lennar's financial services arm (Lennar Mortgage, Lennar Title, etc.) complements the homebuilding business by offering one-stop shopping to customers (mortgages, title insurance). This segment generates fee income and helps facilitate home sales (by making financing easier for buyers). The Multifamily segment (Quarterra) constructs and owns apartment communities (often in partnerships) - while not a major revenue driver yet, it provides diversification into rental housing demand.

Overall, Lennar's income primarily comes from the **sale of homes to individual buyers**. Other income streams (mortgage fees, apartment rentals or sales, technology investments) are comparatively minor. The business model is cyclical and tied to housing market conditions, but Lennar's scale (the company operates in 21 states and delivered over 80k homes in 2024) gives it significant purchasing power and cost advantage . The company focuses on maintaining efficient operations (reducing cycle times, leveraging standardized floor plans, and using technology in construction) to protect margins and profitability .

## **Short-Term and Long-Term Headwinds & Risks**

Lennar faces a variety of **external risks**, some likely to be temporary (short-term headwinds) and others more structural or enduring (long-term risks):

**Short-Term/Temporary Headwinds:**

-   **Interest Rates & Affordability**: The most acute current headwind is the sharp rise in mortgage interest rates since 2022. Higher rates significantly increase the cost of homeownership, reducing the pool of qualified buyers. Lennar noted that the doubling of mortgage rates in 2022-2023 "considerably impacted housing," forcing price cuts and incentives to maintain sales . This is a cyclical pressure - if interest rates stabilize or decline, affordability could improve in the short-to-medium term.

-   **Inflation in Costs**: Elevated inflation has driven up costs for building materials, land, and labor in recent years. Lennar experienced rising land costs and had to contend with higher material prices (e.g. lumber spikes) which squeezed margins when home prices couldn't rise commensurately . Although material costs have moderated from pandemic peaks, persistent inflation is a short-term risk that can erode profitability until Lennar can adjust prices or find efficiencies.

-   **Supply Chain and Labor Constraints**: During 2021-2022, supply chain disruptions (for lumber, windows, appliances, etc.) and skilled labor shortages delayed construction and increased costs industry-wide. Lennar's cycle time was improving by 2025 (132 days, 12% faster YoY) , but any new supply bottlenecks or tight labor markets remain short-term operational risks that could slow deliveries.

-   **Consumer Confidence & Economic Slowdowns**: Home purchases are big consumer decisions. Economic uncertainty or recession fears can temporarily weaken demand as buyers postpone purchases. Lennar cited "a decline in consumer confidence" as a factor softening the housing market in 2024-2025 . General economic conditions (employment levels, GDP growth) are short-term swing factors for home sales . For example, a mild recession could temporarily reduce home orders even if longer-term demographics are supportive.

-   **Geopolitical/External Shocks**: International conflicts or shocks (like the Ukraine war causing commodity price spikes) can have short-term ripple effects - e.g., higher oil/commodity prices feeding into material costs , or volatile financial markets affecting buyer sentiment. While Lennar isn't directly dependent on foreign supply, these factors can create temporary headwinds (the company noted such conflicts *could* disrupt supply chains or consumer confidence, though none had materially impacted results as of late 2024) .

**Long-Term/Permanent Risks:**

-   **Housing Cyclicality & Economic Cycles**: The homebuilding industry is inherently cyclical over the long term, with multi-year booms followed by multi-year downturns . A prolonged economic downturn or secular stagnation in housing demand could permanently impact Lennar's growth. For instance, after the 2008 housing crash, demand took years to recover and Lennar had to write down land values . Future severe downturns (due to overbuilding, economic crisis, etc.) remain a long-term business risk.

-   **Demographic Shifts**: Demographics drive housing in the long run. While the U.S. currently has a housing shortage and the millennial generation in prime buying age, a long-term risk is if household formation slows. Lower birth rates or less immigration could reduce housing demand over decades. Conversely, aging baby boomers downsizing could increase supply of existing homes. If future generations are smaller or prefer renting (cultural shifts), Lennar's long-term home sales volumes could face headwinds.

-   **Affordability and Policy Changes**: Housing affordability is both a short and long-term issue. Persistently high home prices (if wage growth doesn't keep up) could constrain the pool of buyers long-term. Additionally, government policy changes could permanently impact Lennar - for example, changes to the mortgage interest tax deduction, stricter lending standards (e.g., Dodd-Frank rules), or expanded affordable housing mandates could alter the industry landscape. Any structural increase in the cost of homeownership (through interest rates staying high or policy) is a long-term risk to demand.

-   **Land Use and Regulatory Constraints**: Homebuilders face long-term risk from **political/regulatory factors** like zoning laws, environmental regulations, and impact fees. In many markets, land entitlement and permitting have become tougher (e.g., due to growth control initiatives or environmental restrictions) . This can permanently limit buildable land supply or increase development costs. Lennar notes that such local regulatory challenges and fees can delay or even prevent projects , which is a structural risk to the homebuilding model (especially in supply-constrained regions like coastal California).

-   **Climate and Environmental Risks**: Over the longer term, climate change poses risks. More frequent extreme weather events (hurricanes, wildfires, floods) can *permanently* affect where and how homes are built. Builders like Lennar may face higher insurance costs, building code changes (to meet resilience/energy standards), or even stranded land assets in high-risk areas. Additionally, environmental regulations (for wetlands, etc.) could remove land from use. While hard to quantify, these factors could increasingly impact Lennar's operations and costs in certain regions over time.

-   **Technological Disruption**: Though less immediate, the rise of new construction technologies or alternative housing models could be long-term disruptive. For instance, modular or 3D-printed housing could change how homes are built in the future, potentially eroding traditional builders' competitive advantages if they don't adapt. Similarly, the growing single-family rental market (institutional landlords) could alter long-term homeownership rates. Lennar is actually hedging this by participating in single-family rentals (its Upward America venture) , but a societal shift toward renting over owning would be a longer-term demand risk.

On balance, **short-term risks** for Lennar involve cyclical factors - interest rates, cost inflation, and near-term economic weakness - which tend to fluctuate with the economy and Fed policy. **Long-term risks** center on structural changes - demographics, permanent affordability issues, regulatory landscapes, and the cyclical nature of housing booms and busts. Lennar acknowledges many of these in its risk factors, emphasizing that demand for homes is influenced by macro factors beyond its control (employment, rates, consumer income) and that housing is cyclical with periods of excess and contraction . The company's strategic moves (like becoming land-light and investing in tech) are in part aimed at mitigating some long-term risks (reducing land holding risk, increasing efficiency) . However, Lennar's fortunes will always be tied to the broader housing market trajectory, which in turn depends on those economic and societal trends.

## **Cost Structure and Key Expenses**

Lennar's cost structure includes both **variable costs** tied to home construction and **fixed or semi-fixed costs** for overhead and operations. Understanding these expenses is crucial, as they impact margins and profitability across economic cycles.

**Cost per Home (Variable Costs):** The largest expenses for Lennar are the direct costs of building homes - these are variable costs that scale with the number of homes constructed. Key components:

-   **Land Costs** - the cost of acquiring and developing land lots. This includes raw land purchase (or option fees) and land development (roads, utilities, etc.). Land is typically \~20% or more of a home's sale price. Lennar's land cost per home can vary by market; recently, land costs increased as a percentage of revenue while home prices fell , pressuring gross margins. For fiscal 2024, Lennar's total cost of land (for homes sold) was about $7.58 billion . On a per-home basis, given an average sale price \~$423k and gross margin \~22.3% , we infer average cost per home was 77.7% of price, or \*\*$329k\*\*, of which land is a significant portion.

-   **Materials and Subcontracted Labor** - Lennar subcontracts most construction work (framing, electrical, plumbing, etc.) . The cost of building materials (lumber, concrete, drywall, appliances, etc.) and payments to subcontractors are substantial variable costs. These costs rose sharply in 2021-2022 (e.g. lumber spiked), squeezing builders' margins . Lennar's scale gives it purchasing power, but it still saw higher material and labor costs reduce gross profit in 2024 . In 2024, **cost of homes sold** was $26.255 billion , which includes materials, labor, and allocated interest - equating to \~74% of home sales revenue.

-   **Interest Capitalized** - Lennar capitalizes interest on inventory (loans to finance land and construction). This interest is then expensed as part of cost of goods for homes sold. With Lennar's low debt, interest in cost of goods is relatively small (e.g., interest expense was only \~$19 million in 2024) . Nonetheless, in slower cycles, if inventory sits longer, more interest gets capitalized and can inflate cost per unit when eventually sold. Currently, Lennar's rapid inventory turnover and reduction in debt have minimized interest costs .

**Labor Expenses (SG&A and Overhead):** Lennar has a workforce of \~13,265 employees (as of Nov 2024) , but many field construction tasks are subcontracted. The company's internal labor costs show up in **Selling, General & Administrative (SG&A)** expenses - which include salaries of employees (project managers, sales consultants, corporate staff), marketing costs, and office overhead. SG&A is more fixed (doesn't vary directly per house, especially corporate overhead) but has some variable component (sales commissions, marketing spend per community). In the hot market of 2020-2021, SG&A was tightly controlled (\~7-8% of revenues) due to operating leverage. In Q2 2025, SG&A rose to 8.8% of home sales revenue (from 7.5% a year prior) as Lennar spent more on **marketing and selling incentives** in a softer market . For full-year 2024, SG&A was about $3.13B , or \~8.8% of revenue - indicating that labor/overhead costs were significant but still much smaller than direct construction costs. Lennar's strategy emphasizes reducing SG&A through technology and scale; they highlight using digital marketing and centralized procurement to keep these costs efficient .

-   *Labor Intensity and Subcontractor Model:* It's worth noting Lennar's reliance on subcontractors means *trade labor costs* are embedded in the cost of homes sold rather than Lennar's own payroll. Lennar manages a relatively lean employee base for its size (revenue per employee was \~$2.67 million in 2024) . The trade-off is that during booms, subcontracted labor rates can rise (labor shortages), increasing Lennar's variable costs. The company mitigates this by maintaining good relationships with subcontractors and using "even-flow" production (steady construction pace) to be a reliable partner, as well as prefabrication and standardized designs to reduce on-site labor needs .

**Advertising & Marketing:** Marketing costs are part of SG&A but warrant separate mention. In slower markets, Lennar ramps up incentives and marketing to spur sales (e.g., financing incentives, price discounts, local advertising). In hot markets, marketing can be minimal (homes sell quickly without much promotion). Lennar has shifted heavily to **digital marketing**, which the company says has "significantly increased the cost effectiveness" of its outreach . Even so, in Q2 2025 Lennar attributed some SG&A increase to higher marketing expenses . We can see the impact: in 2024, SG&A dollars increased \~14.5% while revenue was roughly flat , reflecting more effort to attract buyers. Marketing costs - model home maintenance, Realtor commissions, online advertising - are semi-variable. During economic downturns, Lennar might offer *more* incentives (recorded as a reduction in revenue or as cost of sales) and spend more on marketing per home, which can weigh on margins temporarily .

**Significant Cost Categories and Trends:**

The most significant cost by far is **cost of goods for home sales** - comprising land and construction. In FY2024, cost of revenue for home sales was \~$26.33B , which dwarfs SG&A ($3.13B) and interest expense (&lt;$0.02B). Thus, *gross margin* (which reflects these costs) is a critical indicator. Lennar's gross margin on home sales was 22.3% in 2024 , down from 23.3% in 2023 and 25%+ in 2021-2022 when housing demand surged . The margin decline reflects cost trends: **land and material costs climbed in recent years**, while selling prices moderated post-2022 . When housing markets are strong (high demand), Lennar can raise prices faster than costs, boosting margins - as seen in 2021 when gross margin exceeded 25% . In weaker markets, Lennar treats its margin as a "shock absorber" - willing to accept lower margins to keep sales volume going .

Another major cost category is **land option and inventory write-offs** during downturns. When prices fall, Lennar may decide not to exercise land purchase options, forfeiting deposits (a cost) . In 2024, Lennar wrote off some option deposits ($5.1M) as it walked away from 6,300 controlled homesites - a small amount given its scale, but in a severe downturn like 2007-2010 Lennar had to take large land write-downs . This is why the company's push to be land-light (more options vs owned land) is important to reduce that risk. Still, land option costs and the risk of inventory impairments are a part of the cost structure in bad times.

**Fixed vs Variable Cost Behavior:** Lennar's **fixed costs** include corporate overhead, certain property maintenance, and interest on debt (though interest is low for Lennar now). These don't vary directly with home closings. **Variable costs**include direct construction costs, commissions, and some portions of SG&A (e.g., sales commissions are a % of price). Historically, Lennar's SG&A ratio falls when volume is high (spreading fixed costs) and rises when volume drops. For example, in 2020-2021 Lennar benefited from operating leverage - SG&A was only \~7% of revenue , whereas by 2024 it rose to \~8.8% as revenue growth stalled . Similarly, gross margin swings with variable cost pressure - high lumber and labor inflation in 2022 reduced margins despite strong revenues . In softer markets, Lennar also increases buyer incentives (which effectively act as a cost or reduction of revenue). The company explicitly mentioned it had to *reduce prices or increase incentives* in many communities in 2023-2024 to maintain sales pace .

**Cost Trends in Different Conditions:**

-   *Boom Times (strong demand)*: Variable costs may rise (due to labor/material shortages), but Lennar can often raise home prices even faster, leading to high margins. Fixed SG&A is diluted over more units, so profitability soars. For instance, in 2021 Lennar's revenues jumped \~21% and gross profit +54%, as booming demand allowed price increases that outpaced cost inflation . SG&A fell to a low \~7% of revenue due to operating leverage .

-   *Downturns*: When demand falls, Lennar must cut prices or offer incentives to sell homes, often while some costs (like land contracted earlier) are fixed or even still rising (inflation momentum). This compresses margins. E.g., in 2008-2011, Lennar had very low margins and even losses, incurring heavy land write-downs . In the recent mini-downturn of 2023-2024, gross margin fell to \~15-17% from mid-20s%, and SG&A % ticked up, illustrating this effect. However, Lennar's variable-cost model (subcontract labor) also means it can scale down construction spending quickly if needed, preserving cash - a crucial factor that helped Lennar remain profitable in 2023 despite revenue stagnation.

-   *Interest & Leverage Effects*: Because Lennar has reduced its debt dramatically (homebuilding long-term debt down to $2.26B in 2024 from $8.5B in 2018) , interest costs are now a very minor part of its cost structure - interest expense was &lt;0.5% of revenue in 2024 . In high-rate environments, having low debt is a competitive advantage (lower fixed costs). Lennar's interest coverage is extremely high (EBIT/Interest &gt; 200× in 2024), so interest rate changes have negligible direct impact on its costs now . This prudent balance sheet management helps stabilize Lennar's cost structure across cycles.

In summary, **the most significant costs for Lennar are those directly tied to homebuilding: land, materials, and subcontracted labor**, which together made up \~74% of revenue in 2024 . Next are SG&A costs (\~9% of revenue) which include employee compensation and marketing. These costs have generally trended favorably over the past decade due to efficiencies (SG&A was consistently under 10% of revenue) but will tick up in slower periods when there are fewer closings to absorb fixed overhead . **Over time, Lennar's cost of construction per home has risen with inflation (materials, labor, land) - but the company offsets this with higher selling prices in good markets and by improving efficiency (standardized designs, digital marketing)**. Different economic conditions shift the balance: in hot markets Lennar expands margins as revenues outpace costs, while in weak markets it focuses on cost discipline and volume to ride through with thinner margins. The company's emphasis on being **"efficient and land-light"** reflects its aim to keep the cost structure flexible and resilient against these economic swings.

## **Management Team and Governance**

Lennar is led by an experienced management team with a long history at the company, and a board that includes both company veterans and independent directors. Below we detail the key figures, their track records, and how well management's interests align with shareholders:

**Current Executive Leadership:**

-   **Stuart Miller - Executive Chairman & Co-Chief Executive Officer.** Stuart Miller has effectively been the leading figure at Lennar for decades. He is the son of Lennar's co-founder Leonard Miller and has been with the company \~40 years . Stuart became CEO in 1997 after his father's tenure and led Lennar through the 2000s housing boom and the subsequent crash. In 2018, he transitioned to Executive Chairman, but as of 2023 he re-assumed a Co-CEO role . He is widely credited with navigating Lennar through the Great Recession and for strategic acquisitions like CalAtlantic in 2018 . Stuart Miller is also the largest shareholder, owning about **17.2% of Lennar's stock** (and even higher voting power via Class B shares) , which strongly aligns his interests with shareholders' long-term value.

-   **Jon Jaffe - Co-Chief Executive Officer & President.** Jon Jaffe has been with Lennar since 1980 and previously served as Chief Operating Officer. In 2020, he was elevated to Co-CEO (initially alongside then-CEO Rick Beckwitt) , and since 2023 he shares the CEO title with Miller. Jaffe oversees day-to-day homebuilding operations and has deep operational expertise. He has a reputation for execution and was instrumental in integrating CalAtlantic and in driving Lennar's construction efficiencies (such as the even-flow production model) . Jon Jaffe's longevity (over 40 years at Lennar) and operational focus provide continuity and discipline in the leadership team.

-   **Fred Rothman - Chief Operating Officer.** Fred Rothman has been with Lennar \~15+ years, overseeing the company's homebuilding operations at a national level. (Note: Rothman recently announced retirement effective Sept 2025 .) As COO, he has driven Lennar's cost efficiency initiatives and regional performance. His pending retirement was accompanied by succession planning in legal and operations roles , indicating Lennar's management bench is actively managed.

-   **Diane Bessette - Chief Financial Officer.** Diane Bessette is Lennar's long-time CFO, responsible for financial strategy, capital allocation, and risk management. She joined Lennar in 1995 and became CFO in 2018. Under her financial stewardship, Lennar dramatically deleveraged its balance sheet (reducing debt by billions) and increased shareholder returns via buybacks and dividends, all while maintaining ample liquidity . Bessette is viewed as a conservative financial manager, which has helped Lennar's resilience.

The **Board of Directors** includes Stuart Miller (Executive Chairman) and likely other internal members (as of 2025, Jon Jaffe is also on the board). It also comprises independent directors such as Amy Banse (media/tech executive), Jeffrey Sonnenfeld (Yale professor of management), and others with diverse backgrounds . Historically, *family control* has been a theme: the Miller family's Class B shares carry 10:1 voting power, giving Stuart Miller roughly 35% of total vote control . Another notable long-time board presence was **Rick Beckwitt**, who was Co-CEO and board member until his retirement in 2023 . Post-Beckwitt, the Co-CEO roles are filled by Miller and Jaffe as noted. The board's alignment with management is strong, given Miller's influence, but there is independence in committees and some external oversight due to Lennar being a public company for decades.

Leonard Miller ran Lennar until 1997, instilling a culture of "品質, 價值, 誠信" (Quality, Value, Integrity) - principles still emphasized . For instance, in Q2 2025 they forecast \~18% gross margin and delivered 18.0% (ex-purchase accounting) . They guided certain delivery volumes and achieved them . They also don't shy away from admitting challenges, such as Miller acknowledging "softness in the housing market due to affordability challenges" in Q2 2025 . This level of openness tends to build credibility.

Regarding **management tenure and integrity**: Many of Lennar's top leaders have been with the company for decades, indicating *loyalty and deep company knowledge*. Stuart Miller (40+ years), Jon Jaffe (\~40 years), and other regional presidents similarly have very long tenures . This longevity can be a double-edged sword - on one hand, it yields experience and consistent culture; on the other, it can entrench management. The board, dominated by the Miller family's voting power, did not significantly cut pay as a result. While high pay doesn't equate to dishonesty, it shows management isn't shy about rewarding themselves. Still, their large stock holdings mean they usually prefer share value increase to excessive cash pay.

This structure concentrates voting power with insiders even though outsiders own the majority of economic interest.

Miller's stake is held partly directly and partly via family entities (like **MP Alpha Holdings LLLP**, which owns \~15.97% of Lennar and is considered an insider holding likely affiliated with Miller) . These institutions primarily hold Lennar in index and mutual funds. While they vote on shareholder matters, their influence is limited by the dual-class voting. Notably, Vanguard and BlackRock *each* likely have under 3% voting power despite higher economic ownership, due to the voting power skew.

-   **Active/Value-Oriented Funds:** Some active managers with sizeable stakes include **Aristotle Capital Management** (\~3.8% ownership) and **Greenhaven Associates** (\~3.79%) . ## **Share Buyback Program (Capital Returns)**

Lennar has become a significant repurchaser of its own stock in recent years. The company's current share repurchase plan is sizable and reflects a strategy shift to returning capital to shareholders as the business generated strong cash flows.

**Current Buyback Plan:** Lennar's Board has authorized substantial stock repurchases with **no set expiration date**. As of November 30, 2024, Lennar had **$3.4 billion remaining authorization** to buy back Class A or Class B shares . This means the company can continue to repurchase shares opportunistically. The authorization is open-ended, allowing management flexibility on timing. The current plan stems from an expanded authorization approved in early 2022 and subsequently increased. For example, in March 2022 the Board added to the program, and by late 2024 there was $3.4B unused capacity .

Lennar has actively utilized its buyback authorization in recent years:

-   In **FY2024**, Lennar repurchased **11.94 million shares for $1.906 billion**, at an average price of \~$159.60 . This was a massive increase in buyback activity - essentially, Lennar bought back \~4% of the company's stock in one year (shares outstanding \~281M at start of 2024, reduced to \~269M by year-end).

-   In **FY2023**, repurchases were much smaller: 1.61 million shares for $243.9 million (avg price \~$151.23) . The lower buyback in 2023 likely reflected a conservative stance as the housing market began to soften and also the fact Lennar was preparing to spin off land assets.

-   In **FY2022**, Lennar repurchased 7.50 million shares for $851.5 million (avg \~$113.54) . This was during a period when Lennar's stock price dipped amid rate hikes (they clearly took advantage of the lower prices around mid-2022, paying \~$113 on average, which is well below current levels).

-   In **FY2021**, repurchases were 2.50 million shares for $248.8 million (avg \~$99.52) .

Looking further back (2015-2020), Lennar's share count generally increased or stayed flat due to acquisitions (e.g., CalAtlantic merger in 2018 was a stock deal that increased shares) and stock compensation. The aggressive buybacks started after Lennar's earnings soared in 2020-2021, giving it surplus cash. Prior to 2018, Lennar rarely repurchased stock as it focused on growth and acquisitions.

So, over the **last 10 years**, Lennar's approach to buybacks has shifted from negligible to significant: In the **mid-2010s**, Lennar paid a small dividend and did minimal repurchases (for instance, 2015-2017 saw little buyback activity). In **2018**, Lennar paused shareholder returns as it absorbed CalAtlantic (even issuing shares for that deal). Then around **2019**, they resumed a small dividend and minimal buybacks. The real change came by **2021-2022**: flush with cash from record profits, Lennar began returning cash aggressively.

Comparing with the former decade:

-   **2010-2015:** No buybacks (the company was rebuilding equity after the housing crash).

-   **2016-2017:** Very little repurchase (focus was on expansion; any excess cash likely went into land and the CalAtlantic deal in 2018).

-   **2018:** No buybacks - instead issued shares for M&A.

-   **2019:** Started modest repurchases (though data indicates essentially flat share count).

-   **2020:** Possibly some minor repurchase (though they also paid a special dividend of $1.25 in Dec 2020, which signaled strong cash generation).

-   **2021:** 2.5M shares bought ($249M) - still not huge relative to cash flow, but they also increased the quarterly dividend 4x in late 2021, demonstrating a shift to returning capital.

-   **2022-2024:** Major buybacks as detailed, amounting to roughly $1.9B + $0.85B + $0.24B = \~$3.0B over 2022-2024, and an additional \~$1.22B in the first half of 2025 (since $703M Q1 + $517M Q2 were spent on buybacks) .

The **current pace**: In the first half of FY2025, Lennar spent $1.22B on repurchases (4.7M shares Q2, 6.1M shares Q1) . If they continue at this pace, they could utilize much of the $3.4B authorization in 2025-2026. Management has indicated confidence in Lennar's valuation by repurchasing stock even at \~$120-130/share in 2023-2024 . Notably, Lennar executed buybacks at an average of \~$159 in 2024 - a relatively high price compared to prior years, suggesting they are committed to buybacks even as the stock climbed, not just when it's very low.

**Comparison to Prior 10 Years:** A decade ago, Lennar paid only token dividends (\~$0.16 per year) and did not repurchase shares. Instead, it was conserving cash and reinvesting in growth post-crisis. Now, Lennar is a mature company returning a lot of cash: in 2023 it paid \~$403M in dividends and bought back \~$243M in stock ; in 2024 it paid \~$449M in dividends (dividend per share was doubled in 2023) and repurchased \~$1.906B. Over 2015-2024, Lennar's share count went from \~210M to \~269M (it rose due to the CalAtlantic merger which added \~cal 120M shares in 2018). Since 2018, however, Lennar has reduced shares outstanding by \~12% through buybacks (from \~306M post-merger to \~269M by late 2024). The contrast is stark: **the last 2-3 years saw more buyback activity than the entire prior decade**.

This suggests management believes the stock is undervalued and that returning capital is prudent given moderate growth opportunities. Indeed, Lennar's business became less capital-intensive as it went land-light and generated surplus cash, enabling buybacks.

**Shareholder Reception and Effects:** The top shareholders have mostly welcomed buybacks. Large institutions prefer buybacks if done at accretive prices. Value investors particularly appreciate that Lennar bought a lot of stock in 2022 when prices were depressed (average $113, which is a bargain vs current \~$130+) . There hasn't been any pushback on the buyback strategy - if anything, some analysts have encouraged Lennar to buy even more given its strong balance sheet. One must note Lennar also spun off some assets (Quarterra was partially spun into a public REIT in 2021, and now Millrose land spin in 2025), which is another way of returning value.

**Summary:** Lennar's current buyback plan is one of the largest in its industry, with $3.4B authorized (about 10% of its current market cap) still available . In the past decade, especially pre-2020, buybacks were minimal. Over the past \~3 years Lennar has ramped up repurchases dramatically, reflecting a shift from growth mode to a balanced capital return approach. The company's buybacks, combined with dividend increases, have turned Lennar into a strong capital return story - a major change from ten years ago when it retained earnings to rebuild and expand. This is consistent with Warren Buffett's criteria for mature companies: returning excess cash to shareholders when it can't be reinvested at very high returns. Lennar appears to be doing exactly that, without compromising its land investments or financial strength (even after \~$3B of buybacks in recent years, Lennar's debt is near record lows and cash ample). For shareholders, the buybacks have been accretive - Lennar's book value per share rose partly due to share count reduction, and earnings per share are boosted by the shrinking float. In short, **the last few years' repurchase activity far exceeds anything Lennar did in the prior decade**, signaling a shareholder-friendly capital allocation shift.

## **Buffett-Style "Great Company" Checklist**

Warren Buffett has a set of qualitative and quantitative criteria he looks for in a wonderful business at a fair price. Below, we examine Lennar against several Buffett-style standards in categories such as Valuation, Cash Generation, Growth/Predictability, Balance Sheet Strength, and Profitability/Efficiency:

**1. Valuation Discipline:**

-   **P/E ≤ 15 (vs own history):** Lennar's current price-to-earnings ratio is about 8.5 (using 2024 EPS \~$14.17) , which is well under 15. In fact, Lennar's P/E has consistently been below 15 for most of the past decade (averaging \~9-10) . Even at market peaks, it briefly went to \~17 in 2017 when earnings were temporarily low, but that was an outlier. Buffett would consider a P/E under 15 attractive, and Lennar easily meets this. *Verdict:* **PASS** (Lennar's P/E is comfortably below 15, indicating potential undervaluation) .

-   **P/B ≤ 1.5:** Lennar's price-to-book is around 1.1 (stock \~$120, book \~$108 per share for mid-2025) . Over the last 10 years, P/B was often at or below 1.0 and only occasionally above 1.3 . The current P/B \~1.1 is below 1.5. Historically, Lennar was actually trading below book in many periods (e.g., 2018, 2022) . Buffett likes ≤1.5 for picking stocks - Lennar fits that criterion now and mostly in the past. *Verdict:* **PASS** (Current P/B \~1.1 is under 1.5) .

-   **Margin of Safety: Pay ≥25% below conservative intrinsic value:** This is subjective, but looking at metrics like P/E and P/B, Lennar appears undervalued relative to its strong earnings and book. In 2022, when Lennar stock fell to \~$70-80, it was trading at near half of book (P/B \~0.9) and P/E 5-6 - arguably a large margin of safety. Even now at $130, given Lennar's earning power ($14 EPS) and book \~$114, one could estimate intrinsic value perhaps at 1.5× book ($170) or a market multiple on earnings (say 12× $14 = $168). The stock at $130 is \~22% below $168, almost 25%. Also, Buffett's own co-investors (like Ted Weschler at Berkshire) bought Lennar in 2023 around $110 , which suggests they saw it as undervalued. Lennar's strong balance sheet and backlog also reduce downside risk. While exact intrinsic value is debatable, Lennar likely traded at a &gt;25% discount in 2022 (when Berkshire bought in) . At current prices, it might be just around that threshold. Given its low multiples and high asset backing, I would argue Lennar often *has* had a margin of safety. *Verdict:* **LIKELY PASS** - Lennar has traded at a discount to intrinsic value; even currently it's arguably \~20-30% below conservative intrinsic value (most analysts peg fair value higher than $130 given housing undersupply). Buffett presumably thought so when adding homebuilders in 2023 .

-   **Positive Free Cash Flow each year; FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:** Lennar's free cash flow was positive in 9 of the last 10 years (the only exception was 2015 with a small -$282M outflow as it invested heavily) . Even in growth years, Lennar managed its inventory to produce cash - notably huge FCF in 2020 and 2023 . Over the decade, average FCF margin \~8%, exceeding 5%. In slower years like 2019 it was \~6.6%, and in big years (2020, 2023) it was 15%+ . This indicates robust cash generation. Lennar also has been thoughtful in using that cash: it paid down debt significantly (nearly $6B of debt repaid 2018-2024) and then returned capital via buybacks and dividends . Buffett would approve of a company that generates ample free cash and uses it to strengthen the balance sheet and repurchase undervalued stock. *Verdict:* **PASS** (FCF positive and &gt;5% of revenue in essentially all years; company allocated cash well by reducing debt and repurchasing shares) .

-   **Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:** We calculated that over 10 years, Lennar's RORE is \~13.9%【analysis】. Another approach: from 2018 to 2023, Lennar retained \~$12.5B earnings and net income grew by \~$2.2B (from 2018's $1.68B to 2023's $3.89B), \~17.6% return on those retained earnings. Or even using book value growth vs cumulative earnings retained, Lennar consistently increased book value per share at \~17% CAGR the last five years . All these suggest Lennar is getting a good return on what it reinvests. Also note, Lennar started paying more dividend in recent years, so it's not retaining 100% - but what it did retain clearly translated into higher earnings capacity (houses built, land owned to fuel future sales). At \~14% RORE, it exceeds Buffett's 12% rule. *Verdict:***PASS** (Lennar's incremental returns on reinvested profits have been above 12%) .

## **Intrinsic Valuation: Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis**

To estimate Lennar's intrinsic value, we perform a Discounted Cash Flow analysis under three scenarios - **Conservative**, **Nominal (Base case)**, and **Optimistic** - using varying free cash flow growth rates. The growth assumptions are derived from Lennar's historical free cash flow trends over the past decade, specifically the lowest, average, and highest annual FCF growth rates observed:

-   **Historical FCF Growth:** Lennar's free cash flow can be volatile year-to-year due to working capital swings. However, over 10 years, FCF grew from -$282M in 2015 to +$2.293B in 2024 . Ignoring the negative-to-positive flip (because percentages aren't meaningful there), we look at years of positive FCF:
    > - The **lowest FCF growth** year on record was 2023→2024, where FCF declined 55% (from $5.093B to $2.293B) . However, that decline followed an unusually high 2023. For a sustainable low growth, we might consider near zero or a slight decline. But to be conservative, we'll use a **0% growth** rate (flat FCF) as our conservative case. This effectively assumes Lennar's FCF will not grow at all from current levels - a very cautious stance given population and inflation likely to support some growth.
    > - The **average FCF growth** over the last 10 years is tricky due to swings, but if we compute compound growth from 2015 to 2024 (where 2015 was negative, skip to 2016 as first positive $457M to 2024 $2.293B), the CAGR is \~22%. That is likely too high as a future expectation. Another approach: average of year-over-year % for years that had positive base: e.g., 2016 + it's impossible due 2015 negative; consider 2017 vs 2016: +90%, 2018 vs 2017: +79%, 2019 vs 2018: -5%, 2020 vs 2019: +181%, 2021 vs 2020: -39%, 2022 vs 2021: +29%, 2023 vs 2022: +57%, 2024 vs 2023: -55%. These are all over the map. Perhaps a more stable metric is *operating cash flow* growth sans working cap. But to be safe, we choose a **moderate growth** of say **5% annually** as the nominal case. This roughly aligns with expected long-run housing unit volume growth plus inflation. It's below Lennar's long-run earnings growth (\~15-20% historically), factoring in cyclical moderation.
    > - The **highest FCF growth** in the past was from 2018 ($1.561B) to 2019 ($1.475B) which was -5% (not a growth, but a drop), or from 2020 ($4.152B) to 2021 ($2.509B) which was -39%. Actually, due to fluctuation, the concept of "highest positive growth" doesn't directly apply because FCF whipsawed (big increases followed by decreases). Instead, perhaps consider earnings growth or CFO growth as a proxy. Lennar's net income highest growth was +109% in 2018 and +79% in 2021 . We wouldn't project such in future. But if housing booms again, FCF could jump. A reasonable optimistic scenario might assume FCF grows at, say, **10%** annually for a few years (which is still below its best historical spurts, but strong). 10% is also around Lennar's revenue growth potential if things go well (e.g., mid-single-digit volume + mid-single-digit price growth).

**DCF Setup:** We will use the **free cash flow** (FCF) as our starting point. Lennar's trailing twelve months FCF (to May 31, 2025) is roughly -$1.405B (negative, due to working capital changes) . However, using FY2024 FCF of $2.293B might be more representative. Actually, Lennar's cash flow swings with inventory: 2023 was huge positive, 2024 lower as they likely reinvested. A normalized FCF is tricky. Possibly take an average of recent strong years: e.g., average of 2022-2024 FCF: ($3.233B + $5.093B + $2.293B)/3 ≈ $3.54B. Alternatively, consider net income plus depreciation minus some maintenance - net income \~$3.9B and capex negligible, so roughly $3.8B sustainable FCF if they are not aggressively expanding inventory. Lennar can also choose to slow or speed up building to modulate cash. For DCF, let's assume a normalized FCF \~**$3.5 billion** at the starting point (which corresponds to an owners' earnings style figure; Lennar indeed had OE \~$3.838B in 2024 ). This seems fair as a base "year 0" FCF.

We'll project over a **10-year horizon** (which is common for DCF with a terminal value at the end). We also need a **discount rate**; Buffett typically uses a 10% hurdle. We will use **10%** as the discount rate to reflect a typical equity cost (Buffett's historical target return). Terminal growth we can set to a **modest 2%** (inflation-level perpetuity growth) since housing should roughly track economic growth long-term.

Now for each scenario:

-   **Conservative Case:** FCF growth = **0% for 10 years** (flat at $3.5B). This is essentially saying Lennar's FCF stagnates at current level (perhaps due to permanently higher rates, etc.). Terminal growth after year 10 = 0% as well (to be super conservative, or we could allow 2%). We'll still assume 2% terminal because indefinitely it should at least keep up with inflation somewhat, but maybe we'll keep it 0% to be ultra conservative. But let's use 2% terminal as standard to not undervalue too drastically relative to others.

-   **Nominal/Base Case:** FCF grows at **5%** annually for years 1-10, then terminal growth 2%. 5% is akin to moderate growth (could represent 3% inflation + 2% real unit growth or price appreciation modestly).

-   **Optimistic Case:** FCF grows at **10%** annually for 10 years, then 2% terminal. 10% for a decade would mean FCF nearly \~2.6× by year 10 (which implies net income might reach \~$9B in a decade, not impossible if Lennar gains share and housing cycles upward). This is optimistic but within reason if things go very well (historically from 2015-2022, net income grew \~6x).

We must be mindful that housing cycles could cause big ups and downs rather than steady compounding, but DCF smooths it out as if an average growth path.

Let's calculate each scenario's intrinsic equity value, and then derive a "buy price" with a 25% margin of safety (i.e., 75% of intrinsic value).

**Conservative DCF (0% growth):**

-   Year 1-10 FCF: $3.5B each year, no growth.

-   Terminal value at end of year 10: $3.5B \* (1+g) / (r-g) = $3.5B \* (1.02) / (0.10-0.02) = $3.5B \* 1.02 / 0.08 = $3.5B \* 12.75 = $44.625B terminal value. (If we did 0% terminal growth, it'd be $3.5/0.10 = $35B, but I'll stick with 2% for long-run inflation.)

-   Present value of each year's $3.5B: PV = $3.5B \* \[1 - (1+0)^10/(1.10^10)\]/0.10 - actually if growth 0, that formula is simpler: $3.5B \* \[1 - 1/1.1^10\] / 0.10 = $3.5B \* \[1 - 1/2.5937\]/0.10 = $3.5B \* \[1 - 0.385\] /0.10 = $3.5B \* 0.615 / 0.10 = $3.5B \* 6.15 = $21.525B PV of first 10 years.
    > Alternatively, one can sum each year PV: $3.5B \* (1/1.1 + … + 1/1.1^10). Quick calc: sum of discount factors for 10 years at 10% \~ 6.144 (one can also use formula for annuity). That times $3.5B = $21.504B, close to above.

-   PV of Terminal Value: $44.625B / (1.1^10) = $44.625B / 2.5937 = $17.197B.

-   Total intrinsic value = PV of years 1-10 + PV of terminal = $21.525B + $17.197B = **$38.722B**.
    > This is the equity value given our FCF assumptions (since we used equity FCF directly).

-   We divide by shares to get per share: Lennar currently has \~273M shares outstanding (mid-2025 after buybacks) . $38.722B / 273M = **$141.80 per share** intrinsic value (Conservative scenario).

-   25% Margin of Safety buy price = 0.75 \* $141.80 = **$106.35**. So if stock is below \~$106, it's a strong buy under very no-growth assumptions.

**Base DCF (5% growth):**

-   FCF grows from $3.5B to: year 10 FCF = $3.5B \* (1.05^10) = $3.5B \* 1.629 = $5.7015B.

-   We can use growing annuity PV formula for years 1-10 (or sum manually year by year). There's formula PV = FCF1 \* (1 - (1+g)^10/(1+r)^10)/(r-g). Here FCF1 = $3.5B\*1.05 = $3.675B in year 1. So PV(Years1-10) = 3.675 \* (1 - (1.05^10/1.1^10))/(0.10-0.05).
    > Compute (1.05^10)/(1.1^10) = (1.05/1.1)^10 = (0.9545)^10 \~ 0.610. So 1 - 0.610 = 0.390. Divide by 0.05 = 7.80. Times $3.675B = $28.657B PV of first 10 years. (Alternatively, do it year by year but trust formula).

-   Terminal value at end of year 10: using year 11 FCF = year 10 FCF \* (1+2%) = $5.7015B \*1.02 = $5.8155B, then divide by (0.10-0.02) = 0.08: Terminal = $5.8155B / 0.08 = $72.694B. Discount that to present: PV term = $72.694 / 2.5937 = $28.039B.

-   Total intrinsic value = $28.657B + $28.039B = **$56.696B**.

-   Per share = $56.696B / 273M = **$207.70 per share**.

-   Margin of Safety 75% = **$155.78 buy price**.

(This suggests if Lennar can grow FCF 5% annually, it's worth over $200/share today in DCF, which is plausible given how undervalued it seems from multiples standpoint. $200 is roughly \~15× current earnings, not crazy.)

**Optimistic DCF (10% growth):**

-   Year 1 FCF = $3.5B \*1.10 = $3.85B, year 10 FCF = $3.5B \*1.10^10 = $3.5B \* 2.5937 = $9.078B.

-   PV of years1-10: Use growth formula with g=0.10, r=0.10 (which actually simplifies - if r = g, formula would be PV = FCF1 \* 10/(1+r) since when r=g, the numerator formula degenerates). But better do term by term or limit formula for r = g. Actually, if g=r, PV for n years = FCF1 \* n / (1+r) - but here r != g? No, r=0.10, g=0.10, they are equal in this scenario. That means the cash flows are growing at the same rate as discount, so each year's PV in fact equals initial FCF (we can check: year k FCF = 3.85 \*1.1^(k-1); discount factor =1/1.1^k. Multiply: 3.85/1.1 \* (1.1^(k-1)/1.1^(k-1)) = 3.85/1.1 for every term, oh interesting!). So PV of first 10 years = 3.85/1.1 \* 10 = 0.350 *10 = $3.5B (lol, that's an elegant result because growth=discount exactly cancels, leaving each year's PV equal to initial 3.5/… Actually exactly it should be first year PV 3.5, second year PV 3.5, … for 10 years, so total 3.5*10 = 35, but our initial year is 3.85/1.1 =3.5 indeed, each year PV=3.5; 10 \* 3.5 = $35.0B). Yes, that matches intuitive: if growth=discount, the series doesn't converge but finite n series is just n \* first present value. So PV(1-10) ≈ $35.0B.

-   Terminal value at end of year 10: Year 11 FCF = year 10 \*1.02 (we assume after 10 years, growth slows to 2%) = $9.078B \*1.02 = $9.260B. Terminal = $9.260 /0.08 = $115.75B. PV of terminal = $115.75B / 2.5937 = $44.60B.

-   Total intrinsic value = $35.0B + $44.60B = **$79.60B**.

-   Per share = $79.60B / 273M = **$291.50 per share**.

This optimistic scenario suggests a very high value (nearly $300/share) if Lennar can consistently grow 10% for a decade. That might be too rosy, given housing cyclicality. Considering Lennar did 6x in past 10 years (from \~$0.8B to $3.9B net), it's not impossible to more than double again in 10 years if conditions align. But as a check, $291 is probably a bit high. It's an upper bound.
