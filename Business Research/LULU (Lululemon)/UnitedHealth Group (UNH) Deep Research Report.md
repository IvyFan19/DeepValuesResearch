# **UnitedHealth Group (UNH) Deep Research Report**

## **Latest Quarter Earnings (Q2 2025)**

UnitedHealth Group reported mixed **Q2 2025** results amid cost pressures and updated its full-year outlook. **Revenue** for the quarter was **$111.62 billion**, up about **13%** from $98.86B in Q2 2024 . This slightly **beat analyst expectations** of \~$111.59B . However, **earnings per share (EPS)** (adjusted) came in at **$4.08**, missing the $4.45 consensus. GAAP net earnings were **$3.74 per share**, with the difference due to adjustments for one-time costs . Higher medical cost "trend" - meaning patients are using more healthcare services - squeezed profits. The **medical care ratio** (medical costs as % of premium revenue) jumped to **\~84.8%** in Q2 (vs 84.3% last year) reflecting elevated outpatient and physician care, which outpaced expectations . As a result, operating margin contracted and net earnings fell year-over-year.

UnitedHealth **trimmed its 2025 outlook**, now forecasting **$445.5-$448.0 billion** revenue (vs prior \~$449B consensus) and **at least $16.00** adjusted EPS . This is **well below analysts' prior $20.90** EPS estimate , a substantial guide-down reflecting the cost pressures. The company cited "higher realized and anticipated care trends" for the rest of 2025 and expects earnings growth to resume in 2026 . Following these developments, UNH's stock **fell over 5%** on the earnings release , as investors reacted to the profit miss and reduced guidance. Despite short-term margin compression, revenue growth remains solid (high single digits), showcasing the company's ability to expand membership and service offerings even in a challenging cost environment.

**Key Q2 2025 figures:** Adjusted EPS $4.08 (-15.7% YoY), revenue $111.6B (+13% YoY), operating margin \~3.7% (down from \~4.3% a year ago) . UnitedHealth also reinstated a full-year earnings forecast (after having suspended guidance in May) at **≥$16.00** adj. EPS for 2025 . This cautious outlook and the acknowledgment of higher medical utilization signaled to the market that healthcare cost trends are a headwind in the near term.

## **Background Research**

### **1. Business Model and Sources of Income**

UnitedHealth Group is a diversified health care company with **two major businesses:** **UnitedHealthcare** (health insurance benefits) and **Optum** (health services). These segments work in tandem to cover insurance, care delivery, pharmacy benefits, and data/technology services. In 2024, **UnitedHealthcare (UHC)** accounted for \~**54%** of revenue, while **Optum** contributed \~**46%** . Below is a breakdown of key income sources:

-   **Premiums (UnitedHealthcare):** UHC provides medical insurance plans for employer groups, individuals, Medicare and Medicaid beneficiaries. Insurance premiums are the largest revenue source (\~$298 billion in 2024) . UHC's sub-segments include Employer & Individual (commercial plans), Medicare & Retirement (seniors on Medicare Advantage and supplemental plans), Community & State (Medicaid and state programs), and Global (international). For example, the Employer & Individual unit had $78.2B revenue in 2024, Medicare & Retirement $139.5B, and Community & State $80.6B . This insurance business generates revenue from premiums, while profits depend on controlling medical claim costs (medical care ratio was \~85.5% in 2024) .

-   **Optum Health Services:** **Optum** is comprised of OptumHealth (care delivery & value-based provider services), OptumRx (pharmacy benefits and services), and OptumInsight (healthcare data analytics & consulting). In 2024, Optum's revenue was **$253.0B** (up 12% YoY) . OptumHealth ($105.4B in 2024 revenue) runs clinics, surgery centers, and manages care for patients (including 4.7 million people in value-based care arrangements by end of 2024) . OptumRx (15% revenue growth in 2024) manages pharmacy benefits, processing \~**1.62 billion**prescriptions in 2024 . OptumInsight ($18.8B in 2024) provides analytics, technology, and consulting to healthcare providers/payers . **Optum's growth** is strong as more services move in-house: it served 600,000 more patients in value-based arrangements in 2024 and won new health system partnerships .

**Income Mix Pie Chart:** UnitedHealthcare (premiums) \~54% and Optum (services) \~46% of total 2024 revenues . Within Optum, OptumHealth \~26%, OptumRx \~33%, OptumInsight \~5% of total revenue . This diversified model means UNH earns money both from **insurance premiums** and from **direct healthcare services** and products. Such integration is a competitive advantage: for instance, insurance members can receive care from Optum clinics, use Optum's pharmacy services, and benefit from data-driven care coordination, keeping more of the healthcare dollar within UnitedHealth.

**Customers/Clients:** UnitedHealth's reach is massive. **UnitedHealthcare insurance** serves about **50 million+ people** in the U.S. health benefit programs . At the end of 2024, UHC's U.S. medical membership was **49.35 million** (29.73M commercial; 7.85M Medicare Advantage; 7.43M Medicaid; 4.34M Medicare Supplement) . This grew by \~2.1M in 2024 (particularly in employer plans and Medicare) . Optum's customers include other insurers, employers, government health programs, and directly millions of consumers via clinics and pharmacy services. OptumHealth served **4.7 million patients** under value-based care by 2024 ; OptumRx manages pharmacy benefits for over 100 million people (processing 1.62B scripts) . UnitedHealth also employs or partners with a huge provider network (it is the **largest employer of physicians** in the U.S.) .

**Units/Subscriptions Sold:** Rather than traditional "units," UNH's volume metrics are enrollment and service utilization. Key figures:

-   Medical insurance **membership** \~50.7 million in U.S. (Q3 2024) . Within that: \~29.7M commercial members, 7.8M Medicare Advantage members (\~25% of all U.S. MA enrollees) , 7.4M Medicaid members , and several million international members (though global membership fell after selling some operations) .

-   **Prescriptions**: OptumRx processed \~1.62 billion adjusted scripts in 2024 .

-   **Patients**: OptumHealth 4.7M in value-based care (target +650k in 2025) ; Optum's provider services (including clinics and surgery centers) see tens of millions of patient visits annually (exact figure not disclosed publicly, but implied by revenue).

-   **Clients**: Thousands of employer customers and governmental agencies contract UHC for insurance; OptumInsight has a contract backlog of $32.8B in 2024 , indicating many long-term tech/service clients (hospitals, health plans). The sheer scale of these metrics underscores UNH's position as an industry leader.

### **2. Short-Term and Long-Term Headwinds & Risks**

**Short-Term (temporary impacts):** In the near term (next 1-2 years), UnitedHealth faces several cyclical and industry-specific headwinds:

-   **Elevated Medical Cost Trends:** As seen in 2023-2025, a surge in deferred care (e.g. seniors getting surgeries that were postponed) has driven up UHC's medical claims costs . If hospital utilization or expensive new therapies spike, UNH's margins can be squeezed until premiums can be adjusted. UNH warned that 2025 profits will be hit by medical cost growth above expectations . This is a short-term earnings drag but may normalize in subsequent years (the company expects to recover in 2026).

-   **Regulatory Rate Changes:** Government healthcare programs are significant for UNH (Medicare Advantage, Medicaid). Recent Medicare Advantage payment formula changes (and star quality rating downgrades) reduced 2024 revenue rates . While likely not permanent, these annual rate adjustments and **Medicaid eligibility redeterminations** (post-pandemic) temporarily cut enrollment or revenue growth in 2024 . For example, Medicaid membership fell by \~0.4M in 2024 due to the expiration of continuous coverage requirements . These policy headwinds could abate or reverse in coming cycles (UNH already expects Medicaid enrollment to return to growth in 2025 after contract wins) .

-   **Economic Factors:** A potential **recession** or higher unemployment could hit commercial enrollment as fewer people have employer insurance. However, this can be partially offset by more Medicaid enrollment (counter-cyclical). Inflation in medical supplies, labor, or pharmaceuticals is a short-term risk if costs rise faster than premium pricing. Thus far, UNH managed through general inflation, but persistent high inflation would pressure margins until premiums reset.

-   **Leadership Changes & Uncertainty:** In mid-2025, UNH's then-CEO Andrew Witty unexpectedly stepped down, and long-time former CEO **Stephen Hemsley** returned to lead . Management transitions can create uncertainty for a few quarters as the new/returning leadership reassesses strategy (indeed UNH paused and then cut guidance during this period). Investors will be watching closely how Hemsley addresses short-term operational issues. That said, his deep experience may quickly stabilize execution.

**Long-Term (structural risks):** Over the longer horizon, UnitedHealth must navigate significant external challenges:

-   **Regulatory/Political Risk of Healthcare Reform:** Being the largest private insurer, UNH is perennially exposed to U.S. health policy changes. The prospect of **Medicare for All** or a public option (though not imminent) represents a potential existential threat to the private insurance model. Even incremental reforms - such as aggressive **drug price controls** (which could squeeze OptumRx's margins) or stricter **Medicare Advantage oversight** - could permanently alter industry economics. For example, the U.S. Department of Justice is investigating risk adjustment practices in Medicare Advantage (including UNH) which could lead to fines or changed formulas long-term . Heightened antitrust scrutiny is also a risk: regulators are examining whether UNH's consolidation of insurance and providers (Optum buying doctor groups) is anti-competitive . A DOJ antitrust probe in 2024 into UNH's business combinations signals potential restrictions on future acquisitions or forced divestitures in extreme cases . These are structural issues that could cap UNH's growth or profitability permanently if not managed.

-   **Healthcare Cost Curve and Affordability:** Long-term, UNH's success depends on bending the healthcare cost curve. If U.S. healthcare spending continues to outpace GDP, eventually payers (including government and employers) may push drastic changes. UNH must prove that its integrated model can reduce costs. Otherwise, pressure from employers to cut premiums, or from government to lower Medicare Advantage rates, could become permanent. Similarly, an aging population means more high-cost patients - a growth opportunity for Medicare plans, but only if managed affordably. There's also the risk of **consumer/political backlash** against insurers and "middlemen" like pharmacy benefit managers (OptumRx). Industry reputation issues could materialize into restrictive regulations (e.g. banning certain PBM rebate practices) that permanently alter how UNH can profit .

-   **Technology Disruption:** Over time, tech companies or healthcare upstarts could encroach on UNH's businesses. For instance, new care delivery models (telehealth, at-home care) and data-driven platforms might reduce the need for traditional insurers or allow providers to take on risk directly. While UNH is investing heavily in technology (OptumInsight, etc.), the risk is that an outside innovator or disintermediation (e.g. large employers contracting providers directly) could erode UNH's role. However, UNH's scale and adaptive strategy (acquiring or partnering with disruptors) somewhat mitigate this risk.

-   **Workforce and Provider Relationships:** As the largest employer of physicians (tens of thousands through Optum) and with 400,000 total employees , UNH faces long-term risks in managing labor costs and avoiding provider alienation. If doctors or hospitals view UNH's reimbursement rates as too low, networks could narrow or quality suffer. The long-term sustainability of its provider-insurer integrated approach relies on balancing provider incentives. Additionally, retaining talent and culture in such a huge organization is an ongoing challenge; any deterioration could impact service quality and thus the customer base permanently.

In summary, **temporary headwinds** like the current care-cost surge or cyclical rate cuts are significant but likely manageable through pricing and time. **Permanent risks** revolve around the healthcare system's evolution - regulatory overhaul, cost control demands, and competition. UnitedHealth's size and diversification give it resilience, but also make it a prime target for reform efforts. The company's strategy of offering end-to-end solutions is partly to address these very risks by proving value. Nonetheless, investors should monitor policy developments and competitive trends closely, as they could fundamentally impact UNH's long-run growth trajectory.

### **3. Cost Structure: Fixed vs Variable Costs**

UnitedHealth Group's expenses include both **variable costs** that scale with customer utilization and **fixed costs** to run its operations. Understanding these costs is key to assessing margins and how they behave in different environments:

-   **Medical Claims (Variable Cost per Unit):** By far UNH's largest expense is **medical costs** - payments for members' healthcare services. In 2024, medical costs were about **85.5% of premium revenue** (medical care ratio) . These costs are variable, driven by how much care members use. On a per-member basis, UNH monitors "per member per month" (PMPM) costs. For instance, if utilization spikes (as in 2023-25), the PMPM cost rises. UNH then adjusts premiums in the next contract cycle. Essentially, **cost of goods sold** for the insurance segment is claims expenses, which run \~$0.85 per premium dollar . Each additional insured member adds variable claim costs. However, through scale and care management, UNH tries to reduce the cost per service.

-   **Operating Expenses (Fixed & Semi-variable):** UNH's **selling, general & administrative (SG&A)** costs include employee salaries, technology, buildings, marketing, etc. In 2024, SG&A was about **13.2% of revenue** (operating cost ratio) , down from 14.7% in 2023 due to efficiency gains. Many SG&A components are **fixed or step-fixed** - e.g. the cost for IT systems, corporate staff, or office leases does not directly rise with each new member. UNH had \~400,000 employees in 2024 ; labor is a major cost. **Labor expenses:** These include thousands of clinicians at Optum clinics and administrative staff for claims processing and customer service. While frontline healthcare labor (doctors, nurses) might scale with patient volume, administrative headcount can often handle growth up to a point (efficiencies of scale). UNH has been successful in keeping admin cost growth slower than revenue - evidenced by SG&A ratio declining, suggesting fixed-cost leverage . **Cost per employee** likely varies widely (from clinicians to call center reps), but total payroll and benefits run in the tens of billions annually (exact figure undisclosed, but given SG&A $53B in 2024 , much is labor).

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** UNH does incur marketing costs, especially to enroll seniors during Medicare Annual Enrollment and to win employer accounts. While not broken out publicly, these are part of SG&A. As a percentage of sales, marketing spend is relatively modest (UnitedHealth's brand and scale means it doesn't need to spend as heavily per new customer as smaller rivals). For instance, in 2024, SG&A was $53B , which included not only marketing but also technology and general overhead. We can infer marketing is a few billion dollars per year. This expense is semi-variable: during growth pushes (like entering new markets or product launches), UNH may ramp up marketing, but it can also be pared back in lean times.

-   **Key Cost Drivers and Trends:** The **most significant costs** are medical claims (variable) and salaries (partly fixed). **Medical cost trend** (inflation in unit cost of care and utilization rate) is the \#1 driver of short-term profitability swings. For example, in 2024 the medical cost ratio rose to 85.5% from 83.2% in 2023, reducing margins . Conversely, administrative efficiency improvements saved \~1.5% of revenue in 2024 . Over economic cycles, some costs shift: In a **recession**, utilization might temporarily dip (people defer care, helping margins), but bad debt could rise and membership mix shifts to lower-margin Medicaid. In **boom times**, higher utilization or wage inflation can pressure costs, but membership growth and pricing power improve. UnitedHealth historically manages medical costs via provider contracting and care management (e.g. encouraging preventive care through Optum). **Fixed-cost leverage** is a strength: as membership grew \~25% from 2015 to 2023, SG&A per member fell. The company's scale in IT and operations yields a durable cost advantage - it can spread fixed costs over 50 million members.

**Cost per Unit Example:** In 2023, net medical cost was $315 billion (estimated) spread over 1.1 billion member months (roughly 50M members \* 12 months \* some adjustment), yielding around $285 per member per month in medical costs on average. Labor cost per employee is harder to pinpoint, but if we assume an average $80k fully-loaded cost, 400k employees would be $32B (in line with a large portion of SG&A). **UnitedHealth's largest variable cost per member** is claims ($0.85 per $1 premium), while largest fixed cost is administrative ($0.13 per $1). The remainder (\~$0.02 per $1) becomes operating margin .

**Trends in Different Conditions:** During **high-growth periods**, fixed costs grow slower than revenue, boosting margins (as seen by operating margin rising from \~7% in 2015 to \~8.6% in 2022) . In **high inflation** times, UNH experiences margin pressure until it reprices contracts (usually annually). The company's cost structure is resilient - it quickly adjusts provider payment models and internal budgets to preserve margins. For example, after a period of elevated costs in 2022-2023, UNH cut discretionary expenses and improved efficiency (operating cost ratio fell 150 bps in 2024) . Overall, UNH's scale and diversification give it flexibility: fixed costs can be spread, and variable costs can be partly controlled (through provider networks, etc.). But healthcare's inherent volatility (e.g. a pandemic or new costly drugs) means UNH must continually manage these costs or face permanent margin shifts.

### **4. Management Team Analysis**

**Current Executive Leadership:** UnitedHealth's top management has recently changed. **Stephen J. Hemsley** is CEO (since mid-2025) . Hemsley previously led UNH as CEO from 2006-2017, overseeing tremendous growth (including the creation of Optum) . After stepping aside to Executive Chairman, he returned to the chief executive role in May 2025 when former CEO Sir Andrew Witty resigned for personal reasons . Hemsley is a seasoned leader known for operational discipline; he chaired the board from 2017-2025 and is deeply familiar with UNH's strategy. **John Rex** is the Chief Financial Officer (since 2016) , providing continuity through multiple CEOs. Rex is respected for prudent financial management - under his watch UNH consistently hit earnings targets (until the recent guidance revision) and allocated capital effectively (dividends, buybacks, acquisitions). The **UnitedHealthcare division** is now led by CEO **Tim Noel**(appointed Jan 2025) , after the tragic death of former UHC CEO Brian Thompson in late 2024 . Noel is an internal veteran, expected to maintain UHC's momentum. The **Board of Directors** includes figures like former Medtronic CEO Bill McNabb and others re-elected in 2025 , providing independent oversight. Overall, UNH's leadership features a mix of long-tenured insiders (Hemsley, Rex) and newer executives with fresh perspectives.

**Historical Management Performance:** Generally, UNH's management has a strong execution track record. **Goal attainment:** They have a history of meeting or exceeding earnings guidance (the Q2 2025 miss is a rare stumble). Over the past decade, management promised diversified growth and delivered - e.g. expanding Optum from \~25% to nearly half of revenue . They also set targets for dividend growth (20%+ hikes) and followed through each year. **Transparency and Problem Disclosure:** There have been instances raising questions - notably, in 2023-2024 management was arguably slow to warn investors of rising medical cost trends. In fact, a class-action lawsuit filed in 2025 alleges UNH leadership (Witty and Rex) misled investors about the company's strategy and the impact of rising costs . Additionally, management did not disclose a DOJ antitrust investigation to the public immediately; it came out via media, during which time some executives sold stock . These incidents have sparked some scrutiny of transparency. That said, UNH's leadership is generally regarded as competent and ethical, albeit operating in a complex, heavily-regulated space where **litigation and investigations** are not uncommon. When facing analysts, management typically provides extensive detail - for example, on earnings calls they detail membership growth, cost ratios, etc. Analysts find UNH's team to be highly knowledgeable, though recent calls have been more defensive given cost issues. The new/returning CEO Hemsley has a reputation for candor and focus on fundamentals, which may improve investor confidence. Importantly, many top leaders have been with UNH for decades (Hemsley since 1997, Rex since 2012, etc.), indicating stability and deep institutional knowledge.

**Tenure and Integrity:** The **average tenure** of the executive team is long. Hemsley (67) was CEO for 11 years previously. His interim successor David Wichmann served 2017-2021 before Witty took over. Witty, though an external hire (ex-GlaxoSmithKline CEO), did continue UNH's strategy; his departure was sudden, but reportedly for personal reasons and not due to performance. Stephen Hemsley's prior tenure was not without controversy - in the mid-2000s, UNH was involved in a stock options backdating scandal under then-CEO Bill McGuire, and Hemsley (then President) settled with the SEC (returning some compensation). Since then, Hemsley has restored trust and driven growth. Still, the recent stock sales by insiders ahead of public news (in late 2024) led to shareholder lawsuits claiming they possessed "material non-public information" . This has raised some eyebrows regarding integrity, but no findings of wrongdoing have been made and management asserts trades followed proper protocols . On balance, UNH's management is viewed as highly competent operationally, with some need to shore up investor trust post-2025 guidance missteps.

**Management Incentives and Ownership (Value Alignment):** UnitedHealth's leaders are significantly invested in the company's success. Many top executives receive the bulk of compensation in stock and options. For example, returning CEO Hemsley has an **annual salary of $1 million** and forwent annual cash bonuses, instead getting a one-time equity award ($60 million in stock after 3 years) - aligning his rewards with shareholder value. He already owned substantial UNH stock from his prior tenure (at one point he held over 1% of shares, making him a near-billionaire; some has been sold/vested over time). The **CEO-to-median employee pay ratio** was 352:1 in 2023 , reflecting large equity-based pay - which is only valuable if the stock does well. CFO John Rex was paid $16.1M in 2023 , largely equity. Insider ownership overall isn't extremely high (the largest individual holder was the late founder Phil Burgess and later Brazilian businessman Edson Bueno's estate \~0.97% ), but management collectively owns millions of shares. However, more recently, insider transactions have been scrutinized (the 2024 pre-investigation sales). We haven't seen large open-market insider purchases yet in 2025's downturn, which some investors would prefer to bolster confidence.

**Will Management Earn More from Stock Growth than Salary?** Likely yes. For Hemsley, his $60M stock grant (vesting only if he stays 3 years) dwarfs his $1M salary . Other execs similarly have substantial unvested stock. This theoretically incentivizes them to focus on **long-term share performance**. UNH has a culture of performance metrics in bonuses (e.g. medical cost targets, earnings, customer satisfaction). They tend to reinvest in the business (through acquisitions like Catamaran, Change Healthcare) which have paid off, thereby increasing share value over time. So far, management has delivered a **22% average ROE** in recent years and grown dividends over 20% per year - actions consistent with shareholder value creation. The recent stumble is a test: if management navigates the cost issues and resumes earnings growth, their credibility will be restored. **Integrity Questions:** Outside observers generally rate UNH's management highly, though politicians often criticize insurers broadly. There was an allegation in 2023 that UNH's algorithm denied care in Medicare plans leading to a lawsuit . The Board has also dealt with governance issues like the 2006 options incident (leading to improved governance practices since). Summarily, UNH's leadership has a strong execution history with minor blemishes. The return of Hemsley - who is deeply aligned via equity and legacy - suggests a renewed emphasis on consistent, transparent performance for investors going forward .

**Use of Voting Power:** It is worth noting that when UNH's board faced the star-rating downgrades and DOJ inquiries in 2024, there was *no* shareholder revolt - likely because the situation was fluid and management responded with corrective action (e.g. legal challenges, operational fixes). The likes of Vanguard/BlackRock generally engage via letters or meetings rather than proxy fights. In summary, **top shareholders** have been supportive and have not exercised voting rights to challenge management in a significant way. Their stands are usually aligned with governance best practices - e.g., they expect independent board leadership, which UNH has (Hemsley was Executive Chairman but stepped down from the board when he became CEO again, to maintain separation). As long as UNH performs, these shareholders will likely continue to quietly back the company, occasionally pushing for increased disclosures or climate risk oversight through their broader ESG policies.

### **6. Share Buyback Plan - Current and Historical**

UnitedHealth has consistently returned capital to shareholders via **share repurchases**, on top of its dividend program. The **current share buyback plan** (as of 2025) authorizes management to repurchase shares opportunistically; the exact remaining authorization isn't publicly disclosed in recent filings, but UNH's board typically refreshes the buyback authorization periodically. For example, in 2022 the board added authorization to bring the total to 100 million shares (not all used at once). In **2024**, UnitedHealth **repurchased about $9 billion of its stock** , following \~$8 billion in 2023 . The company indicated it returned **"over $16 billion to shareholders through dividends and buybacks" in 2024** , of which \~55% was buybacks, 45% dividends. During **Q1 2025**, UNH bought back nearly $3 billion worth (out of $5 billion total returned that quarter) - showing that despite stock price volatility, management continues repurchases, presumably considering the stock undervalued.

Comparing to the past decade, **share count has steadily declined** (from \~1.02 billion shares in 2013 to about 929 million in 2024) . That's roughly a **9% reduction over 10 years**, indicating modest but consistent buybacks. In dollar terms, annual repurchases ranged from \~$1.5-$4.5B in the early 2010s to **$5-$10B per year in the late 2010s/early 2020s**. The repurchase pace accelerated as UNH's earnings grew. For instance, in 2017-2019, UNH spent \~$4-$5B each year, and in 2020-2022 it increased to \~$5-$8B per year. The last few years saw elevated buybacks, e.g. $8B in 2023, $9B in 2024 . Notably, share count actually ticked **up slightly in 2017** (\~985M from 968M) because UNH issued shares for acquisitions (like the large Catamaran PBM acquisition in 2015). But subsequent buybacks offset that dilution. Over 10 years, UNH's **outstanding shares fell \~10%**, reflecting that management prioritizes returning excess cash.

**Current Strategy:** With the stock price down \~50% from its 2024 high, UNH is likely using buybacks as a value tool. The board last announced an authorization increase in mid-2022, and as of year-end 2024 there was ample capacity remaining. At the 2025 meeting, no specific new authorization was disclosed (suggesting existing authorization suffices). So, UNH can continue repurchases without further shareholder action. The **focus** is on offsetting dilution from stock-based compensation and then reducing share count to boost EPS. Historically, the **buyback yield** (repurchases as % of market cap) has been around 1-2% annually in the 2010s, rising to \~2%+ in recent years. In 2023, for example, UNH's \~$8B buyback was \~1.6% of its \~$480B market cap. In 2024, $9B was \~1.9% of the $465B cap.

**Comparison to Last 10 Years:** A decade ago (2015), UNH's buybacks were smaller (\~$1-$2B) due to capital allocated to acquisitions and growing the dividend. But as earnings roughly tripled from 2015 to 2022, UNH scaled up both dividends and buybacks. The last 10 years also saw **consistent dividend raises** (dividend grew from $1.88/share in 2015 to $8.18 in 2024, \~17% CAGR, see below) and buybacks being somewhat opportunistic - e.g. UNH paused net repurchases briefly during the uncertain early COVID period (2020) but then resumed strongly by late 2020 when cash flows proved resilient. Over 2015-2024, **shares outstanding fell \~6%** (from 986M to 929M) , meaning buybacks contributed to EPS growth, but most EPS growth came from actual earnings increase. Compared to some companies that retired 20-30% of shares in a decade, UNH's buyback approach is more moderate and balanced with its need to fund acquisitions. However, given the recent stock dip, management signaled confidence by continuing repurchases in 2025 (nearly $3B in Q1) . This suggests they view the stock as undervalued and are accelerating buybacks to capitalize on the low price - aligning capital return with shareholder interests.

In summary, UNH's buyback program has been **steady and growing** over the past 10 years, complementing a robust dividend. The current plan likely authorizes a similar or higher level of repurchases going forward (subject to earnings and investment needs). As of mid-2025, with the stock at multi-year lows, we expect UNH to at least fully utilize its existing authorization. Historically, management has been **disciplined** - repurchasing more shares in times of excess cash and attractive valuation, and slowing when shares were very high or when major acquisitions needed funding. This pattern should continue, benefiting long-term shareholders via reduced share count and enhanced EPS.

### **8. Intrinsic Value Estimation (Warren Buffett Method)**

To estimate **intrinsic value** of UNH, we'll use a simplified Warren Buffett-style **discounted cash flow (DCF)** focusing on "owner earnings" (which for UNH we approximate by free cash flow). Key assumptions: a **10% discount rate** (Buffett often uses \~10% as a hurdle), a **30-50% margin of safety** target, and a **terminal growth &lt;3%** (to be conservative given a mature business). We'll project three scenarios - **Conservative**, **Average**, and **Optimistic** - based on historical free cash flow (FCF) growth rates:

-   **Historical FCF:** UNH's FCF grew from \~$8.2B in 2015 to \~$25.7B in 2023 , roughly 15% CAGR, though 2024 saw a drop to $20.7B . The lowest growth year in the past decade (excluding the 2024 decline) was \~+9.7% (in 2023) and highest was +43% (2017) with typical years \~15-20%.

**Scenario 1 - Conservative:** **FCF growth = 5%** annually for 10 years (much lower than historical average), then 2% terminal growth. This might reflect a permanent slowdown (higher costs, saturation). Using 10% discount, we get an intrinsic value of **\~$360 per share**. Applying a **30% margin of safety** (MOS) would require buying below \~$250. A 50% MOS would be \~$180. *Current price $282 is above the 30% MOS threshold here*, meaning not quite a "deep value" under these pessimistic assumptions, but notably below the fully intrinsic $360 (so \~22% margin of safety exists).

**Scenario 2 - Average/Base:** **FCF growth = 12%** annually (the approximate historical average), for 10 years, then 2% terminal. Discounting at 10% yields intrinsic value \~**$630 per share**. With a 30% MOS, the buy target is \~$440; with 50% MOS, \~$315. *At $282, the stock is even below a 50% discounted value in this scenario*, implying a &gt;55% margin of safety - a potentially very attractive proposition if UNH can indeed sustain long-term double-digit growth.

**Scenario 3 - Optimistic:** **FCF growth = 18%** (near the higher end of past growth, though below the one-time 43% spike), 10 years, 2% terminal. This results in a value of **\~$1,010 per share**. Even a 50% MOS price would be \~$505. At this growth pace, the current price is extremely undervalued (stock would more than triple to reach fair value). However, this relies on UNH overcoming current issues and compounding earnings at a near-historical peak rate, which may be too rosy.

**Cross-Validation:** Let's check with Buffett's simpler earnings yield approach. UNH's 2024 adjusted EPS was about $24 (excluding the one-offs). At $282, that's a P/E \~11.7, which implies an earnings yield of \~8.5%. If we expect earnings to grow \~10% a year, then buying at this price yields a \~18.5% expected return (8.5% + 10% growth). Indeed, solving for the discount rate that equates DCF value to $282 suggests the market is pricing in roughly a **17-22% annual return**under mid-range growth assumptions (we found \~17% if 12% growth, \~22% if 18% growth, see analysis) - essentially the market is pessimistic.

**Expected Return (Margin of Safety = 0%):** If one **buys at $282**, what return can one expect? Using our base scenario (12% FCF growth), one would solve for the discount rate that yields $282 present value. That comes to about **\~17.2%**expected annual return. In the conservative case (5% growth), buying at $282 yields \~**12.1%**/yr - still above our 10% hurdle, but not by much. In the optimistic case (18% growth), it would yield an enormous \~22%/yr. These figures suggest that at $282, if UNH can even achieve somewhere between conservative and average growth, an investor could reasonably expect mid-teens returns - indicating a **significant margin of safety** is present relative to a 10% required return. For a more stringent investor requiring 15% returns, our DCF (conservative) gave \~$215 value, which is below current price - implying the stock needs some growth reacceleration to satisfy a very high hurdle rate.

In Buffett's terms, UNH at $282 appears to be **"selling at a significant discount to intrinsic value"** given its strong cash generation and prospects. Even accounting for caution (higher medical costs), the company's cash flows, if grown modestly, justify a substantially higher price. Our intrinsic value range roughly $360 (worst-case) to $630 (base-case) suggests the stock could be undervalued by **20-55%**. Buffett would also consider qualitative factors - UNH's moat in managed care, its diversification, and ability to raise dividends. Those are strong, which adds confidence that the intrinsic value will eventually be realized.

Of course, these estimates depend on execution. If structural issues permanently cut FCF (like regulatory changes reducing margins long-term), our growth assumptions might be too high. But UNH's historical adaptability and current low valuation tilt the odds in favor of an investment delivering solid returns. In conclusion, using Buffett's methodology with a fixed 10% discount and a healthy margin of safety, **UNH appears to offer an attractive risk/reward**, with current price well below intrinsic value (even after applying a sizable safety cushion in more optimistic scenarios).
