## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Salesforce (ticker: CRM) reported a strong **second quarter of fiscal 2026** (three months ended July 31, 2025), exceeding guidance on all major metrics. **Revenue** came in at **$10.2 billion**, up **10% year-over-year** (9% in constant currency). This was driven by robust growth in subscription and support revenue (the bulk of Salesforce's business) which rose **11% YoY to $9.7 billion**. GAAP **earnings per share (EPS)** were $2.91 (Non-GAAP EPS $2.12) for the quarter, beating consensus estimates of $2.78. The **operating margin** continued to expand - GAAP op margin reached **22.8%** (a company record), while non-GAAP op margin hit **34.3%**. Salesforce also saw strong cash flow, with operating cash flow up significantly, positioning fiscal 2026 to potentially reach **$15 billion in operating cash flow** for the full year, according to CEO Marc Benioff.

Notably, Salesforce returned substantial capital to shareholders in the quarter - about **$2.6 billion** total, including **$2.2 billion in share repurchases** and **$399 million in dividends**. In fact, Salesforce **expanded its share buyback authorization by $20 billion** (bringing the total program to **$50 billion**) in light of its strong cash generation. Management cited this as evidence of confidence in the business and a commitment to shareholder returns.

**Guidance** was also optimistic. Salesforce initiated Q3 FY26 revenue guidance of \~$10.25 billion (+8-9% YoY) and raised the full-year FY26 revenue outlook to $41.1-41.3 billion (about +8.5-9% YoY). Full-year FY26 non-GAAP operating margin guidance was increased to **34.1%**, underscoring Salesforce's focus on profitable growth. Overall, the quarter's results - record revenue, expanding margins, and boosted capital returns - led to a **positive stock reaction initially (stock rose \~1.4% after hours)**, though some analysts noted slightly *softening longer-term growth guidance*might have tempered enthusiasm in subsequent trading.

In summary, **Salesforce's latest quarter showed solid growth and margin improvement**, benefiting from strong demand for its cloud software and new AI offerings. Key metrics like remaining performance obligation (backlog) also rose 11% YoY to $29.4 billion, signaling healthy future revenue. Management highlighted the success of new AI products (such as "Agentforce") in over 12,500 deals and reinforced FY26 as on track for record cash flow. The company appears to be balancing growth with increased efficiency, which bodes well for shareholder value.

## **Business Model**

**Salesforce's Business Model:** Salesforce is a cloud-based software provider specializing in *Customer Relationship Management (CRM)* platforms delivered via a Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) model. The company generates revenue primarily by selling subscriptions to its enterprise cloud applications (such as Sales Cloud, Service Cloud, Marketing Cloud, etc.) and providing related support services . In other words, Salesforce's main source of income is recurring *subscription fees* from customers who use its CRM and other cloud software on a multi-year or ongoing basis.

-   **Main Sources of Income:** Salesforce reports two revenue segments: **(1) Subscription & Support** and **(2) Professional Services & Other** . Subscription and support is by far the dominant source, comprising roughly **93% of total revenue** in recent years. This includes fees for cloud software access and additional support beyond standard service. Professional services (including consulting, implementation, and training) make up the remaining \~7%. Within subscriptions, Salesforce further breaks down revenue by product categories. The largest product lines are its flagship application clouds:

    -   **Service Cloud** (customer service & support solutions) - about **23-24% of revenue**.

    -   **Sales Cloud** (sales force automation) - roughly **22% of revenue**.

    -   **Platform & Other** (includes the Salesforce Platform, Slack, AppExchange, etc.) - \~19%.

    -   **Integration & Analytics** (MuleSoft integration and Tableau analytics) - \~15%.

    -   **Marketing & Commerce Cloud** - \~14%.

    -   **Professional Services & Other** - \~6%.

-   *Visualizing Revenue Mix:* In FY2025, Subscription & Support contributed **$35.7 billion** (94% of $37.9B total revenue), while Professional Services & Other contributed **$2.2 billion** (6%). The pie chart below illustrates Salesforce's revenue breakdown by major product segment in FY2025:

(Service Cloud is the single largest product segment, about 23-24% of revenue, whereas consulting services are the smallest segment around 6%.)

-   **Number of Customers/Clients:** Salesforce serves a vast and diverse customer base. As of 2025, Salesforce had over **150,000 business customers worldwide**. (These are organizations of all sizes, from small businesses to Fortune 500 enterprises, that use Salesforce's platform.) Notably, Salesforce no longer discloses an official customer count in financial filings after 2016, but the **estimated client count surpassed 150k by 2025** based on industry analyses. This includes many of the world's largest companies across industries - for example, 90%+ of the Fortune 500 are Salesforce clients in some capacity. The **user base** (individual end-users at those client companies) numbers in the millions, since each enterprise customer might have dozens, hundreds, or even tens of thousands of employees using Salesforce's applications.

-   **Units/Subscriptions Sold:** Salesforce's offerings are sold primarily on a per-seat (per user) subscription license model, often with multi-year contracts. Thus, "units" can be considered as user subscriptions or cloud product licenses. While Salesforce doesn't publicize a total "seats sold" figure, we can infer scale from some metrics:

    -   **Total subscription contracts:** Salesforce had **over 12,500 deals for its new AI product Agentforce** within a few months of launch (6,000+ of which are paid subscriptions), indicating rapid uptake of new offerings.

    -   **Fortune 100 Adoption:** Nearly half of all Fortune 100 companies use Salesforce's AI/Data Cloud solutions. All top 10 Salesforce deals in late FY2025 included those new solutions.

    -   **Platform Usage:** Over **150,000 companies** use Salesforce overall . Assuming even a modest average of, say, 200 users per company, that would imply tens of millions of individual subscriptions. Some clients (like large multinationals) have thousands of Salesforce seats.

-   In summary, Salesforce's revenue model is high-volume and recurring: hundreds of thousands of cloud subscriptions generating billions in recurring revenue each quarter. The company's strategy is often to "land and expand" - i.e. sign up a new client for one cloud product, then cross-sell additional clouds or add-on features, increasing the number of subscriptions (and revenue per customer) over time.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

Like any global software leader, Salesforce faces both short-term headwinds and longer-term risks that could impact its business. We break these into **temporary, external factors** and **more structural, permanent challenges**:

**Short-Term / Temporary Headwinds:**

-   **Macroeconomic Slowdown:** A softening global economy or cautious corporate spending environment can slow Salesforce's growth in the near term. For example, in 2023 enterprise software purchasing became more scrutinized - customers delayed deals and optimized budgets, leading to Salesforce's revenue growth deceleration to single-digits. High inflation and rising interest rates made some businesses pull back on IT investments, which temporarily elongated Salesforce's sales cycles and lowered its growth from the double-digit rates of prior years. While Salesforce is resilient, it's **not fully immune to broader economic slowdowns**, which remain a short-term risk.

-   **Currency Fluctuations:** With about 33% of revenue from outside the U.S., foreign exchange volatility can be a headwind. A strong U.S. dollar, for instance, reduced Salesforce's reported revenue growth by \~1 percentage point in recent quarters. FX impacts quarter-to-quarter are transient, but can dampen reported results temporarily.

-   **Post-Pandemic IT Spending Priorities:** During 2020-21, many firms accelerated digital transformation (benefiting Salesforce). By 2022-23, some of that demand normalized or shifted. **Temporary budget realignments**- e.g. more spending on cybersecurity or infrastructure, less on new CRM projects - could create short-term soft spots for Salesforce until budgets expand again.

-   **Leadership Transitions & Reorganizations:** Salesforce saw significant **leadership turnover in late 2022-2023**, including the departure of Co-CEO Bret Taylor and several other key executives. While Marc Benioff remains firmly at the helm, these transitions caused some short-term uncertainty internally. The company's major restructuring in early 2023 - which included a 10% workforce layoff - could also have momentarily disrupted operations or sales productivity . These actions were aimed at improving margins, and indeed succeeded, but constituted short-term turbulence.

-   **Competitive Pressure on Deals:** In the near term, heightened competition (discussed more below) can force Salesforce to discount more or fight harder to win deals, especially large enterprise contracts. Recently, **Microsoft**in particular has aggressively bundled its competing Dynamics 365 CRM with other products. This could create *short-term pricing pressure* or slower win rates until Salesforce further differentiates with new features (like AI).

**Long-Term / Structural Risks:**

-   **Intensifying Competition (especially AI-driven):** Over the long run, Salesforce faces **stiff competition from mega-cap tech rivals** and upstarts alike. Microsoft, Oracle, SAP, Adobe, and others have deepened their CRM and cloud software offerings, often embedding AI capabilities. Microsoft's Dynamics 365 + Power Platform, for instance, leverages Microsoft's broad ecosystem and Azure AI, posing a formidable alternative for many customers. As AI becomes the new battleground in CRM, competitors with strong AI (like Microsoft's OpenAI integrations) could **chip away at Salesforce's market share** if Salesforce's own AI innovation falters. Additionally, smaller specialized SaaS firms (e.g. HubSpot in SMB CRM, ServiceNow in workflows) continue to nibble at segments of the market. Long-term, competition could erode Salesforce's growth or pricing power if the company doesn't maintain clear product leadership.

-   **Market Saturation & Slowing Growth:** Salesforce's revenue growth has moderated into the high-single digits recently. At over $40 billion annual revenue, the law of large numbers is catching up. The CRM market itself, while still growing, may not sustain past double-digit growth rates indefinitely. **Slowing revenue growth is a core concern** for investors - Salesforce averaged \~25% growth for many years, but guided \~8-10% for FY2026. Permanent low growth, if it sets in, could lead to a valuation reset for a stock historically priced for high growth. Management will need to find new growth vectors (like expanding into new industry clouds or deeper international penetration) to avoid long-term stagnation.

-   **Execution Risks (Integration & Innovation):** Salesforce has grown in part via large acquisitions (MuleSoft, Tableau, Slack, etc.). Successfully integrating these into a cohesive platform is a long-term challenge. For example, the $27B Slack acquisition (2021) and $15B Tableau (2019) took time to integrate and monetization has been gradual, raising questions about efficient capital allocation. A recent example: Salesforce announced an $8 billion deal to acquire *Informatica* (a data management firm) to boost its AI/data capabilities. However, if Salesforce struggles to integrate Informatica or any large buy, it could **distract management and dilute margins**. Execution missteps in product strategy (especially around AI features) are a longer-term risk - the company must continually **innovate its platform** to remain relevant. Failing to deliver meaningful AI ROI for customers by 2027 (the timeline Salesforce gave for major AI financial impact) could undermine its competitive edge.

-   **Regulatory and Data Risks:** As a custodian of vast amounts of customer data, Salesforce faces long-run regulatory risks around data privacy and security. Laws like GDPR in Europe and CCPA in California impose strict requirements on how data is stored and used. Compliance costs are rising. Moreover, as Salesforce embeds AI that uses customer data, it must navigate privacy carefully. Any major data breach or misuse of data could permanently damage trust. Additionally, **antitrust scrutiny** is a distant possibility - while Salesforce is not a monopoly (CRM market is fragmented), its dominance in certain verticals could draw regulator attention if it made another huge acquisition.

-   **Changing Customer Preferences:** Over decades, technology paradigms shift. If a paradigm like **low-code platforms** or **open-source CRM** were to disrupt the enterprise software landscape, Salesforce would need to adapt. There's a long-term risk that new approaches to managing customer relationships (for instance, AI agents or a unified data lake approach) could lessen the need for a traditional CRM interface. Salesforce's recent repositioning as "the \#1 AI CRM" shows awareness of this risk - it is betting on AI to augment its software. Nonetheless, failing to anticipate the next big wave (as happened to some legacy software firms in the cloud transition) is an existential long-term risk.

-   **Shareholder Activism & Governance Concerns:** In early 2023, several activist investors (Elliott, Starboard, ValueAct) took stakes in Salesforce, pushing for higher margins and reduced spending. While Salesforce cooperated and improved profitability dramatically, activists remain vigilant. A longer-term governance risk is if management lapses back into heavy spending or if leadership stability falters, activists could renew pressure. Additionally, **shareholder dissatisfaction** was evident when a majority of investors **voted against Salesforce's executive pay plan in 2024**. Such governance signals can hurt morale and retention of top talent, posing a subtler long-run risk if not addressed. Salesforce will need to keep aligning management incentives with shareholder interests to avoid prolonged discontent.

In summary, Salesforce's short-term headwinds center on *macro and execution noise*: a tough economy, FX swings, and internal reorgs. Its longer-term risks revolve around *strategic challenges*: maintaining growth in a competitive, evolving industry and executing well on integrations and innovation. The company's ability to navigate these will determine whether current headwinds remain temporary or become more permanent drags on the business.

## **Cost Structure**

Salesforce operates a software business model with high gross margins but significant operating expenses, especially in sales & marketing and R&D. We can break down the cost structure into **fixed vs. variable costs** and major cost categories:

-   **Cost of Revenue (Variable Cost per Subscription):** The direct cost to deliver Salesforce's cloud services (often called *cost of goods sold* for software) is relatively low per unit, since delivering an additional subscription largely involves providing access on Salesforce's multi-tenant cloud infrastructure. This includes expenses like data center hosting (server and network costs, whether Salesforce's own data centers or cloud hosting fees), customer support personnel, and third-party license royalties. Salesforce's **gross profit margins are around 75-78%**, indicating that cost of revenue is roughly 22-25% of sales. In FY2025, for example, gross margin was 77.2% - meaning for each $1 of subscription revenue, only \~$0.23 was variable cost of delivering the service. Thus, the "cost per unit" (per subscription) is low - perhaps on the order of 20-25% of the subscription price on average. **High gross margins**are a hallmark of SaaS: after initial development, serving additional users is relatively inexpensive, giving Salesforce strong economies of scale.

-   **Sales & Marketing (Largely Fixed/Labor Cost):** Salesforce historically has had very high Sales & Marketing (S&M) expenses, mainly consisting of salaries/commissions for sales reps and marketing program costs (advertising, events like Dreamforce, partner commissions). This is *partially variable* (e.g. sales commissions scale with bookings) but largely a fixed upfront investment to drive growth. In fiscal 2023, Salesforce spent **over $13 billion on Sales & Marketing**, which was more than double its R&D spend and made S&M the **largest operating expense by far** . Notably, S&M was **\~36% of revenue in FY2023** (13B on 36B revenue), although this ratio has been coming down after cost cuts. Much of S&M is labor - Salesforce employs thousands of sales reps; in fact, *sales personnel are the largest group of employees at the company* . Pre-2023, Salesforce was known to spend \~45-50% of revenue on S&M to fuel growth (a significant fixed cost commitment). During 2023's restructuring, Salesforce reduced S&M expense growth (laying off underperforming salespeople) to improve margins . Still, S&M remains the biggest cost category and critical for driving new subscriptions. It tends to move somewhat with revenue (commissions, etc.), but there is a large fixed component (e.g. base salaries, marketing campaigns) that management can adjust in downturns, as seen in 2023.

-   **Research & Development (Fixed/Labor Cost):** R&D is another major cost, comprising the salaries of software engineers and product developers creating new features and maintaining the platform. Salesforce's annual R&D expense in FY2025 was about **$6.0 billion** (roughly 16% of revenue), making it the second-largest operating expense after S&M . R&D does not vary directly with the number of customers; it's more fixed in the short term - the company invests heavily in developing AI capabilities, platform improvements, etc. to stay competitive. Over time, R&D has grown but Salesforce has kept it in the mid-teens percentage of revenue. This cost may tick up in new innovation cycles (like the current AI push) but provides long-term leverage once developed.

-   **General & Administrative (Fixed Cost):** G&A includes corporate overhead (finance, HR, facilities). This is smaller (on the order of 8-10% of revenue typically). It's mostly fixed costs (office leases, executive salaries, etc.). In 2023, Salesforce cut some G&A as part of its efficiency program (e.g. reducing real estate footprint by subleasing offices).

-   **Capital Expenditures (Variable with scale, but relatively small):** Salesforce's capex primarily goes to building out data center capacity and office facilities. The company's capex has been modest relative to revenue. For example, in FY2025 operating cash flow was $13.1B and free cash flow $12.4B, implying capex around $0.7B - only \~2% of revenue. Thus, **cost per unit (maintenance capex)** is low, since Salesforce can serve increasing users with fairly small incremental capex (thanks to cloud efficiencies). In cloud software, many "capacity" costs (like renting servers) are actually OpEx (hosting fees) rather than capex, depending on accounting.

**Most Significant Costs:** The single most significant cost for Salesforce is **personnel - especially in Sales/Marketing and Engineering**. Salaries, commissions, and stock-based compensation for its \~76,000 employees are a huge expense. Indeed, in FY2023, stock-based compensation alone was $3.3 billion , and total employee-related costs consumed a large portion of revenue. Within operating expenses, **Sales & Marketing is the largest component** (historically consuming \~50% of revenue at its peak, now being managed down into the 40% range or lower) . After S&M, **R&D** is the next biggest spend (\~16% of revenue in recent years). **Infrastructure/hosting (within cost of revenue)** is significant but smaller than the operating expenses categories - gross margin above 75% indicates these direct costs are relatively low. In summary, **labor costs dominate** - Salesforce is essentially spending to acquire customers (salespeople) and to develop product (engineers). Marketing events (like the massive Dreamforce conference) and advertising are notable but still part of that S&M budget.

**Fixed vs Variable and Trends:** Salesforce's cost structure has high fixed costs in the short run (big teams, R&D projects) but scales well as revenue grows. Over the past couple of years, Salesforce deliberately shifted from a "growth at all costs" approach to an "efficient growth" mindset due to activist pressure and market conditions. In early 2023, Salesforce cut \~10% of its workforce (around 7,000 people) to trim fixed costs. This had an immediate effect: the company's operating margin expanded substantially (to 19% GAAP in FY2025 from just 2% GAAP in FY2023). **In boom times**, Salesforce had been hiring aggressively and S&M and R&D costs grew in tandem with revenue (keeping margins low). But in tougher economic times, management proved able to *reduce or slow cost growth*, boosting margins. For example, travel and marketing spend was curtailed during the pandemic and not fully restored, helping profitability. Going forward, Salesforce signaled a "new focus on disciplined spending" - we see S&M expense as a percentage of revenue declining in a trend toward \~35% or lower, and non-GAAP operating margins already hitting mid-30s%.

During different **economic conditions**, Salesforce's costs behave as follows:

-   In **recessions or slowdowns**, Salesforce can pause hiring, reduce event marketing, and rationalize its real estate footprint (as it did in 2023). Thus, fixed costs become somewhat flexible - they did layoffs and also **froze major hiring in 2022-23**, which significantly improved operating income. We saw Salesforce's headcount actually drop \~11% in 2023 due to these cuts .

-   In **high-growth periods**, Salesforce invests heavily - adding thousands of staff, spending freely on marketing (glitzy conference keynotes, etc.), which boosts the top line but compresses margins. This was evident in the 2010s when Salesforce consistently reinvested to chase 20%+ growth, keeping GAAP profits near zero.

To illustrate cost trends: In FY2020 (calendar 2019), operating margin was only \~4% because Salesforce plowed \~46% of revenue into S&M and \~18% into R&D. By FY2025, after the cost reforms, GAAP operating margin hit 19% - S&M had dropped to \~36% of revenue and R&D held around \~16%. So **in summary**, Salesforce's cost structure has a large fixed component (people costs), but management has shown it can adjust these when needed. The most significant cost area is Sales/Marketing, which is being managed more tightly now (with more focus on efficient digital marketing vs. always expanding the sales force). If economic conditions worsen, Salesforce can further trim variable spend (like reducing contractor usage or marketing ads) to protect margins. Conversely, if growth reaccelerates, expect Salesforce to ramp up investments again (though likely not to prior extremes, given investor expectations for profitability).

Overall, Salesforce's costs are now on a trajectory of **lower growth relative to revenue**, enabling margin expansion. The company demonstrated in 2023 that it can maintain growth while significantly cutting cost growth - for instance, achieving 10 consecutive quarters of operating margin expansion through mid-2025. This trend indicates a maturation of the cost structure: fixed costs are being leveraged better over a larger revenue base, moving Salesforce into a more cash-generative phase after years of heavy reinvestment.

## **Management & Governance**

**Executive Leadership and Board:**

-   **Marc Benioff (Co-Founder, Chair & CEO):** Marc Benioff is the iconic founder who has led Salesforce since its inception in 1999. He is the longest-serving executive and the public face of the company. Benioff served as co-CEO for a period (with Bret Taylor in 2020-22) but returned to sole CEO in early 2023. As of 2025, he remains **Chairman of the Board and CEO**, driving strategy especially around growth and new initiatives like AI. Benioff is known for his visionary approach to cloud computing and strong sales instincts, as well as his outspoken style on social issues and corporate philanthropy (Salesforce's 1-1-1 model). He also retains a significant personal stake (\~2.3% of shares as of 2025), aligning him with shareholder outcomes.

-   **Current Executive Team:** Beyond Benioff, Salesforce's C-suite in 2025 includes:

    -   **Robin Washington - President and Chief Operating & Financial Officer (COO & CFO)**. (Washington is a newer appointee as CFO, stepping in after longtime CFO Amy Weaver moved to another role. Robin Washington had been a board member and was named interim CFO; she now oversees day-to-day operations and finance.)

    -   **Gavin Patterson - Chief Strategy Officer** (former President and CEO of Salesforce International, and ex-BT Group CEO).

    -   **Paula Goldman - Chief Ethical and Humane Use Officer** (unique role reflecting Salesforce's values).

    -   **Parker Harris - Co-Founder & CTO**, still at Salesforce since 1999 focusing on technology direction.

    -   **Other key execs** include the heads of each Cloud (e.g., CEO of Tableau/Salesforce Analytics, CEO of Mulesoft unit, etc.), though Salesforce has streamlined some of these roles post-acquisitions.

-   The **Board of Directors** in 2025 is composed of a mix of technology and business leaders. Notables include **Sanford Robertson** (a veteran tech banker), **Neelie Kroes** (former EU Commissioner), **Colin Powell** (until his passing), and recently added **Mason Morfit** (CEO of ValueAct Capital) who joined as part of activist discussions. The board has 12 members, and Benioff as Chairman wields significant influence.

-   **Historical Leadership:** Salesforce's growth was fueled by key past executives like **Bret Taylor** (former co-CEO and prior CTO, who resigned in Nov 2022) and **Keith Block** (former COO & co-CEO 2018-2020). Other co-founders like **Parker Harris** remain in senior roles (CTO). Longtime CFO **Mark Hawkins** (2014-2021) oversaw many acquisitions. The departure of Bret Taylor (who co-created Google Maps and joined Salesforce via acquisition) was notable - he was seen as a potential successor to Benioff, but left to pursue his own ventures. Another prominent historical figure is **Stewart Butterfield** (CEO of Slack) who briefly stayed after Slack's acquisition but left in early 2023. The executive turnover around 2022 led Benioff to "restack" the bench, even re-hiring some former execs ("boomerangs") like Ariel Kelman as CMO.

-   **Founder's Influence:** Marc Benioff, as founder, brings continuity and a culture of innovation. His presence ensures Salesforce remains mission-driven (with philanthropy integrated) and product-focused. There is no other "founder figure" of similar weight on the current team (Parker Harris is low-profile). Thus, Benioff's eventual succession is a key issue - currently unresolved after Bret Taylor's exit. For now, Benioff appears committed to leading for the foreseeable future, especially as Salesforce transitions into an AI-centric strategy.

**Past Performance & Integrity:**

-   **Execution on Goals:** Salesforce's management has a track record of aggressive goal-setting and generally meeting or exceeding guidance. For many years, Benioff publicly aimed for revenue milestones (e.g., $20B, then $50B targets) and pursued them via both organic growth and acquisitions. There have been instances where growth targets were revised - for example, Salesforce tempered its FY2023 guidance when macro headwinds hit, but then over-delivered on margin improvements beyond initial promises. On the recent efficiency push, management *did*follow through: they promised 25%+ non-GAAP operating margin by FY2026, and as of mid-2025 they were already at 34% non-GAAP, far surpassing that goal. This suggests management can execute on cost control when committed.

-   **Transparency with Investors:** Generally, Salesforce's management has been candid about broad issues but sometimes *obfuscates specific metrics*. For instance, they stopped reporting precise customer counts after 2016, focusing on other metrics like remaining performance obligation. Some analysts have noted that Salesforce can be **opaque about acquisition integration costs or exact organic growth rates**, folding acquisitions quickly into consolidated numbers. There's no evidence of wrongdoing, but a complexity in reporting that can mask underlying trends. However, on calls, Marc Benioff is often quite open about challenges - in 2022 he acknowledged Salesforce had issues with sales execution in certain verticals and pledged to fix it. A noteworthy event: in 2022 and 2023, **management faced tough questions from analysts about slowing growth and attrition of sales talent**. Benioff was at times defensive (famously attributing some sales execution issues to "new remote employees not being productive" during the pandemic). Nonetheless, the company did address the problems (changing sales leadership and cutting underperformers). There haven't been major scandals about hiding performance problems; Salesforce typically addresses issues within a quarter or two.

-   **Analyst Communication:** On earnings calls, Marc Benioff is charismatic but can be *light on specifics*, often pivoting to big-picture themes (like "the agentic AI enterprise"). The CFO (previously Amy Weaver, now Robin Washington) provides more numerical answers. Analysts have prodded about things like decelerating billings or exact Slack revenue - management's answers are sometimes vague, focusing on combined platform success rather than breaking out details. This has led to some frustration, but not to the level of mistrust - more like Salesforce is *promotional* in tone. Importantly, in early 2023 when activists got involved, Benioff and the board took their feedback and instituted changes (cost cuts, new board members), showing a willingness to adapt to investor concerns.

-   **Integrity and Scrutiny:** There have not been serious allegations of financial misconduct by Salesforce's leadership. The **largest integrity question** came in 2022 when shareholders voted down the executive pay packages, signaling a view that management's pay was excessive relative to performance. Proxy advisors cited a lack of clear rationale for large equity grants to Benioff. Management responded by saying they'll consider stockholder input on pay going forward. Another point: some activists argued Salesforce was slow to right-size expenses (i.e., management was "fat and happy" chasing growth while margins lagged). Once pressured, though, management delivered on promised margin improvements faster than expected. There haven't been public questions of personal integrity of key execs (no fraud or scandal around Benioff et al). Salesforce has a generally positive reputation on governance (aside from the pay issue) and is known for its **ethics initiatives** (even a Chief Ethical Use Officer role as mentioned).

-   **Management Stability:** Tenure varies. Benioff has been there 24+ years. Parker Harris (CTO) as well. Others like Bret Taylor and Keith Block had shorter tenures at the top (3-5 years in co-roles). Turnover at the C-suite in 2022 was unusually high, but Salesforce has since stabilized the team by promoting internally and bringing veterans back. The current CFO (Robin Washington) is seasoned (former CFO of Gilead Sciences) and board member, which provides stability. The average tenure of top executives (excluding Benioff) now is perhaps 3-4 years, given newness of some roles - so management *depth* is something to watch. But Benioff's enduring presence is a stabilizing factor.

**Management's Skin in the Game (Value Alignment):**

-   **Equity Ownership:** Marc Benioff owns an estimated **\~21.9 million shares** of Salesforce, roughly 2.3% of the company (worth about $5.3 billion as of Sep 2025). This is a significant portion of his wealth, meaning he is heavily incentivized to increase Salesforce's long-term stock value. Other current executives have smaller stakes, mostly via stock grants. For example, former co-CEO Bret Taylor had accumulated shares but sold upon leaving. Among current execs, insiders collectively (excluding Benioff) hold only a few percent of shares. The board and executives do receive substantial stock compensation annually, tying part of their financial fate to the share price. Notably, **activist Mason Morfit (ValueAct)** on the board likely also holds a stake (ValueAct had \~3.5 million shares in 2023), aligning his board participation with shareholder interests.

-   **Compensation vs Performance:** Salesforce's leadership historically had generous compensation - in FY2024, Benioff's total pay was \~$39.6M, which shareholders deemed too high given performance. The large stock grants in those packages suggest that management's future wealth does depend on stock performance (if shares don't appreciate, those grants won't pay off as much). However, investors clearly signaled they want compensation more tightly linked to sustained results, not one-time grants. We expect Salesforce to modify pay plans to include more performance-based vesting. The fact that **shareholders rebuked the pay plan** shows that management will be under pressure to demonstrate *earned* rewards (through EPS growth, margin, etc.), which could better align their incentives with long-term company health.

-   **Insider Trading Signals:** In 2025, Benioff set up a 10b5-1 plan and has periodically sold small tranches of stock (a few thousand shares) as part of scheduled diversification. These sales have been relatively minor compared to his holdings, and he even **exercised options and held some shares** (indicating he's not cashing out entirely). There haven't been notable *insider buys* on open market dips - rather, management generally receives stock via grants. That said, insiders did not significantly dump shares even when Salesforce's stock fell \~50% in 2022; Benioff in particular still holds roughly **11.9 million shares directly after recent plan sales**, on top of trust holdings. Some activists would like to see Salesforce execs *personally buy stock when it's down* (as a show of confidence), but so far insider purchases have been rare. Management's tone suggests confidence in AI driving a new growth cycle, but investors will watch if FY2027+ returns to, say, low-teens growth or not.

In conclusion, **Salesforce's management and governance** present a mix of founder-driven vision, recent responsiveness to investors, and a need for continued discipline. ## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

Warren Buffett's criteria for a great company include reasonable valuation, consistent earnings/cash flow, strong returns on capital, and solid financial footing. Let's evaluate Salesforce against each suggested standard:

**1. Valuation Discipline:**

-   **P/E ≤ 15 (vs own history):** *Fail.* Salesforce's price-to-earnings ratio has historically been far above 15. Even now, after the stock's pullback and margin improvements, its trailing P/E is around **26** (using FY2025 GAAP EPS \~$6.50 and stock \~$165) - well above the ≤15 benchmark. In earlier years, Salesforce's P/E was not meaningful due to near-zero earnings (e.g., P/E was effectively hundreds or negative in 2015-2020) . So by Buffett's traditional metric, Salesforce has been too expensive relative to earnings. Only if one uses forward *non-GAAP* earnings could P/E approach the high-teens, but on GAAP it's still elevated. Historically, investors paid a premium for Salesforce's growth (P/S multiples &gt;8 and P/E in triple-digits). Thus, it fails the ≤15x earnings yardstick.

-   **P/B ≤ 1.5:** *Fail.* Salesforce's price-to-book is about **2.7** in 2025 ($164 share vs \~$62 book per share) - and that's after a stock decline. For most of the decade, P/B ranged from \~5× up to 10× in growth years (since book value was small relative to market cap until recent acquisition-driven equity boosts). For example, in 2018, P/B was \~7.8 ; even now it's \~2.7. Salesforce's abundant goodwill/intangibles (from acquisitions) inflate book value to $61B , but the market cap is \~$157B, still higher. Buffett's 1.5× book test is very stringent for a software company - Salesforce doesn't meet it.

-   **Margin of Safety (Pay ≥25% below intrinsic value):** *Probably Fail.* This is subjective, but given Salesforce's current valuation and growth, one could debate intrinsic value. Our rough DCF later suggests an intrinsic value per share perhaps around $205 in a conservative case (and higher in optimistic cases). The stock at \~$165 is about 20% below that $205 - that's close but *not a full 25% discount*. If one were more conservative about growth or used a higher discount rate, intrinsic might be lower than market price. So a clear 25% margin of safety is not evidently present. In mid-2022 when CRM stock hit \~$130, one could argue it approached a margin of safety, but at current levels, it's not obviously ≥25% undervalued relative to conservative intrinsic value estimates. Thus by Buffett's "pay 75 cents on the dollar" rule, Salesforce stock does not deeply satisfy this - it's possibly near fairly valued or modestly undervalued, but not a screaming bargain with huge margin of safety.

**2. Cash Generation & Allocation:**

-   **Positive Free Cash Flow each year; FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:** *Pass.* Salesforce has generated positive free cash flow consistently for over a decade. Even when GAAP net income was negative, operating cash flow was strongly positive (due to upfront subscription collections and high non-cash expenses like stock comp). For instance, in FY2015, FCF margin was \~15%; in FY2024, FCF was $9.5B on $34.9B revenue (≈27% margin) , and in FY2025 FCF margin was \~33% (over $12B FCF on \~$38B revenue) . These easily exceed the &gt;5% criterion. Salesforce's FCF has been robust, typically ranging from 15% to 30% of revenue, which is an excellent sign. It *passes* both the consistency and the &gt;5% threshold with flying colors.

-   **Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:** *Inconclusive/Fail.* This metric looks at how effectively the company turns retained earnings into market value or additional earnings. Salesforce historically retained all earnings (no dividend until recently) and even accumulated negative retained earnings until 2022 (due to past losses). Now that it's profitable, we can attempt to gauge: From FY2015 to FY2025, Salesforce's cumulative retained earnings (or increase in book equity excluding new stock issuance) is hard to parse due to many acquisitions funded by stock, which muddies the measure. Alternatively, one can compare the increase in EPS to retained earnings. Salesforce's EPS grew from roughly -$0.06 in 2015 to \~$6.50 in 2025, but this was not linear and accompanied by acquisitions, so not purely from retained profits reinvested. Another approach: Buffett often measures *RORE* by how each $1 retained translated into market cap gain. Over 10 years, Salesforce retained all profits and the market cap rose from \~$39B to \~$157B (increase of $118B). It retained maybe $15B in cumulative earnings over that period (a rough guess, given net losses then big gains). If so, that's a very high return - but again, acquisitions and bullish market conditions helped. It's complicated, but given the inconsistency (e.g., large decline in market cap in 2022 despite profits), we can't say Salesforce steadily converts retained earnings to 12%+ returns. Arguably, the *recent* retained earnings (FY2024 net $4.1B, FY2025 net $6.2B) were largely used for buybacks and internal investment, and the stock is about flat over that span, indicating subpar near-term return on those retained funds. Overall, I'd mark this as **not clearly passed** in Buffett's sense, since Salesforce's heavy reinvestment via M&A didn't yield proportional per-share earnings growth until very recently. It's not a straightforward pass or fail, but leaning fail given the long period of low ROE.

**3. Growth & Predictability:**

-   **EPS record: Positive & rising every year for 10 yrs:** *Fail.* Salesforce's GAAP EPS was negative or negligible in many years prior to 2018, so it does *not* have a 10-year streak of growing earnings. In fact, EPS swung from a loss to profit and then dipped again in 2023. For example, EPS (GAAP) approximately: 2015: -$0.42; 2016: -$0.07; 2017: $0.47; 2018: $0.46; 2019: $0.15; 2020: $0.15; 2021: $1.05; 2022: $1.48; 2023: $0.21; 2024: \~$4.00; 2025: \~$6.50 (these are rough). Clearly not consistently rising - there were declines (notably 2023 EPS collapsed due to big one-time charges) . Buffett likes an earnings chart that goes up monotonically - Salesforce's looks more jagged. If using non-GAAP EPS (adding back stock comp etc.), it is smoother (non-GAAP EPS grew more steadily and was positive each year). But Buffett would insist on GAAP. Thus Salesforce fails the "every year up" test - it had years of earnings decline and even losses in the 10-year span.

-   **Book value per share 10-year CAGR ≥ 10%, no year of decline:** *Pass (with caveat).* Salesforce's *book value per share* grew from \~$6.02 in 2015 to \~$66.25 in 2025, a CAGR of \~27% - far above 10% . And book value per share never declined year-over-year in that period; in fact it leaped in years where stock-financed acquisitions closed (e.g. +117% in 2020 from Tableau) and continued rising each year. So strictly speaking, yes, 10-year book CAGR &gt; 10% and no annual contraction in book value. However, this was **not due to retained earnings from operations alone** - it was largely fueled by issuing shares at high valuations to acquire companies, thus increasing equity. Buffett's original intent is organic growth in intrinsic value. Here, book value growth is skewed by M&A. But from a numbers perspective, it passes this criterion (shareholders' equity never shrank and grew very strongly). One caution: Buffett would adjust for intangible goodwill - much of Salesforce's book is goodwill. If one considered *tangible* book, that might be flat or lower in some years. But the stated metric is book per share, so on that measure Salesforce passes robustly.

-   **Owner's Earnings positive each year, OE/Market Cap ≥ 10%:** *Partially Pass.* "Owner's Earnings" (Buffett's term) roughly equals free cash flow (net income + depreciation/amort - capex - working capital needs). Salesforce's owner earnings have been *positive every year* for at least the past decade (given positive free cash flow yearly). Even when GAAP net was negative, Salesforce's large depreciation, amortization, and deferred revenue allowed it to produce cash. For example, in FY2016 when net was -$47M, operating cash flow was +$1.61B - clearly owner's earnings were positive. So it passes the consistency of OE &gt; 0 each year.
    > Now, **OE/Market Cap ≥ 10%** is a tough hurdle (that implies a 10% "earnings yield"). Currently, using FY2025 FCF \~$12.4B as owner's earnings and market cap \~$157B, we get about **7.9%** (OE/MC) - below 10%. In earlier years, the ratio was much lower because the market valued Salesforce at a premium to its cash flows (e.g., 2020 FCF \~$3.8B vs market cap $162B was \~2.3%). Only briefly in late 2022 did Salesforce's stock dip enough that FCF yield neared 5-6%. The criterion asks for ≥10%, which Salesforce has *never* offered until perhaps the recent slump - and even now it's under 8%. So it fails the 10% owner earnings yield test. This indicates Salesforce was always priced richly, not at a bargain yield.

**4. Balance-Sheet Safety:**

-   **Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:** *Pass.* Salesforce's debt/equity ratio is quite low. By FY2025, total debt $11.4B vs equity $61.2B = **0.19** . Even at peak debt (after Slack acquisition FY2022, debt \~$14B, equity \~$58B), D/E was \~0.24 . In earlier years, D/E floated around 0.5 or below (e.g., 2015 debt $2.19B, equity $3.97B, D/E ≈0.55 - slightly above 0.5 that year) , but for most of the decade it was under 0.5. Salesforce finances acquisitions partly with cash/debt but also issues equity, so it hasn't become overleveraged. Its **current D/E \~0.2** is very comfortable. So overall, yes, it passes this with ease (except arguably FY2015 where it was just above 0.5 due to small equity base, but as an overall pattern it's good).

-   **Interest Coverage (EBIT/Interest ≥ 3×):** *Pass (mostly).* Salesforce's interest expense on its debt (which is mostly low-rate bonds) is quite low relative to operating income. In FY2025, operating income was $7.205B ; interest expense was around $**(roughly $200M)** (the FY2025 10-K shows interest \~$163M net expense for first 9 months, so annual maybe \~$220M). That yields EBIT/interest \~ **33×**, hugely above 3×. Even in lean years like FY2023, EBIT $660M vs interest \~$185M gave coverage \~3.6× - barely above 3, but still technically above 3x . The worrisome moment was FY2023: operating profit dropped to $660M while interest on Slack-related debt was still owed, coverage got tight but stayed just above 3. In all other years, either EBIT was high enough or interest negligible, so coverage was fine. Thus Salesforce generally passes this test (with the note that in FY2023 it was borderline; however, that year had heavy one-time charges depressing EBIT). Given FY2024-25 coverage is back to robust levels, no ongoing concern here.

-   **Interest Expense / Operating Income ≤ 15%:** *Pass.* This is effectively the inverse of coverage. For FY2025, interest \~$0.2B vs op income $7.2B is under 3%. In FY2024, op income $5.0B vs interest \~$200M ≈4%. Even in weak FY2023, interest $185M vs op $660M \~28% - okay, that one year fails the ≤15% threshold due to near-zero op profit . But aside from that anomaly, Salesforce's interest expense is a small fraction of operating profit. Buffett's criterion likely expects normal years; Salesforce's normalized interest/EBIT is well under 10%. The company carries debt but at low rates, and it generates far more operating earnings than needed to cover interest. So essentially passes (except the FY2023 hiccup). Salesforce's balance sheet is strong enough that interest burden is minimal in the context of earnings.

-   **Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:** *Fail.* Salesforce's current ratio (current assets/current liabilities) has typically been just over 1.0. It tends to carry large deferred revenue (a current liability) that balances out cash. For instance, at Jan 31, 2025, current assets were \~$27.0B vs current liabilities \~$23.5B (including $22.7B deferred revenue) - giving a current ratio around **1.15** (approx). Historically, it's hovered near 1.0-1.2. It's rare for a subscription software company to have a high current ratio because deferred revenue (collected cash for future service) boosts liabilities. Salesforce's **quick ratio** (excluding deferred rev) is healthier since deferred revenue isn't a cash outlay obligation but a service obligation. But strictly on current ratio, Salesforce is below 1.5 in most years. It usually maintains enough cash to cover obligations but not a lot more (because it invests excess or does buybacks now). So by Buffett's conservative &gt;1.5 measure, it fails. However, one could argue the nature of deferred revenue means liquidity risk is low - Salesforce has plenty of cash on hand ($14B cash vs $13B of deferred rev due &lt;1yr) . Still, numerically, current ratio &lt;1.5, so not a pass.

**5. Profitability & Efficiency:**

-   **Gross Margin ≳ 40%:** *Pass.* Salesforce's gross margins are \~73-78% annually. Well above 40%. Even including professional services (low margin), overall gross margin never dipped below \~73% in the last decade. So clearly passes.

-   **Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:** *Fail.* Salesforce's net margin is improving but still below 20%. In FY2025 it was \~16.4% . Before that, it was single digits or near zero. Only on a non-GAAP basis (excluding big stock comp and amortization) does Salesforce's net margin approach 20% (e.g. non-GAAP net margin in FY2025 was about 22% by some analyses). But Buffett uses actual net income. Historically, Salesforce prioritized growth over profit, yielding low net margins. It's now targeting high teens GAAP net margin going forward, but hasn't averaged ≥20%. So this criterion is not met.

-   **ROE ≥ 15% average for 10 yrs:** *Fail.* Return on equity (net income/equity) was very low for much of the decade because net income was low and equity was growing. For instance, ROE was around 0-3% in years 2015-2020, spiked to \~10% in 2025. Over 10 years, the average ROE is probably under 5%. Even currently, FY2025 ROE = $6.2B / $61.2B ≈ **10.1%** . So not 15%. Salesforce's ROE is dampened by the large equity from acquisitions. But Buffett wants 15% sustained - Salesforce hasn't delivered that on GAAP basis. It's improving (if net margin hits \~20% and asset turnover remains decent, ROE could rise), but historically fails.

-   **ROIC (or ROTC) ≥ 12% (10-yr avg):** *Fail.* Return on invested capital (ROIC) for Salesforce has been modest. Taking an approximation: NOPAT / (debt + equity). In FY2025, NOPAT (\~$5.8B after tax) / (debt $11B + equity $61B) ≈ 8.5%. In earlier years it was much lower. So 10-yr average ROIC is likely in low single digits. This is common for growth companies that accumulate goodwill. Still, by Buffett's standard, Salesforce hasn't shown a high ROIC. Only if we use *non-GAAP earnings* (which add back amortization of goodwill) might "adjusted ROIC" look a bit better (maybe low teens in 2025), but on reported numbers, no.

-   **ROA ≥ 6%:** *Borderline.* Return on assets = net income / total assets. In FY2025, ROA = $6.2B / $102.9B = **6.0%** , just meeting the criterion. However, 10-year average ROA is far below 6% (because net was tiny while assets grew). Only in the last two years did ROA approach the mid-single digits (FY2024 ROA \~4.2%, FY2025 \~6%). Buffett wants a consistent ≥6%. Salesforce is only now hitting that threshold. So historically, fail; currently, just barely a pass at 6%. We'd mark it as not consistently meeting the standard over the period evaluated.

-   **Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:** *Pass.* Salesforce's depreciation plus amortization has been running at about 5-8% of gross profit. For example, in FY2025, D&A was roughly $2.0B (est) while gross profit was $29.25B, so \~6.8%. Even including heavy amortization of acquired intangibles, it stays under 10%. Earlier years it was similar or even lower (because Salesforce historically used operating leases etc., so depreciation on owned assets was small; amortization grew with acquisitions but still manageable). Thus, the company is not overly asset-intensive; a large portion of expenses is human capital (R&D, S&M) rather than depreciation of equipment. Buffett likes businesses that don't require heavy capex - Salesforce fits that, as its **capex and depreciation are a small fraction of its gross profits**. This criterion is comfortably met.

**Summary of Buffett Checklist:** Salesforce **fails many of Buffett's classic tests** for a "great company at a great price." Specifically, its valuation has typically been too high (P/E, P/B, earnings yield all out of range), and its earnings record was erratic until recently. It only scores well on certain quality metrics like high gross margins, positive free cash flow, and low debt - signs of a strong business model financially. But on the metrics of *consistent high returns on capital* and *predictable growth in per-share earnings*, Salesforce falls short. It only in the last couple years started showing double-digit net margins and decent ROE, whereas Buffett's ideal company would have done that for a decade or more.

In Buffett terms, Salesforce's **"moat"** (market dominance in CRM) is undeniable, but it came at the cost of heavy spending - meaning past profitability was low. Now margins are improving, so one could argue it's moving in the right direction. However, if we strictly apply Buffett's checklist:

-   Earnings and book per share growth: Book grew (mostly via acquisitions), earnings did not steadily grow until recently - *not the textbook consistent compounding he prefers*.

-   Return on retained earnings: unclear, likely not great historically.

-   The stock's valuation only now, after a \~30% drop from highs, is approaching a possibly fair value (but still not obviously a "margin of safety" bargain).

On the plus side, **Salesforce has tremendous free cash flow generation and a strong balance sheet**, which Buffett would appreciate. But as of 2025 it likely wouldn't tick the box of a proven long-term cash cow with stable high returns on equity like, say, an Apple or Coca-Cola in Buffett's portfolio.

Thus, Salesforce *partially meets* the quality standards (market leadership, high gross margin, growing FCF) but **fails the strict financial criteria** (valuation, 10-year profit consistency, high average ROE). It might be seen as a great company in the making, but it's only recently turning its growth into significant profits. Buffett might admire Salesforce's business, yet historically he'd avoid its stock due to the high price and uneven earnings record. (In fact, Berkshire did invest briefly in Salesforce in 2020 as a smaller position, indicating some interest, but it was not a long-term big holding - possibly reflecting these very concerns.)

## **DCF Valuation (Conservative, Nominal, Optimistic)**

To estimate Salesforce's intrinsic value, we perform a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis under three scenarios:

-   **Conservative Case:** Uses the **lowest free cash flow (FCF) growth rate** Salesforce has seen in the past decade as the future growth rate.

-   **Nominal (Base) Case:** Uses the **average FCF growth rate** of the past decade.

-   **Optimistic Case:** Uses the **highest FCF growth rate** observed historically.

**Step 1: Determine historical FCF growth rates.** Salesforce's free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capex) has grown significantly, but with fluctuations:

-   In FY2023, FCF was $6.313B, up \~19.5% from prior year .

-   FY2024 FCF $9.498B, up 50.4% .

-   FY2025 FCF $12.434B, up 30.9% .

-   Earlier years saw variability: e.g., FY2022 FCF was $6.313B (down \~11.9% from FY2021), FY2021 FCF \~$7.17B (which was up \~62% from FY2020's \~$4.42B).

-   The **lowest year-over-year FCF growth** in the past 10 years was actually negative: FY2022 saw -11.9% (a decline, due to Slack acquisition costs and one-time items) . Excluding declines, the lowest positive growth was +*about 2%*in one earlier year, but let's take a cautious approach: we'll use **5%** annual growth in the conservative scenario (essentially assuming very mild growth, roughly in line with GDP inflation, effectively stagnation).

-   The **average FCF growth** over 10 years is high (because FCF grew from \~$0.8B in 2015 to $12.4B in 2025, a CAGR \~32%). However, that was fueled by margin expansion and acquisitions - not sustainable indefinitely. A more normalized forward growth might be lower. We can calculate an approximate average of yearly growth rates for last several years: say (50%, 19%, -12%, 51%, 31% etc.) - it's volatile but roughly averages to \~28%. That seems too high going forward. Instead, to be realistic, we'll use **15%** as an average sustainable FCF growth (Salesforce's own FY2026 guidance is \~12-13% OCF growth, but with improving margins maybe mid-teens is doable in the medium term).

-   The **highest FCF growth** year was FY2021 at +83% (pandemic rebound, cost cuts), or FY2018 which more than doubled from small base. The peak sustainable might be around 30-40%. We'll choose **25%** for optimistic, as an ongoing growth rate (sustaining 83% is unrealistic, but 25% for a period might represent strong AI-fueled expansion).

**Step 2: Set assumptions for each case:**

-   **Conservative:** FCF growth = **5% per year** for next 10 years, then terminal growth 2%. This assumes Salesforce's cash flow barely grows (perhaps due to saturation or economic stagnation). 5% is essentially the floor growth one might expect given inflation and some business expansion.

-   **Nominal/Base:** FCF growth = **15% per year** for 10 years, then terminal growth 2%. This assumes Salesforce continues moderate growth (mid-teens) as it expands internationally and monetizes new products (AI Cloud, Data Cloud), but not hyper-growth. 15% is within reach considering recent 10%+ revenue growth plus margin expansion.

-   **Optimistic:** FCF growth = **25% per year** for 10 years, then terminal growth 3%. This assumes a robust scenario: Salesforce successfully leverages AI to upsell customers, significantly boosts revenue growth back to \~15% and also expands margins, yielding very high FCF growth. 25% annually for a decade is aggressive - it implies FCF would almost decuple (grow \~9.3× in 10 years). This could happen if, say, operating margin goes to 40% and revenue doubles, but it's a best-case stretch.

We'll use Salesforce's FY2025 free cash flow as the starting point: FCF\_2025 = **$12.434B** .

We also need a discount rate. Buffett often uses \~10% as a hurdle for equities. We'll use **10%** as the discount rate (reflecting Salesforce's risk profile: large cap, stable cash flows, perhaps a WACC \~8-9%; we use 10% for margin of safety).

Terminal growth rate: For conservative and base, use 2% (approximately inflation). For optimistic, maybe 3% if we think long-term CRM market could outpace inflation slightly.
