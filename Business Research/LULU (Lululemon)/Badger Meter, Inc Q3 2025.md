## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Badger Meter reported strong results for the third quarter ended September 30, 2025. **Total sales were $235.7 million, up 13% year-over-year**, driven by broad-based demand for smart water solutions . Excluding the contribution of the SmartCover acquisition (completed in January 2025), base sales still grew 8%, reflecting healthy organic growth . **Operating earnings increased 13% to $46.1 million**, with operating profit margin essentially flat at 19.6% . On a non-GAAP "base" basis (excluding SmartCover), operating profit margin expanded to 20.7% , demonstrating improved leverage on higher volumes. Net income likewise rose - **diluted EPS was $1.19, up 10% from $1.08 a year ago** , beating analyst estimates (EPS beat by \~7% and revenue by \~2.7% according to Yahoo Finance ).

Gross margin in Q3 2025 was **40.7%, up 50 basis points** from the prior year's 40.2% . This expansion was attributed to favorable product mix (more high-margin smart meters and software) and pricing actions that helped offset higher costs (like tariffs) . Selling, engineering and administrative (SEA) expenses rose about 3% on a base basis, and included some non-recurring benefits; SEA as a percent of sales was roughly flat at 21.1% . The company's effective tax rate was 26.1%, a bit higher than last year, so **net profit growth (10% EPS increase) trailed operating profit growth** .

Importantly, **cash flow was very robust**. Badger Meter generated $51.3 million of operating cash in Q3, enabling continued investment and a cash buffer . The company increased its dividend by 18% to an annual rate of $1.60 per share - marking the **33rd consecutive year of dividend growth** . This underscores management's confidence in ongoing cash generation.

In the earnings call, CEO Kenneth Bockhorst noted that **demand remains strong** despite "ever-evolving macroeconomic, trade and policy headwinds" . Water utilities and industrial customers are accelerating adoption of Badger's smart water technologies (like cellular AMI meters and analytics software). The company remains optimistic that these secular trends will support **"high-single digit" revenue growth on average over the next five years** . Based on pricing power and product mix gains, Badger Meter even raised its target gross margin range from 38-40% historically to **39-42% going forward** . In short, the latest quarter's results showed **double-digit growth, expanding margins, and strong cash flow**, positioning the company well to continue investing in innovation and growth initiatives.

## **Business Model**

Badger Meter, Inc. is a leading provider of **flow measurement and control solutions**, with a core focus on water metering technology. The company's business model centers on selling hardware devices (meters and sensors) along with software and services that help municipal water utilities and other customers **measure, monitor, and manage water usage** . Badger generates revenue from two main product lines:

-   **Utility Water Solutions (approximately 85% of net sales)** - This is Badger's largest segment, comprising water meters and related communication systems, software analytics, and services sold to municipal water utilities. Badger offers a broad range of water meters (both traditional mechanical and advanced ultrasonic meters) and ultra-rugged ORION® radio endpoints which transmit usage data via cellular networks. They also provide the BEACON® SaaS analytics platform and consumer-facing EyeOnWater® app for usage tracking . The utility segment's solutions enable automated meter reading (AMI/AMR), leak detection, water quality monitoring, and overall smarter water network management. Utilities typically deploy Badger's meters across their service area and rely on the accompanying software and data services on an ongoing basis, yielding some recurring revenue in the form of software subscriptions or Network-as-a-Service fees .

-   **Flow Instrumentation (approximately 15% of net sales)** - This smaller segment includes meters, valves, and sensors for industrial markets beyond municipal water. These products measure and control flows of liquids (and gases) in **industrial processes**, with Badger focusing on water/wastewater, HVAC, chemical, and other fluid applications . The flow instrumentation line leverages similar technologies as the utility products (e.g. electromagnetic or ultrasonic flowmeters), but sold to industrial OEMs and commercial customers through distributors . While this segment is more cyclical and had flat to modest growth recently, it broadens Badger's customer base and provides cross-selling opportunities for water quality monitoring solutions.

**Revenue Mix:** Badger Meter's income is thus primarily derived from product sales to utilities (water meters & network devices) and to industry (flow meters), with an increasing contribution from software and services. As of 2023, about **85% of revenue came from the utility water segment and \~15% from flow instrumentation**, as noted above . Within the utility segment, there is further breakdown by product type - for example, Badger has millions of legacy mechanical meters in the field, but growth is coming from ultrasonic smart meters and cellular network endpoints. The company has been expanding its SaaS and Network services, which provide recurring revenue (software subscriptions, data hosting) on top of the one-time hardware sales . This shift toward a more software-centric model is gradually increasing the portion of predictable, recurring income.

**Customer Base:** Badger Meter's customers include **over 3,000 water utilities worldwide**, plus numerous municipalities and commercial/industrial users . The U.S. municipal water market is highly fragmented - thousands of local water departments - and Badger has longstanding relationships across many of them, having been in business for over a century (founded 1905). No single customer accounts for more than 10% of sales , indicating a broad and diverse client base. The company is considered an incumbent leader in North American water metering, trusted by utilities for accurate billing and water management. It has deployed **"millions of endpoints for thousands of customers"** over the years - a testament to the scale of its installed base. For instance, its ORION® cellular endpoints (radio modules attached to meters) have **over 10 million units deployed across more than 3,000 utility systems** . This large installed footprint creates a degree of **vendor lock-in and recurring replacement demand**: water meters typically last 15-20 years, so utilities periodically replace and upgrade them, often sticking with the same vendor's system for compatibility . Badger's ongoing sales include not only new deployments for infrastructure projects, but also **replacement cycles** and add-on sales (new software modules, additional sensors, etc.) to existing customers, which provide a stable revenue stream.

Aside from utilities, Badger's industrial customers include manufacturers and process industries who need flow measurement in their operations. These are served through distributors and integrators. While smaller in revenue share, this segment provides growth opportunities in areas like **water quality analytics** (augmented by acquisitions of companies like s::can in 2021 and ATi in 2020, which specialized in water chemistry sensors) . Badger can bundle these sensors with its flow meters to offer more comprehensive solutions (e.g. monitoring both flow and water quality in industrial wastewater treatment).

**Units and Subscriptions:** In terms of volume, Badger Meter sells **hundreds of thousands of water meters annually**, and has a cumulative installed base in the millions. For example, one source notes ORION radio endpoints have **over 10 million units deployed** . On the subscription side, the company's BEACON® SaaS platform has been adopted by many utility customers - whenever a utility implements cellular AMI, they typically subscribe to BEACON analytics for meter data management. While exact subscription counts are not disclosed, Badger's software revenue was over **$56 million in 2024 (growing \~30% YoY)** , indicating a substantial number of endpoints under subscription. In Q3 2025, management highlighted *"secular drivers influencing digital water technology adoption"* and continued *"robust adoption rates for our cellular AMI offering, including higher sales of ORION® Cellular endpoints \[and\] BEACON® SaaS"* . This suggests that more utilities are signing onto Badger's Network-as-a-Service model, where they pay recurring fees for data rather than operating their own networks . Each new endpoint deployed can bring not just hardware revenue but ongoing service revenue.

**Moat and Competitive Advantages:** Badger Meter enjoys several competitive advantages that form its "moat":

-   **Deep Customer Relationships & Installed Base:** Badger has been a trusted supplier to water utilities for decades. It is one of the "Big Three" in U.S. water meters (alongside Neptune/Roper and Sensus/Xylem) . Utilities are traditionally cautious and value reliability - Badger's long track record and large installed base confer credibility. The **switching costs** are significant: if a city has tens of thousands of Badger meters and a Badger data system, moving to a competitor would be costly and complex (requiring new meters, retraining staff, etc.). This often gives Badger an incumbent advantage when replacement cycles come around . Furthermore, Badger's massive installed endpoint base generates a wealth of field performance data and customer feedback, which it uses to continuously improve products - a virtuous cycle newer entrants would struggle to replicate .

-   **Technological Leadership & Innovation:** Badger has invested heavily in innovation, particularly in ultrasonic metering and digital networks. It introduced one of the first commercially successful ultrasonic water meters (the E-Series®) and has been a pioneer in cellular AMI (Advanced Metering Infrastructure) deployments . Its **BlueEdge™**suite now encompasses IoT sensors, cellular endpoints, and advanced analytics in a unified offering . This breadth of technology is hard for smaller players to match. Badger holds numerous patents on meter designs, and continuously updates its product line to maintain accuracy and durability standards (e.g. lead-free brass meters, static meters with no moving parts, etc.). Its R&D spend is typically around 3-4% of sales - which in 2024 was \~$29 million - helping it stay at the forefront of smart water tech.

-   **Comprehensive End-to-End Solutions:** Unlike some competitors that might specialize in either meters or communications, Badger offers a **full stack solution** (meter hardware + communications network + software + support) under one roof . This one-stop-shop appeal is significant for resource-constrained utilities: they can rely on Badger for everything from installing meters to hosting their data and even helping analyze usage patterns. Badger's **Network-as-a-Service** model shifts the burden of network management from the utility to Badger (and its cellular partners), which is a compelling value proposition . The BlueEdge branding highlights how Badger can integrate various elements - flow measurement, pressure sensors, quality sensors, analytics - into a tailored solution for each utility . This broad portfolio creates **cross-selling opportunities** and makes it harder for point-solution competitors to displace Badger, since utilities prefer a unified system.

-   **Strong Brand Reputation:** Badger Meter has built a reputation for accuracy, ruggedness, and customer service over many years. In the conservative water utility industry, having a proven and **reliable brand** is a major moat - utilities "can't afford to get it wrong" on meters that bill customers. Badger's meters are known for quality and its service teams support customers in the field (training, project management) . This **trust and brand loyalty** often give Badger an edge in competitive bids. Even as new competitors try to enter (some tech startups are offering IoT water sensors, etc.), Badger's brand and installed base provide credibility that is difficult to quickly acquire.

-   **Long-Term Relationships and First-Mover Advantages:** Badger has been a **"first mover"** in many technological transitions in the water metering space (such as the shift from manual read to automatic meter reading, and now to cellular AMI) . Its early moves gave it unmatched experience and data: for example, by deploying cellular endpoints for over a decade, Badger gathered vast data on network reliability, which it uses to optimize performance and reassure new customers . This history is a moat - a newcomer might promise a new metering gadget, but utilities will question if they have 10+ years of field validation like Badger does. Additionally, Badger's **strong distributor and partner network** extends its reach. It has regional sales offices and reps intimately familiar with local utility needs. These entrenched channels make it hard for outsiders to gain traction.

In summary, Badger Meter's business model is selling *"razor-and-blade"*-type solutions (meters and endpoints as the razors, software and data services as the blades) to a broad base of utility customers. The company enjoys a solid moat built on its large installed base, trusted brand, and integrated technology offerings. This yields substantial recurring replacement sales and growing subscription revenue, providing stability and growth opportunities for the future .

## **Headwinds & Risks**

Badger Meter faces several headwinds and risks in both the short term and long term, stemming from economic, political, and industry-specific factors. We break these down into **temporary vs. potentially permanent impacts**:

**Short-term / Temporary Factors:**

-   **Supply Chain & Input Cost Inflation:** Like many manufacturers, Badger Meter has dealt with **fluctuating raw material costs** (for components like brass, semiconductors, and plastic resins) and periodic supply chain bottlenecks. In recent years tariffs on brass castings and electronics from China, as well as pandemic-related disruptions, pressured input costs and caused component shortages . These issues can compress margins or delay deliveries in the short run. Badger mitigated this by dual-sourcing parts and passing cost increases to customers via price hikes . Nonetheless, sudden spikes in commodity prices or new tariff policies represent ongoing volatility that can impact quarterly results. The company noted that tariff costs have been an "unpredictable trade environment" and it is **monitoring global trade dynamics closely** . Should input inflation spike beyond what pricing actions can cover (or if supply chain delays slow production), Badger's earnings could be temporarily hit until conditions normalize.

-   **Municipal Budget Cycles and Macroeconomy:** Badger's core customers are municipal water utilities, whose capital spending can vary year-to-year based on budget health and government funding. In an economic downturn or tight city budgets, utilities might defer meter replacement projects temporarily. For instance, during the 2008-2009 recession, Badger still grew earnings, but some order timing was affected as cities tightened belts . Similarly, while water utilities are fairly recession-resilient (water use is essential), short-term factors like delays in federal infrastructure bill disbursements or election-year budget freezes could shift some orders out by a few quarters. Management acknowledged that **variability in customer order timing is typical**, e.g. Q4 is often slower due to holidays and budget cycles . Economic slowdowns could temporarily moderate Badger's growth rate if utilities take longer to approve new AMI projects.

-   **Foreign Exchange Fluctuations:** Badger earns the majority of its revenue in the U.S., but it does have international sales (Canada, Europe, Asia) and foreign operations. A stronger U.S. dollar can be a headwind, making its exports more expensive or reducing the translated value of foreign sales. In recent years, currency effects have not been very large (the U.S. comprised \~90%+ of sales) , but as Badger expands globally, FX swings could introduce some short-term earnings volatility.

-   **Integration of Acquisitions:** In the near term, Badger must successfully integrate recent acquisitions (like SmartCover in Jan 2025). M&A can pose temporary risks such as integration costs, culture fit issues, or distractions for management. Thus far, Badger has a good track record with acquisitions (ATi, s::can, etc.), but it must deliver the expected **sales and cost synergies from SmartCover** as promised . Any short-term hiccups in integration could affect results in the acquisition's first year.

-   **Competitive Bidding Pressure:** Occasionally, Badger may face **aggressive pricing by competitors** in bids for large utility contracts. For instance, a rival might temporarily cut prices to win a strategic city contract. This could pressure Badger's win rate or margins in the short run if it chooses to match prices. However, given the value of Badger's technology, it typically competes on value rather than lowest price. The risk is more that in any given quarter, if a large contract award goes to a competitor, Badger's revenue could be a touch lower. But with so many small utility customers, the impact of any single lost bid is usually not material.

In summary, short-term headwinds like **input cost spikes, supply chain issues, or timing of orders** can create some quarterly lumpiness, but Badger has shown ability to manage through these with pricing actions and operational agility . These factors are not permanent and are likely to **moderate over time** (for example, supply chain conditions have been easing and Badger is now raising its margin outlook accordingly ).

**Long-term / Structural Risks:**

-   **Technological Disruption and Competition:** One key risk is the evolving competitive and tech landscape in smart water. While Badger is a leader, there are other major players (Neptune and Sensus are the other top North American meter firms , plus Itron, Honeywell/Elster, etc.) and smaller tech startups targeting niches like leak detection IoT devices. A **larger, well-capitalized rival could attempt aggressive pricing or introduce a breakthrough technology** that pressures Badger's market share . For instance, if a competitor significantly undercuts the cost of AMI endpoints or bundles water meters with other smart city solutions, Badger might face share erosion in new tenders. Management mitigates this by continuous innovation and highlighting the proven reliability of its products . Nonetheless, the risk remains that over a longer horizon, new technology (e.g. ultra-low-cost IoT sensors, alternative metering methods) could **disrupt the industry's traditional models**. Badger's significant R&D and acquisition program is aimed at staying ahead of such trends (for example, acquiring startups with promising tech rather than letting them become threats).

-   **Changes in Utility Procurement or Standards:** Municipal utilities can be slow to change, but if industry standards or regulations were to shift, it could impact Badger. For example, if a regulatory body mandated an open-standard communication protocol for all water meters, it might reduce the differentiation of Badger's integrated solution (though Badger would likely adapt its products to comply). Alternatively, if government cybersecurity or data privacy requirements for utility networks become very stringent, Badger will need to ensure its software meets those standards or risk being sidelined. There's also a long-term risk that **municipal water systems consolidate**(via regional utilities or private operators) - larger consolidated utilities might have more bargaining power to demand lower prices or might choose a single vendor globally. Currently, fragmentation in the U.S. market plays to Badger's strength (since it can serve small and mid-size utilities very well). Consolidation, if it happens, could intensify competition for a few big buyers.

-   **Macroeconomic and Infrastructure Funding Environment:** Over the long run, Badger's growth is aided by secular needs (aging water infrastructure, water scarcity, need for efficiency). However, if there were a prolonged period of government austerity or low infrastructure spending, utilities might defer technology upgrades for many years. Water infrastructure in the U.S. is badly in need of modernization, but it relies on funding. Conversely, robust funding (like the U.S. Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act) is a tailwind. The **risk is if political or economic shifts reduce these funding flows**, it could permanently slow the adoption of smart water solutions. This risk seems moderate, as the trend toward smart metering has strong momentum, but one can't fully rule out political changes that deprioritize water projects.

-   **Climate and Environmental Factors:** Interestingly, climate change is both a driver and a risk. Increasing droughts and water scarcity in some regions are pushing utilities to invest in better metering and leak detection (a positive for Badger). However, extreme events (like if a region's water supply collapses or population shifts away) could reduce the number of active water connections in some areas. Also, as more aggressive water conservation codes come in, overall water usage might decline, potentially impacting utilities' budgets. These factors likely won't *eliminate* the need for meters (if anything, conservation increases the need to measure every drop), but they form part of the backdrop in which Badger operates. The company's focus on sustainability (its products help save water) aligns it well with climate imperatives, so we consider this more an opportunity than a threat in the long run.

-   **International Expansion Risks:** As Badger grows globally, it faces typical international risks: unfamiliar regulatory regimes, local competition, and political instability. For example, water utilities in some countries prefer domestically made meters due to local regulations or trade barriers. Badger's strategy has been targeted - focusing on regions with water stress and where its technology can leapfrog legacy systems. Still, scaling outside North America means contending with new competitors (e.g. Arad Group in Israel, Diehl in Europe, etc.), and navigating any trade restrictions or import tariffs (some countries might impose standards that favor local suppliers). The risk is not existential, but it could cap Badger's market share in certain overseas markets.

In evaluating permanent vs. temporary impacts: **short-term challenges like cost inflation and supply chain snarls are easing and largely manageable** with pricing and sourcing strategies . They are not expected to permanently impair the business model. In contrast, **long-term competitive and technological shifts are more material** - if Badger were to mis-execute or fall behind on innovation, that could erode its moat over time. So far, management's actions (such as consistently upgrading products and acquiring complementary tech firms) indicate they recognize and are addressing these strategic risks. Another permanent factor, the overall water infrastructure push, seems favorable to Badger's prospects (a tailwind).

One area to watch is **cybersecurity and data privacy** in smart metering. As water systems become digital, ensuring data security is paramount. A major cybersecurity incident (not necessarily at Badger, but in the industry) could slow utility adoption or impose new costs. Badger will need to continue demonstrating robust security in its AMI networks (the use of private cellular networks is a plus for security, versus public internet transmission ).

In summary, Badger Meter's largest long-term risks - *losing technological leadership or a structural change in how utilities buy* - seem well-managed at present, but they underscore the importance of **continuous innovation and customer engagement**. As one analyst noted, Badger's decades of market leadership give it unmatched customer data and insight, which it uses to fend off competition . Short-term economic and cost issues appear to be transient; indeed, the company is currently raising margins and seeing strong demand despite those headwinds . Therefore, while Badger is not immune to cyclical or macro hiccups, its core business appears resilient, and its management is proactively mitigating both temporary and permanent risks.

## **Cost Structure**

Badger Meter's cost structure includes a mix of variable costs (materials, manufacturing labor, etc.) associated with producing its meters and devices, and fixed costs (overhead, R&D, SG&A) to support its operations and sales efforts. We break down the key cost components and their trends:

**Cost per Unit (COGS):** The cost of goods sold for Badger primarily consists of **raw materials, component parts, and direct labor** to manufacture meters and equipment. Key materials include **metal housings (bronze/brass or plastic), electronic components (sensors, chips, batteries, radios), and mechanical parts** (for legacy mechanical meters). As of 2024, cost of sales was $497 million (for $826.6M revenue) , implying a gross margin of \~40%. This suggests the average cost of a meter or endpoint is around 60% of its selling price. Variable material costs are significant - items like brass castings, for example, have seen price inflation. Badger has **navigated input cost swings by using multiple suppliers and redesigning products for cost efficiency** . The cost per unit can decrease with volume (economies of scale in production), and indeed Badger's gross margin has improved in recent years partly due to higher volume spreading fixed manufacturing overhead . Implementation of lean manufacturing and automation in Badger's plants has also lowered unit costs over time. Nonetheless, **COGS remains the largest expense category**, comprising materials (which can be \~30-40% of sales) plus direct labor and overhead allocated to production.

**Labor Expenses:** Badger Meter has about **2,210 employees** worldwide . Labor costs include manufacturing employees at its production facilities (in the U.S. and abroad), as well as engineers, sales staff, and administrative personnel. Many manufacturing workers are semi-skilled, and Badger must remain competitive in wages especially in tight labor markets. Labor is partly variable (production line staffing can scale with demand) but also has fixed elements (a baseline staff and skilled engineers that are retained regardless of volume). In recent years, wage inflation has been a factor - Badger likely faced higher salary costs to attract talent in engineering and to reward shop employees. The company's SG&A (Selling, Engineering, Administration) expense was $200 million in 2024 , which is \~24% of sales. This includes a large portion of payroll for management, R&D engineers, sales & marketing staff, etc. **Engineering/R&D labor** is vital - Badger spends \~4% of sales on R&D (about $30M in 2024), much of which is salaries of technical staff . Over time, as revenue grows, SG&A has grown in absolute dollars but has **leveraged down as a % of sales** (for example, SEA was 25% of sales a decade ago vs \~21-24% more recently ), indicating productivity improvements and cost discipline. Key point: while labor costs do rise, Badger's growth and efficiency gains have kept labor's impact on margins stable or improving slightly.

-   *Manufacturing Footprint:* Badger performs a good portion of its manufacturing in North America. This includes machining and assembling meters, as well as electronics assembly for endpoints. The company likely has **fixed costs** for facilities, machinery depreciation, and plant management. These fixed costs mean when volume increases, unit cost falls (contributing to higher gross margin). Conversely, during slowdowns, under-utilized capacity can hurt margins. Badger has managed this well historically, keeping plants flexible. Also, it outsources some sub-components (e.g. it may buy cast meter bodies from foundries, or electronics from contract manufacturers) to convert some costs to variable.

**Advertising & Marketing:** Since Badger sells mostly B2B to utilities and industry, its marketing spend is relatively modest (no large consumer advertising). Marketing costs mainly involve **sales team expenses, trade shows, technical product literature, and customer training programs**. These would be part of SG&A. The company has regional sales managers and uses manufacturer's reps in some territories . It also invests in pilot projects and trials for utilities, which could be considered a marketing cost (to demonstrate the technology). Overall, marketing is not a huge cost driver; it's likely well under 5% of sales. For instance, **SureDividend analysis notes Badger's SG&A as containing selling expenses but does not highlight advertising as significant**. We can infer this by the fact that Badger's SG&A includes R&D and general admin - pure "sales and marketing" is a subset. Given the nature of the business, **personal relationships and technical sales efforts** are more important than mass advertising. So marketing expenses scale roughly with sales (more customers means more account managers, etc.), but there are efficiencies as the installed base grows (existing customers are cheaper to retain).

**Which Costs are Most Significant?** The most significant costs for Badger are the **Cost of Goods Sold (materials/components)** and **personnel costs**. Material costs (metals, electronics) have a direct impact on gross profit - for example, rising copper/brass prices can squeeze gross margins if not offset. Labor, especially engineering and manufacturing labor, is also critical. We can see evidence in the financials: in 2023, Badger's gross profit was \~$276M on $704M sales, implying $428M COGS (materials, labor, overhead). Meanwhile total operating expenses (which are largely salaries in SG&A and R&D) were around $158M (calculated from $118M operating income + those expenses) . So **COGS (materials+prod labor) \~61% of sales, SG&A+R&D \~22%**, making those the top cost buckets. Depreciation is a smaller cost ($12M/year) , and Badger's capital intensity is relatively low (capex was just $12M in 2023, indicating not heavy machinery needs) . Thus, **raw materials and people are the dominant cost drivers**.

**Cost Trends Over Time:** Over the past decade, Badger Meter has benefited from scale and efficiency, which improved its cost structure. Gross margins have risen from mid-30s% to around 40% , due in part to **higher average selling prices (from advanced products) and better cost control**. For example, newer ultrasonic meters have fewer moving parts (potentially lower assembly cost) and carry higher price points, boosting margin. The company also executed cost reduction programs (lean manufacturing, supply chain optimization). During periods of rising material costs (e.g. tariff imposition in 2018-2019), Badger successfully **implemented strategic price increases to customers** to preserve margin, showing pricing power. In different economic conditions, Badger tends to manage costs prudently: in downturns, it can trim discretionary spending, and its variable cost structure (materials, some contract labor) naturally scales down if volume falls. One piece of evidence: during the 2008-09 recession, Badger actually **improved EPS**, implying it kept costs in check even as others struggled . Similarly, in 2020's COVID-impacted Q2, Badger quickly adjusted production and reduced expenses to maintain profitability (net margin held around 10-12%). Then as demand rebounded, it ramped up efficiently.

**Fixed vs Variable Cost Mix:** Badger has a healthy gross margin, indicating it adds significant value over raw cost. Variable costs include direct materials (which move roughly in proportion to volume) and some portion of labor (e.g. overtime or temp labor for production spikes). Fixed costs include depreciation of equipment, R&D (which Badger maintains regardless of short-term sales), and baseline headcount. Based on margin behavior, Badger's cost structure allows it to **expand margins when sales grow** - a sign that fixed costs are being leveraged. For instance, operating margin expanded from \~15% a few years ago to 19% in 2024 , due to sales growth outpacing SG&A increases. This suggests **high incremental profit on additional sales**, after covering fixed costs. Conversely, if sales stagnated, some fixed costs (like R&D spending) would still remain, potentially compressing margins a bit. However, given the mission-critical nature of R&D and customer support, Badger is unlikely to slash those even in a slow year; they historically maintain investment through cycles.

In summary, **materials and component costs are the largest chunk of Badger's cost pie**, followed by personnel (engineering, sales, admin). The company's fixed costs (factories, R&D) are relatively modest relative to revenue and have been well managed, enabling margin expansion as revenue grew. In different economic conditions, Badger has shown it can be resilient: in strong markets, it scales up efficiently (leveraging fixed costs), and in weak markets, it has flexibility (pass-through pricing, and a variable portion of cost structure) to protect profitability . Going forward, as Badger's revenue mix tilts more to software, the gross margin could further improve (software has low variable cost). That said, reliance on electronic components means it will always watch commodity and parts prices closely. The **most significant cost risk is a sharp jump in input costs** (metals/chips) that lags pricing adjustments, but even that tends to be temporary as Badger and the industry adjust prices accordingly. Overall, Badger Meter's cost structure is well-controlled and has been trending favorably, contributing to its consistent earnings growth.

## **DCF Valuation Analysis**

To estimate Badger Meter's intrinsic value, we conducted a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis** under three scenarios - conservative, nominal (base case), and optimistic - reflecting different free cash flow growth assumptions. The goal is to derive an intrinsic value per share and a corresponding **buy price with a 25% margin of safety** in each case.

**DCF Inputs & Assumptions:**

-   We use **Free Cash Flow (FCF)** as a proxy for "owner's earnings." Badger's FCF in 2024 was about **$142 million** . For the trailing twelve months through Q3 2025, FCF is on the order of \~$160M (est.), reflecting growth in cash flows.

-   **Discount rate (r)**: We assume 10% as a reasonable required rate of return (Buffett often uses around 9-10% for equity). This accounts for the opportunity cost and a bit of risk premium for a small-cap.

-   **Terminal growth rate**: 3% per year (roughly equal to long-term GDP/inflation), reflecting modest perpetual growth after our explicit forecast period.

-   **Explicit forecast horizon**: 10 years. We project FCF for the next 10 years under each scenario, then calculate a terminal value at year 10.

The three scenarios differ in the **FCF growth rate** assumed:

1.  **Conservative Case - Lowest historical growth**: Badger's lowest FCF growth in the past decade was actually negative (e.g. -24% in 2017, or -5% in 2022). To be sensible, we won't assume permanent decline; instead, we'll use **+5% annual FCF growth**, which is very conservative given secular tailwinds (and far below its 10-year FCF CAGR of \~27%). 5% is around the lower bound of what one might expect even if conditions soften (e.g. it's roughly the growth of water utility replacement demand alone).

2.  **Nominal/Base Case - Average growth**: The average FCF growth (arithmetic mean) has been high (\~18% if excluding outlier, or \~36% including the rebound year). We'll use **15% annual FCF growth** for the next few years tapering to about 8% by year 10, which roughly averages out to \~12% over the decade. However, to simplify, we can approximate an equivalent constant growth that yields similar value. For practicality, let's assume **an effective 15% per year for the first half, then taper**. (Our DCF calculation used \~15% constant for illustration, but actual intrinsic will be in that ballpark). This represents Badger continuing to execute well, achieving high-single to low-double digit revenue growth and some margin expansion - a reasonable base-case.

3.  **Optimistic Case - Best historical growth**: Badger's best FCF growth year was extremely high (FCF +187% in 2016 due to a low base). Excluding anomalies, it had +45% in 2024. We'll use **25% annual FCF growth** for a few years then taper to \~10% by year 10 (which might average \~15-20%). To simplify, assume **25% sustained for the projection**, recognizing that's quite bullish. This scenario implies Badger captures significant new markets (smart water globally), expands margins, and sustains a very rapid growth trajectory akin to its recent momentum for a longer period.

Using these assumptions:

-   **Conservative DCF:** FCF grows at 5% for 10 years. In year 10, we compute terminal value = FCF\_year10 \* (1+3%) / (10%-3%). We discount all cash flows at 10%.

-   **Nominal DCF:** FCF grows \~15% (which is front-loaded and might effectively average lower). We did a straight-line 15% in our calc, which is somewhat aggressive but not far off early years growth potential.

-   **Optimistic DCF:** FCF grows at 25% for 10 years (this yields a massive increase, probably overshooting realistic limits because it'd imply nearly 10x FCF in a decade).

**Step-by-Step (Conservative Case):**

-   Starting FCF = $142M (2024 actual).

-   Year 1 FCF = 142 \* 1.05 = $149.1M.

-   Year 2 = $156.6M.

-   …

-   Year 10 = $142M \* (1.05^10) ≈ $231.7M.

-   Terminal Year 11 FCF = $231.7M \* 1.03 = $238.7M.

-   Terminal value at end of Year 10 = $238.7M / (0.10 - 0.03) = $3,410M.

-   Present value of terminal = $3,410M / (1.10^10) = $1,315M.

-   Present value of Year 1-10 cash flows (summing each discounted FCF): approximately $1,106M.

-   Total DCF value = $1,106M + $1,315M = **$2,421M** (i.e. about $2.42 billion).

-   Divide by shares (29.5M) = \*\*$82.1 per share intrinsic value (Conservative)\*\*.

-   Margin of Safety 25% =&gt; target buy price = $82.1 \* 0.75 = **\~$61.5 per share**.

So in the conservative scenario, intrinsic value comes out around **$82**, indicating the stock (at \~$177) is well above this prudent value. One would want to pay no more than \~$61 to have a 25% MOS. This implies Badger is significantly overvalued if it only grows FCF \~5% long-term. Notably, $82 is roughly where the stock traded in 2018-2019, when growth was slower.

**Step-by-Step (Nominal/Base Case):**

-   Starting FCF $142M. Growing \~15%:

-   Year 1: $163M; Year 2: $187M; Year 5: \~$273M; Year 10: \~$576M (15% growth compounding, which is aggressive because that's a 4x increase in a decade).

-   Terminal FCF = $576M \*1.03 = $593M; Terminal value = $593/(0.10-0.03)= $8,471M PV in year 10.

-   PV of terminal = $8,471/(1.1^10)= \~$3,271M.

-   PV of years 1-10 FCF = \~$1,816M.

-   Total value = \~$5,087M.

-   Per share = $5,087/29.5 = **\~$172.4 per share intrinsic (Nominal)**.

-   25% MOS buy price = $172.4 \* 0.75 = **\~$129.3**.

In the base case, intrinsic value ($172) is actually very close to the current market price ($177). This suggests the market is pricing in something like mid-teens growth for a while. A 25% discount from $172 gives \~$129 - the stock has rarely fallen to that level in recent times (it was last around $130 in mid-2022). So one might wait for a pullback to \~$130 or below to consider it a margin-of-safety buy under reasonable growth assumptions.

**Step-by-Step (Optimistic Case):**

A buy price with MOS here is \~$268 - interestingly, that's above the current price. **Margin of Safety Consideration:** For a margin of safety, ideally one would want the stock significantly below intrinsic. Only if one is extremely confident in the optimistic scenario would the current price appear cheap. Given uncertainties (competition, macro, etc.), demanding a 25% MOS is prudent. That would mean waiting for the stock to drop into the low-to-mid $130s (base case) or even as low as \~$60 (if one is very conservative). **Conclusion of DCF:**

-   **Optimistic Case:** *Intrinsic ≈ $358; Buy ≈ $269.* Assumes extraordinary growth \~20%+ for a decade (somewhat of a best-case).
