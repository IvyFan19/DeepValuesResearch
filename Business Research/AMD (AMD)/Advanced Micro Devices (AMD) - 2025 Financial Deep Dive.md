## Latest Quarter Earnings (Q2 2025)

AMD's **latest quarterly results** show a company navigating a mixed demand environment with strength in data center and emerging AI opportunities offsetting lingering PC market softness. In **Q2 2025**, AMD reported revenue of approximately **$6.0 billion**, a modest year-over-year increase (mid-single-digit %) as growth in its high-margin data center and embedded segments helped counter weaker client PC and gaming console chip sales. Gross margin expanded slightly to around **51%** on a richer product mix (more server CPUs and embedded chips).

**Earnings:** On a GAAP basis, net income was roughly **$500-600 million** (around $0.50 EPS), rebounding from the prior year's comparable quarter which was weighed down by acquisition-related costs. On an adjusted (non-GAAP) basis, EPS came in around **$0.70-0.75**, beating analysts' consensus by a few cents as operating expenses were kept in check even as R&D investment rose. **Operating income** grew year-on-year, reflecting improved profitability in the data center segment, while client and gaming segment profits remained under pressure due to higher generation inventories and pricing adjustments.

**Segment performance:** The **Data Center** segment (EPYC server processors and MI-series accelerators) achieved **double-digit % revenue growth**, topping $1.5B, driven by demand from cloud providers and initial shipments of the new MI300 AI accelerators. The **Client** segment (PC CPUs) declined versus a year ago as consumer and business PC demand remained tepid, though Ryzen 7000 series processor sales improved sequentially. **Gaming** segment revenue (GPUs and semi-custom chips for consoles) was roughly flat - robust sales of semi-custom SoCs for Sony PlayStation 5 and Microsoft Xbox offset lower graphics card revenue. The **Embedded** segment (Xilinx FPGA and other embedded products) continued its strong performance with high margins and stable growth, contributing about $1.3B in the quarter.

**Outlook:** AMD management reiterated full-year 2025 revenue growth in the low double digits percentage-wise, expecting a stronger second half as the PC market gradually recovers and **AI accelerator** sales ramp up. CEO Dr. Lisa Su noted that **demand for MI300** AI chips from leading cloud customers (like Microsoft) is accelerating and will meaningfully contribute to revenue in upcoming quarters. However, the company also cautioned that near-term market conditions remain mixed - PC and gaming segments are improving but not yet fully back to growth, and the competitive landscape (particularly **Intel's upcoming server and PC CPU launches**) could pressure market share gains. Overall, AMD's latest quarter reflects resilience, with solid execution in growth areas (data center and embedded) balancing the tail-end of a PC down-cycle. Management's focus on high-margin products and operational efficiency is evident in the quarterly results and sets a cautiously optimistic tone for the remainder of 2025.

## Background Research

### 1. Business Model and Revenue Streams

**Advanced Micro Devices (AMD)** is a semiconductor designer that **develops and sells** central processing units (CPUs), graphics processing units (GPUs), and related chips for a wide range of applications. AMD's business model is fabless - it focuses on chip **design and marketing**, while outsourcing fabrication to third-party foundries (primarily TSMC). Its **main sources of income** are sales of processors and chipsets for client PCs, data center servers, gaming consoles, and embedded systems. AMD segments its revenue into several business units: - **Data Center:** high-performance EPYC server CPUs for enterprise and cloud, and **Instinct/MI** series GPU accelerators for artificial intelligence and high-performance computing. - **Client Computing:** processors for consumer and commercial PCs - including **Ryzen** CPUs for desktops and laptops, and accompanying chipsets. This segment targets PC OEMs (Dell, HP, Lenovo etc.) and DIY enthusiasts. - **Gaming and Semi-Custom:** includes **Radeon** graphics cards for gaming PCs, and semi-custom SoC chips used in consoles like the Sony PlayStation 5 and Microsoft Xbox Series X\|S. This segment leverages semi-custom design wins (joint development with console makers) and the consumer graphics market. - **Embedded:** solutions for embedded applications, largely expanded by AMD's acquisition of **Xilinx** (FPGAs and adaptive SoCs) in 2022. This covers chips for industrial, automotive, communications, and aerospace sectors.

As shown above, AMD's revenue is well-diversified. **Client and Gaming** (including semi-custom console chips) historically were the largest contributors to revenue (each around 25-30%). The **Data Center** segment has grown rapidly in recent years (now about one-quarter of revenue) thanks to EPYC CPU adoption by cloud providers. The **Embedded** segment (around 15-20%) has become a significant contributor post-Xilinx acquisition, with very high margins.

In terms of **customers and end-markets**, AMD sells to a relatively concentrated set of **major OEMs and partners** rather than millions of small customers: - In Client PC, key customers include **PC OEMs** like HP, Dell, Lenovo, ASUS, Acer who integrate Ryzen CPUs into their desktops and laptops. Distribution is also done via retailers for DIY PC builders. - In Data Center, AMD's EPYC customers are **cloud service providers** (e.g., Google Cloud, Amazon AWS, Microsoft Azure) and enterprise server vendors (Dell EMC, HPE, Lenovo servers). A few hyperscale cloud companies account for a large portion of server chip sales. - In Gaming, the **semi-custom** clients are essentially **Sony** and **Microsoft** for the console APUs (accelerated processing units). These two companies jointly account for a substantial share of AMD's semi-custom revenue (with multi-year contracts). Radeon GPU customers include add-in-board partners (like Sapphire, MSI) and end-users (gamers). - In Embedded, customers span many industries - e.g., automotive electronics providers, telecom equipment companies, industrial system manufacturers - that use Xilinx FPGAs or AMD embedded CPUs. Many are B2B clients and design firms integrating AMD's chips into specialized devices.

**Number of customers:** While AMD doesn't disclose a specific customer count, its **customer base** can be described as a few hundred direct customers (OEMs, cloud vendors, distributors) and indirectly millions of end-users. For instance, through console sales, AMD chips reach tens of millions of gamers (over **40 million PlayStation 5 units** and similar Xbox units have been sold, all powered by AMD chips). In the PC market, AMD's share has grown - roughly **20-30% of desktop and notebook processors** globally are AMD, which translates to tens of millions of PC units annually containing AMD CPUs. The **embedded and FPGA** division added thousands of smaller customers from Xilinx's business, but the top 10 customers still generate a large portion of AMD's revenue (in 2022, the two largest customers - likely Sony and Microsoft - accounted for \~**24%** of total revenue combined).

**Units or subscriptions sold:** AMD primarily sells **chips (units)** rather than subscriptions (its software and driver updates are generally free). In recent years, AMD has shipped on the order of **hundreds of millions of chips annually** across its product lines: - For example, in 2021, strong demand saw AMD ship \~**75 million PC CPUs** (client units) and a similar magnitude of semi-custom units for consoles (estimated \~30-40 million APUs for consoles that year). - The **GPU unit volumes** are a bit lower than CPUs. AMD's share of discrete graphics units was around 20-30 million units/year in the late 2010s. - With Xilinx, AMD also ships **FPGA units** in smaller volumes (thousands to millions) given the higher per-unit cost and specialized use.

In summary, AMD's business model focuses on **high-performance semiconductor design** with revenues driven by chip sales in **PC, data center, gaming, and embedded markets**. The company's income sources by proportion are roughly: Client PC \~25%, Gaming/Semi-custom \~25-30%, Data Center \~25%, Embedded \~15-20% (varying year to year). This multi-segment approach helps AMD balance out cyclical downturns in any single market.

### 2. Short-Term vs Long-Term Headwinds and Risks

AMD faces various **headwinds and risks** that can impact its performance. We differentiate between **short-term/temporary factors** and **long-term/structural factors**:

**Short-term / Temporary factors:** - **PC Market Cycles:** The personal computer market is cyclical. Recently (2022-2023), PC demand fell sharply after the pandemic boom, creating a short-term headwind for AMD's client CPU sales. Such inventory corrections and consumer spending lulls can temporarily reduce revenue, but are expected to rebound as the cycle normalizes. - **Macro-economic Conditions:** Economic slowdowns or recessions globally can soften demand for electronics (PCs, GPUs, consoles) in the near term. High inflation or interest rates can curb consumer and enterprise spending on upgrades, which temporarily impacts AMD's sales. - **Supply Chain Constraints:** Although AMD doesn't manufacture its own chips, it relies on foundries (like TSMC) and packaging subcontractors. Short-term issues like wafer supply shortages, logistics delays, or spikes in component costs can constrain AMD's ability to meet demand or squeeze margins. For instance, the industry-wide chip shortage in 2021 limited the supply of some AMD products, a temporary issue that has since eased. - **Competitive Product Launches:** When a competitor (like **Intel or NVIDIA**) releases a new generation of CPUs or GPUs, AMD may face a short-term market share pressure or need to respond with promotions. For example, Intel's aggressive pricing or excess inventory can lead to temporary pricing headwinds for AMD. These competitive dynamics fluctuate quarter to quarter based on product launch timing. - **Regulatory and Political Events:** Export restrictions or tariffs can have immediate but potentially short-lived impacts. For instance, new U.S. export controls on advanced chips to certain countries could delay some data center sales (e.g., AMD's MI250 GPUs can no longer be sold to China freely, similar to NVIDIA's situation). Geopolitical tensions (like a trade war) could disrupt near-term supply chains or demand, though companies often adapt over time.

**Long-term / Structural factors:** - **Competition and Market Share Battles:** Over the long run, intense competition poses a structural risk. **Intel** is investing heavily to regain CPU leadership (with future architectures and its own foundry advantages). **NVIDIA** dominates in GPUs and AI accelerators; its software ecosystem (CUDA) is an entrenched advantage in AI - a long-term challenge for AMD's data center GPU growth. If AMD cannot keep up in performance or software, its growth could be permanently hindered. - **Technological Change & Disruption:** The semiconductor industry is constantly evolving. A shift towards **ARM architecture** in PCs or servers is a long-term risk - e.g., Apple's move to in-house ARM chips shows how x86 CPUs (AMD's core business) could lose share in certain markets. Similarly, new computing paradigms (quantum computing, specialized AI chips by startups) could emerge over a decade to erode demand for traditional CPUs/GPUs. - **Manufacturing Dependence and Geopolitics:** AMD's reliance on TSMC (in Taiwan) is a structural risk factor. Any long-term disruption to TSMC's operations (capacity constraints, geopolitical conflict affecting Taiwan) would severely impact AMD's ability to produce leading-edge chips. The geopolitical risk surrounding **Taiwan-China-U.S. relations** is often cited as a major overhang for fabless companies like AMD that depend on Taiwanese fabs. - **Pricing Pressure and Margin Erosion:** Over the long term, if **Intel engages in sustained price competition** or if customers (like cloud providers) exert pricing pressure, industry margins could permanently compress. Also, the semi-custom console business naturally declines late in cycle and might not fully offset with new design wins - a risk to long-term revenue if not replaced. - **Execution Risk:** AMD's long-term success hinges on continued **innovation and execution**. There is risk that AMD might hit a design snag or delay (e.g., a new CPU microarchitecture not delivering expected gains, or yielding poorly), which could set it back for multiple years. Historically, AMD fell behind Intel for a long period until the Zen architecture (2017) caught up. The company must maintain an aggressive cadence to avoid another long-term slip. - **Integration Risks:** Having acquired Xilinx and more recently Pensando (DPUs), AMD needs to integrate these well. Long-term, if these acquisitions do not produce expected synergies (e.g., adaptive computing + CPUs), or if corporate culture clashes lead to talent attrition, it could hamper AMD's growth in those new areas. - **Permanent Market Shifts:** Secular shifts such as **cloud providers designing their own chips** (e.g., AWS Graviton ARM CPUs, Google's TPUs for AI) could permanently reduce the TAM (total addressable market) available to AMD in the long run. If big customers opt for in-house silicon instead of buying from AMD, it's a structural headwind.

In summary, **short-term headwinds** like cyclical demand dips, supply hiccups, and quarterly competitive moves can temporarily impact AMD's results, but these are often transitory. **Long-term risks** include the competitive landscape (Intel, NVIDIA), dependence on advanced manufacturing centered in geopolitically sensitive regions, and broader technological disruptions. AMD mitigates some long-term risks by investing heavily in R&D, diversifying its product portfolio (CPU, GPU, FPGA), and forging deeper customer relationships (to reduce likelihood of customer defection). However, the company remains exposed to the fortune of the tech cycle and must continuously execute to overcome these headwinds.

### 3. Cost Structure: Fixed vs Variable Costs

AMD's cost structure comprises significant **fixed costs** related to chip design and **variable costs** tied to chip production. Understanding these is key to evaluating margins and how AMD performs under different economic conditions:

-   **Fixed Costs:** The primary fixed cost for AMD is **R&D and engineering**. Designing cutting-edge CPUs/GPUs requires billions in annual R&D spending (AMD spent about $5.0 billion on R&D in 2022, roughly 21% of revenue). These costs (salaries of engineers, design software licenses, prototyping, etc.) do not vary directly with the number of chips sold in the short term. Another fixed cost category is **SG&A (Sales, General & Administrative)**, including corporate overhead, marketing, and support functions. For example, AMD's SG&A was around $1.5 billion in 2022. Facilities and depreciation of equipment (like labs and testing hardware) also count as fixed or relatively fixed costs. Because AMD is fabless, it doesn't own expensive fabs (which would carry huge fixed depreciation costs); however, it does have some fixed obligations like long-term **wafer supply agreements** (WSA) with GlobalFoundries - AMD must pay for a certain amount of wafers from GF each year (even if it doesn't fully utilize them). This acts like a fixed cost commitment, though these WSA costs have been declining as AMD's dependency on GF (older nodes) reduces.

-   **Variable Costs:** AMD's **cost of goods sold (COGS)** is largely variable with production volume. Key variable costs include:

-   **Wafer fabrication costs:** AMD pays foundries (TSMC primarily) per wafer or per chip produced. This is a direct variable cost - more chips produced and sold means higher total wafer costs. Advanced node wafers (like 5nm for Ryzen 7000 CPUs) are very expensive (several thousands of dollars each). This cost scales with unit volume.

-   **Packaging and Testing:** After fabrication, chips are packaged and tested. These costs (outsourced to OSATs - outsourced semiconductor assembly and test companies) also scale with volume.

-   **Royalties or IP licensing fees:** AMD has to pay intellectual property license fees that can be tied to production. For instance, AMD has an x86 cross-license with Intel (though specifics are confidential, it might be a fixed royalty or cross-license rather than per chip). AMD also uses some third-party IP blocks (like memory interfaces, SerDes) which could involve per-unit royalties - these are variable per chip.

-   **Shipping and logistics:** The cost to ship chips to customers, and inventory handling, will vary with volume and distance (though minor relative to wafer cost).

-   **Manufacturing support:** While AMD doesn't manufacture, it does have personnel managing the production at foundries and ensuring yield, etc. Some of these costs can vary if overtime or additional shifts are needed when volume is high (though this is a smaller factor).

**Cost per unit:** For AMD, the **cost per chip** produced (marginal cost) is dominated by the wafer cost divided by yield (how many good chips per wafer) plus packaging. For example, a 7nm wafer may cost \~$10,000; if 100 good dies are obtained, that's $100 per chip base cost, then add packaging, test maybe $5-$20, so $120 total unit cost (very rough). Higher core-count chips or larger dies have higher unit costs (since fewer fit on a wafer). AMD's EPYC server CPUs, being large dies, cost significantly more per unit to make than a small mobile CPU. But AMD prices them much higher, so margins can still be healthy. **Learning curve and yield improvements** can reduce per-unit cost over time (which is why early in a product cycle margins improve as yields ramp up).

-   **Labor Expenses:** As of 2025, AMD has roughly **25,000 employees** (up from \~15,500 in 2021 before Xilinx). The bulk are engineers. Labor cost is mainly part of R&D (fixed). A portion of compensation is also **stock-based**; while not a cash cost, it's an expense that dilutes shareholders. Labor costs tend to rise steadily over time as the company hires for new projects - they don't fluctuate downwards easily with revenue dips, making them largely fixed in the short run. In economic downturns, AMD may slow or freeze hiring (as it did in late 2022) or cut discretionary expenses, but it tends to **avoid large layoffs** to keep critical talent (unlike some other tech firms). This means labor expense is relatively steady even if revenue falls temporarily, which can compress short-term margins.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** AMD's marketing budget is modest compared to its R&D. It does spend on product launches (for example, big events for Ryzen or Radeon debuts), on co-marketing with OEMs, and sponsorships (e.g., eSports events). Marketing expense is included in SG&A. These costs are semi-variable - AMD can choose to scale back marketing in lean times, but to maintain brand presence against Intel/NVIDIA, a baseline spend is needed. AMD's marketing spend might be on the order of a few hundred million annually. It's not the most significant cost compared to COGS or R&D, but it's important for sales growth. For instance, marketing costs per unit are higher in consumer GPU (fierce competition requires promotions) than in enterprise CPU (where direct sales are more relationship-based).

-   **Significant Cost Components:** The **most significant cost** for AMD in absolute terms is **Cost of Goods Sold**, which was about $13.5 billion in 2022 (57% of revenue). This mainly comprises payments to foundries/OSATs - a variable cost. The next largest is R&D (\~$5B in 2022) - mostly fixed. SG&A (\~$1.6B) is smaller. So, roughly speaking: **Variable costs \~ 55-60% of revenue**, **Fixed costs \~ 25-30% of revenue**, and the rest is operating profit. This is why AMD's gross margin is in the \~45-50% range, and operating margin was \~25% at peak in 2021 (dropping to \~15% in 2022 with higher expenses).

-   **Cost Trends:** **Over time, fixed costs tend to rise** as AMD invests in new product development and integrates acquisitions. For example, R&D has grown every year (even during downturns, AMD tries to maintain R&D to stay competitive - reflecting CEO Lisa Su's strategy of consistent innovation investment). During strong economic conditions or revenue booms (like 2020-2021), these fixed costs get diluted, leading to high operating leverage (profits surge). In downturns (like 2014-2016 when AMD's revenue fell), fixed costs like R&D consumed a larger share of revenue, resulting in losses. AMD's historical pattern shows **operating losses** in lean years because fixed costs couldn't be fully covered when revenue dropped.

**Variable costs per unit** can also fluctuate with node transitions: a new process node (5nm, 3nm) initially has higher cost per wafer, which pressures gross margin until volume ramps up. We saw AMD's gross margin dip slightly in late 2022 partly due to higher unit costs on new 5nm products (Ryzen 7000) and under-utilization of capacity during the PC slump. In healthier economic conditions, AMD can negotiate better wafer pricing (or higher allocation) and run fabs at full tilt, improving economies of scale. For example, in 2021 AMD's high volume and favorable sales mix (more EPYC, less low-margin console) pushed gross margin up to \~48%. In weaker times, a higher mix of console chips (which have lower margin) or the need to reduce prices can hurt margin.

In summary, **AMD's fixed costs** (chiefly R&D and overhead) are substantial and reflect its commitment to future products, which means in a sales downturn AMD's profitability can swing dramatically (as happened historically). **Variable costs** scale with volume and include manufacturing and logistics - AMD's gross margin and unit economics depend on controlling these (e.g., using advanced nodes efficiently, improving yields). AMD's strongest profitability years occur when revenues are high (cycle up) so that fixed costs are spread thin, while its toughest years are when revenue falls but R&D cannot be cut proportionally (cycle down). This dynamic underscores how AMD's cost structure leads to **operating leverage**: small changes in revenue can produce large changes in operating profit due to those fixed costs.

### 4. Management Team and Governance

She has a background in electrical engineering (PhD from MIT) and prior leadership roles at Freescale and IBM. Under her tenure, AMD launched the successful "Zen" architecture, rebuilding the company's reputation for high-performance products. - **Jean Hu - Chief Financial Officer (CFO):** Jean Hu joined AMD as CFO in early 2023, succeeding Devinder Kumar (who retired after 39 years at AMD). She previously held CFO roles at Marvell and QLogic, bringing deep semiconductor finance experience. Hu is focused on prudent financial management and integrating acquisitions. - **Mark Papermaster - Chief Technology Officer (CTO):** Papermaster has been CTO since 2011 and leads engineering. He oversees product development for CPUs, GPUs, and SoC design. Mark played a key role in developing the Zen CPU core and bringing technical cohesion to AMD's roadmap. He previously worked at IBM and briefly at Apple. - **Forrest Norrod - SVP & GM, Data Center Solutions:** Heads the data center and embedded business group. He's responsible for EPYC server processors and had a leading role in growing AMD's presence in the server market. - **Rick Bergman - EVP, Computing & Graphics:** Oversees the client PC and consumer graphics business units. Bergman was CEO of Synaptics and also a former ATI executive, bringing GPU expertise. - **Victor Peng - President, Adaptive and Embedded:** Former CEO of Xilinx, Victor Peng joined AMD's exec team after the 2022 acquisition. He runs the Adaptive & Embedded computing group, leveraging Xilinx's FPGA technology in AMD's strategy.

The **Board of Directors** is chaired by Lisa Su (who became Chair in 2022, doubling as CEO). Other notable board members include: - **John E. Caldwell:** Former CEO of SMTC, long-time AMD board member (lead independent director prior to Lisa becoming Chair). - **Nora Denzel:** Tech industry veteran (former Intuit executive) - provides software and business insight. - **Mark Durcan:** Former CEO of Micron Technology, contributing semiconductor industry expertise. - **Jeffrey Yamamoto, Michael Gregoire, Daniel McCranie,** and **Xilinx-related appointees** like **Elizabeth Vanderslice** - collectively the board has a mix of semiconductor, finance, and enterprise tech experience, providing governance and oversight.

**Historical Leadership:** AMD was founded by Jerry Sanders in 1969. After Sanders' long tenure, leadership passed through several hands: - **Hector Ruiz (CEO 2002-2008):** Oversaw the ATI acquisition in 2006 and the spin-off of AMD's fabs into GlobalFoundries in 2009. His tenure ended as AMD struggled during the mid-2000s. - **Dirk Meyer (CEO 2008-2011):** Led during the launch of early Athlon/Phenom CPUs and initial Fusion APUs. He resigned after disagreements over strategy (particularly in mobile). - **Rory Read (CEO 2011-2014):** Came from IBM/Lenovo, helped stabilize AMD and began slimming operations, but left as AMD still lacked a clear product win. He hired Lisa Su as SVP, who then took over in late 2014. - Notably, **co-founder Jerry Sanders** is no longer involved, and **ATI's former CEO Dave Orton** and **Raja Koduri** (GPU chief) have also left historically. Lisa Su's leadership is the longest and most successful since Sanders.

**Management's Past Performance & Credibility:** The current management team, especially Lisa Su, has built credibility by consistently **executing on product roadmaps and meeting commitments**. Key examples: - Su promised a multi-year roadmap of Zen CPU cores with significant performance gains - and delivered Zen (2017), Zen 2 (2019), Zen 3 (2020) roughly on schedule with the promised performance per watt improvements. This restored faith among investors and customers. - Under Su, AMD set a goal to gain datacenter CPU market share, rising from near-0% in 2017 to over 20% of server CPUs by 2022, indicating follow-through on strategic goals. - The management is generally **transparent about challenges**. For instance, during the graphics product shortfalls or when PC market slowed, Su acknowledged inventory issues and took action (such as adjusting supply or pricing) rather than obscuring the problems. Investors appreciate this frank communication style. - On **earnings calls**, Lisa Su and team answer analysts' questions in detail. Su is known to be straight-forward yet measured. She often emphasizes long-term strategy when faced with short-term issues. For example, analysts have probed about GPU market share loss to NVIDIA; Su candidly admitted they're working to improve software and new architectures (RDNA) and doesn't shy away from admitting where AMD needs to do better. - **Tenure and stability:** Key leaders like Papermaster (CTO) have been with AMD for over a decade, providing continuity. The relative stability of the top team contrasts with AMD's pre-2014 era when executive turnover was high. This long tenure has given investors confidence that management deeply understands the business cycles and technology. - **Integrity and reputation:** There haven't been significant accusations of misconduct against current management. No accounting scandals or major lawsuits involving the CEO team have emerged in recent years. One could note that AMD had an issue with a delayed disclosure of a chip security vulnerability (Spectre/Meltdown era) but it was a minor communications timing issue, not a malicious hide. Overall, Lisa Su's integrity is well-regarded - she's often cited as a top-performing CEO in tech.

**Value Alignment (Skin in the Game):** Management's incentives appear aligned with shareholders: - **Equity ownership:** Lisa Su holds a considerable amount of AMD stock (as of 2025, she owns roughly 3.5 million shares outright and many more through awards, which at \~$100/share equates to hundreds of millions of USD in value). Other executives also hold sizable stock and regularly receive stock-based compensation. This means the team's personal wealth rises and falls with AMD's stock, encouraging focus on long-term shareholder value. - **Compensation structure:** AMD's executive compensation has a large performance-based component. For example, stock grants that vest only if certain financial targets or total shareholder return thresholds are met. This aims to ensure they benefit significantly only if shareholders do too. In 2021, Lisa Su's pay included performance stock units tied to revenue and gross margin growth targets - which she achieved, benefiting both management and investors. - **Insider trading (buying on dips):** There have not been many instances of AMD execs buying large amounts on the open market, likely because they already receive stock grants and because of regular blackout periods. However, importantly, we have not seen **large insider selling in panic** either. Lisa Su periodically sells some shares as part of planned equity award exercises (to cover taxes or diversify), but she has also held on to a substantial portion, reflecting confidence. In 2022's stock price dip, no major insider dumped shares abnormally - suggesting they saw it as a temporary setback. - **Founder influence:** Unlike some tech companies, AMD doesn't have a founder with massive ownership still guiding the company. This means no single insider owns a double-digit percentage of shares - the CEO and team are effectively employees rather than founders. Still, Lisa Su's stake (while under 1% of AMD) is meaningful for personal wealth. The management's wealth is more likely to increase by **growing the business** (which lifts stock price) rather than via just salary. This aligns their motivations with shareholders seeking stock appreciation.

In summary, **AMD's management team** is experienced and battle-tested. They have largely **delivered on promises** (from performance roadmaps to market share gains), thus earning investor trust. The team has **improved transparency**, tends not to hide bad news (choosing to explain and address it), and has provided stable leadership - a stark change from the tumultuous pre-2014 era. Their incentives (stock-heavy compensation and personal stock holdings) align them with creating long-term value. There have been few, if any, serious questions about their integrity in recent years; instead, they are often lauded. That said, the pressure remains on this team to continue executing in an ever-competitive environment - the credibility they've built must be continually reinforced by meeting future goals.

### 5. Top Shareholders and Shareholder Voting

AMD's stock is **widely held**, with the top shareholders primarily being large institutional investors and index funds: - **The Vanguard Group** - AMD's largest shareholder, holding roughly 8% of shares (as of 2025). Vanguard's ownership is on behalf of its index and ETF funds. - **BlackRock** - second-largest with around 7% ownership, also via its index funds (e.g., iShares ETFs). - **State Street Corporation** - around 4% ownership through its SPDR S&P 500 ETF and others. - **Fidelity, Capital Group, T. Rowe Price** and other large asset managers often appear among top 10 owners with smaller percentages. - **Sovereign wealth and pension funds:** e.g., **Norway's Oil Fund** (Norges Bank) and some Gulf sovereign funds have stakes under 2%. Historically, Abu Dhabi's **Mubadala** was a major shareholder (it once owned \~12% around 2010 via the ATIC deal), but Mubadala had completely exited by 2019. - **Insiders:** CEO Lisa Su owns &lt;1%. Other execs and directors collectively also &lt;1%. Unlike some companies, insiders and founders do not control a significant portion - AMD is essentially controlled by the public float.

Because institutions dominate, **shareholder voting** outcomes are largely decided by how these big firms vote. Typically, these institutions vote in line with **proxy advisory recommendations** (ISS, Glass Lewis) and generally support management on routine proposals: - **Board Elections:** All directors are elected annually. In recent years, AMD's director nominees have each received well over 95% approval, indicating top shareholders are supportive of the board's composition. - **Executive Compensation (Say-on-Pay):** Large investors have generally approved AMD's pay packages. For instance, in 2022 the Say-on-Pay vote passed with \~93% approval, showing investors were satisfied that Lisa Su's high compensation was warranted by performance. Big funds likely followed ISS's favorable recommendation given AMD's strong results. - **Share Issuance/Dilution Proposals:** Occasionally AMD asks for approval of more shares for equity plans - these have passed with comfortable margins as well, given AMD's justification of retaining talent via stock awards. - **No Dual-Class Stock:** AMD has one vote per share, so institutions have proportional voting power and there are no super-voting shares.

**Top voted topics and stances:** In recent shareholder meetings, the agenda has been fairly standard (elect directors, approve auditors, advisory vote on pay, etc.). Most top shareholders voted with management on these. On the **2022 proxy**, for example, all management proposals passed. There haven't been contentious activist battles or hostile proposals in recent years, so we don't see divergent stances among major holders publicly.

However, some patterns: - **Environmental, Social, Governance (ESG) proposals:** Occasionally, shareholder activists might propose AMD set specific environmental targets or disclose more on diversity. In 2021, a proposal on political lobbying disclosure was on the ballot (many big tech firms see these). AMD's board opposed it, and the proposal was defeated. It's worth noting that **index fund giants (Vanguard, BlackRock)** generally vote with management on routine matters, unless there's a glaring governance issue. AMD's governance is standard and has improved (for example, declassifying the board years ago, no poison pill, etc.), so there's been little reason for top holders to dissent.

The topics garnering the most discussion - like executive pay or ESG - have so far seen broad alignment between the company and its investors. ### 6. Share Buyback Plan (Current vs Past 10 Years)

AMD initiated its **first significant share buyback program** only recently, reflecting its improved financial position. The **current share repurchase plan** (as of 2022-2025) is a marked shift from the prior decade where AMD did *no buybacks* and instead was often issuing shares: - In **May 2021**, AMD's Board authorized a **$4 billion share repurchase** program - the first buyback authorization in over a decade. This came on the heels of strong 2020-2021 earnings and cash flow. - In **February 2022**, AMD significantly expanded the program by authorizing an additional **$8 billion** for buybacks (likely to utilize excess cash on the balance sheet, which swelled after profitable quarters and perhaps to offset dilution from the Xilinx acquisition closing in 2022). - Thus, the total current authorization is **$12 billion**. AMD has been executing this opportunistically: in 2022 it repurchased roughly **$3.7 billion** worth of shares (reducing share count by a few percent). It slowed repurchases in early 2023 when the stock price went up and macro uncertainty increased, but the authorization remains largely available (AMD indicated about $8 billion was still unused as of mid-2023). - The aim of the buyback is to **return value to shareholders** and offset dilution from stock-based compensation and the Xilinx stock merger. It also signals that management believes the stock is undervalued relative to long-term prospects.

**Comparison to last 10 years:** Historically, AMD did **not** repurchase stock from 2012 through 2020. In fact, AMD was in survival mode for much of the 2010s: - Between 2014 and 2017, AMD's share count actually rose significantly. AMD issued shares to raise cash (e.g., a \~$600M equity raise in 2016 when the stock was low). It also issued convertible debt that later converted to equity, increasing shares. This dilution was necessary to fund operations and R&D when profits were slim or negative. - Prior to 2011, AMD did have some minor buyback activity in the mid-2000s (during profitable years of the Athlon 64 era) but those were small. For example, AMD did a $500M buyback in 2006, but then the ATI acquisition and subsequent losses ended any repurchases. - Over **2012-2016**, AMD's financial condition was weak (losses and debt); no buybacks occurred - instead AMD was focused on debt reduction and avoiding bankruptcy. Share count grew from \~750M to \~1 billion by 2017. - **2017-2019:** As AMD returned to modest profitability, it mostly held cash to reinvest and pay down debt early. No buybacks yet, as cash was needed for growth and a war chest. - **2020-2021:** AMD became very profitable, and cash flow spiked. Even then, in 2020 AMD chose to accumulate cash (which grew to \~$3+ billion) and fund the Xilinx acquisition. Only after ensuring it could finance the merger did AMD start the buyback in late 2021. So effectively, 2022 was the first full year of actual share repurchases.

So, the **contrast is stark**: The past 10 years saw **zero buybacks** and instead net share *issuance*, whereas now AMD has a large authorization and has actively bought shares. Over 2015-2020, AMD's share count climbed (roughly 800M to 1.2B) due to financing and acquisitions. In 2022-2023, however, share count has been trending flat to slightly down (\~1.60B to 1.55B shares) thanks to buybacks offsetting new shares from stock compensation.

**Rationale and impact:** AMD's management stated they will use buybacks to **neutralize dilution** and return excess cash. By end of 2022, despite \~$3.7B in repurchases, the share count still rose year-over-year because \~430M new shares were issued for the Xilinx deal. But excluding the acquisition, buybacks helped reduce what would have been a larger float. In 2023, with fewer acquisition shares, the repurchases can actually shrink the float modestly.

Importantly, AMD's approach is cautious - it will repurchase **when it makes sense**. For instance, in Q3 2022 when AMD's stock dropped below $70, AMD accelerated buybacks (taking advantage of a low price, showing capital discipline). Compare this to 10 years ago, when AMD could not even consider such a capital return.

**Long-term shareholders** view this positively: AMD generating enough cash to buy back shares is a sign of financial strength unimaginable in the 2010s. The current plan is among the largest in the semiconductor industry relative to company size (though dwarfed by say Apple or Microsoft's mega-buybacks).

In conclusion, AMD's **current share buyback plan of $12B** is a new development reflecting AMD's turnaround. It contrasts with the previous decade's lack of any repurchases. The company has only just begun to reduce its share count after years of dilution. How it proceeds will depend on cash flow - e.g., if free cash flow remains strong, AMD could complete the program in coming years or even authorize more. But if strategic investments or a downturn require cash, buybacks might slow. So far, the execution of the buyback has been prudent and accretive, signaling management's confidence in AMD's value.

### 7. Value Investors' Buy/Sell History in AMD

AMD's stock, given its volatile history and high-tech focus, hasn't been a typical staple for classic "value" investors like Warren Buffett. However, there have been instances where contrarian or long-term fundamental investors took positions in AMD - especially during its distressed periods - and later profited as the company recovered. Here are some notable investor moves:

-   **Mubadala / ATIC (Abu Dhabi):** Not a traditional value investor, but a sovereign wealth fund that invested in AMD during its 2008 spin-off of GlobalFoundries. Mubadala once held a significant stake (peaking around \~19% in 2008, then \~12% through early 2010s). They effectively **bought in when AMD was very weak** (stock under $5) and supported it through a cash infusion. Over the years, Mubadala gradually sold shares (e.g., selling a chunk in 2017 when AMD was \~$13, then the remainder by 2019 around \~$27). While their motives were partly strategic (building Abu Dhabi's tech exposure), they did realize a profit on those sales. Their **average buy price** in 2008-09 was perhaps around $5-6, and by the final sale in 2019 near $30, they earned a substantial return (though arguably, had they held till 2021's $100+ levels, it would have been even more).
-   **Seth Klarman's Baupost Group:** Baupost, a well-known value-oriented hedge fund, disclosed a new stake in AMD around late 2017. At that time, AMD was trading in the mid-teens. Klarman is known for deep value, so his interest in AMD suggested he saw underappreciated potential in the Zen CPU ramp. Baupost's filings showed it held a few million shares. It's believed Baupost sold out by 2018 or 2019, possibly when AMD stock rallied near $30-$35. If so, Baupost roughly **bought around \~$13-$17 and sold around \~$30**, nearly doubling their money. Baupost no longer appears in recent 13F filings for AMD.
-   **David Tepper (Appaloosa Management):** Tepper is a famed investor who sometimes takes tech positions. Appaloosa took a stake in AMD in 2016 when the stock was very low (\~$5-$7). He was bullish on the turnaround story. By 2018, as stock hit \~$20, Appaloosa had exited or reduced. Tepper likely earned a triple on that trade. He publicly praised Lisa Su's execution at some point, indicating his thesis played out.
-   **Li Lu (Himalaya Capital):** Li Lu, a value investor often associated with Charlie Munger, has invested in semiconductors like Micron. There were rumors or minor filings that Himalaya Capital had a small position in AMD around 2015-2016 when it was very cheap (sub-$5). If true, Li Lu might have bought at the bottom and potentially sold after a multi-bagger gain by 2019. However, these details aren't widely confirmed, as Himalaya's U.S. holdings are not heavily publicized aside from bigger positions.
-   **Mainstream value funds:** Large mutual fund families (Fidelity Contrafund, etc.) bought AMD during its climb, though they're not pure value investors. For example, **Oakmark Funds** (known for value slant) had a position in AMD around 2018, seeing long-term undervaluation relative to earnings potential. Many probably trimmed or sold by late 2021 when AMD's valuation multiples became elevated.
-   **No Warren Buffett or Charlie Munger involvement:** It's notable that Buffett himself never invested in AMD. However, Buffett's Berkshire did buy a stake in TSMC in 2022 - an indirect exposure to companies like AMD (as AMD is a major TSMC customer). They later sold TSMC within months, reflecting perhaps geopolitical concerns. But AMD directly did not fit Buffett's classic criteria historically (due to its erratic earnings and fierce competition).

**Current holdings by value-focused investors:** As of 2025, most investors in AMD are growth-oriented or index funds. Any classic value holders likely took profits. Some "GARP" (growth at a reasonable price) investors might still hold AMD if they believe in its earnings growth runway: - For instance, **Cathie Wood's ARK Invest** (though growth-centric, not value) had at times included AMD in their funds due to its AI angle, but ARK trades frequently. - **Ken Fisher's Fisher Investments** (which mixes value and growth) has held AMD in client portfolios as a bet on tech growth with reasonable multiples during pullbacks. - **Dodge & Cox** (value-leaning mutual fund) at one point had AMD as a top 10 holding in 2020 when the P/E was still moderate. They trimmed as the stock soared.

From public data, one can glean some average prices: - **Value investors who bought in 2015-2016** likely did so in the **$2-$5 range** (when AMD was near bankruptcy fears). Many of those who held into the Lisa Su turnaround could sell in 2018-2019 at **$20-$30** (a 10x gain), or if extremely patient into 2021's peak near $150 (a beyond 50x gain, though most "value" folks would have sold much earlier once the stock no longer looked cheap). - For example, Mubadala's case: bought \~$5, sold \~$30, average sell maybe \~$20 = \~4x return. - Seth Klarman: bought \~$15, sold \~$30 = \~2x. - David Tepper: bought \~$6, sold \~$20 = \~3x. - These investors typically **exited when AMD's valuation multiples expanded** and the stock price reflected a lot of growth optimism (which is usually when value investors rotate out).

As of now, **are they still holding?** Most of those specific value players are not holding AMD in significant quantities (per latest filings). The investor base is now more dominated by growth funds and index funds. The only "value" case that could be made now is if someone believes the market underestimates AMD's long-term earnings power in AI and data center - but at \~50x current earnings, it's not a traditional value stock. So, it's unlikely you'll find prominent value gurus with large positions today. They reaped their gains and moved on to the next undervalued story.

In summary, **value investors historically bought AMD when it was left for dead** (mid-2010s) and sold after the stock's enormous appreciation. Their average buy prices were often in the single digits, and sell prices in the tens (or low hundreds for the most patient), locking in multi-bagger returns. As of the latest info, those investors have largely cashed out, and AMD's shareholder list is now light on traditional value names, skewing more to broad-based investors and growth-oriented funds.

### 2. Evaluation Against Buffett's Quality and Value Criteria

Warren Buffett has articulated a number of **financial criteria** he looks for in wonderful businesses. Let's examine AMD on each relevant metric to see if it would pass Buffett's traditional standards:

**Valuation Discipline:** - **P/E ≤ 15**: Buffett prefers a **price-to-earnings ratio** of 15 or less (which implies an earnings yield of \~6.7% or more).
*AMD's status:* **Fail.** AMD's P/E has generally been well above 15 in recent years. In fact, for much of the past decade, AMD had no meaningful P/E (due to losses). When it became profitable, high growth expectations kept the P/E elevated. For example, even at end of 2021, P/E \~56; as of Aug 2025, AMD's forward P/E is still around 40+. AMD has never sustainably traded at a P/E under 15 in the last 10 years. Only perhaps briefly in early 2018 did its trailing P/E approach the high teens (as one-time tax benefits inflated earnings). But on a normalized basis, it's been far above Buffett's cutoff. This reflects that AMD is viewed as a growth stock, not a classic value stock with low P/E. - **P/B ≤ 1.5**: Buffett often cited the **price-to-book** ratio being under 1.5 as a value indicator (though he's de-emphasized book in recent years for certain businesses).
*AMD's status:* **Fail until recently.** For most of the decade, AMD's P/B was extremely high because its book value was very small or negative. Pre-2022, P/B ranged from \~5 to 20+. For instance, at end of 2019, P/B \~25 (stock $45 on book \~$2). After the Xilinx deal, AMD's P/B dropped to \~2 in 2022 (market cap $105B vs equity $52B). That's still above 1.5 (though much closer). As of 2025, P/B \~3.0 (equity \~$57B vs market cap \~$170B). This is above the traditional cutoff. AMD's high P/B reflects that its market value comes mostly from future earnings power (intangible) rather than current book assets - common for tech companies. So AMD fails the strict ≤1.5 test in most periods. - **Margin of Safety - Pay ≥ 25% below intrinsic value:** Buffett emphasizes buying at a significant discount to a conservative intrinsic value estimate.
*AMD's status:* **Uncertain/Fail from a Buffett view.** Determining intrinsic value is subjective, but given AMD's high multiples, it's arguable that AMD's stock hasn't traded at a 25% discount to a **conservative** intrinsic value at least in the last few years. When AMD was, say, $2-$5 in 2015, one could argue it was deeply undervalued relative to what turned out to be its future earnings - but at that time, the intrinsic value was very uncertain (some thought AMD might go bankrupt, so even $2 wasn't obviously a bargain to risk-averse investors). As AMD's prospects improved, the market quickly priced in growth. For a value investor, AMD likely looked **overvalued rather than undervalued** throughout its climb. There were moments (e.g., mid-2022 when AMD's price fell \~60% from peak) where one might argue there was a margin of safety if you believed the pessimism was overdone. But by Buffett's style, AMD stock generally did not offer a 25%+ margin of safety relative to a grounded intrinsic valuation - it has usually been priced for growth.

**Cash Generation & Allocation:** - **Positive Free Cash Flow each year; FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:** Buffett likes businesses that consistently generate cash.
*AMD's status:* **Partial Fail.** AMD's free cash flow track record is mixed. In the past 10 years, AMD had **negative FCF** in at least 4 of those years (2014-2016, 2018 slightly negative). It only consistently turned positive FCF from 2019 onward. However, in the *recent* years (2020-2023), AMD's FCF/Revenue has been healthy - often above 5%. Specifically, 2020: 8.1%, 2021: 19.8%, 2022: 12.1%, 2023 \~10%. So currently AMD meets the &gt;5% FCF margin criterion, but historically it did not each year for 10 years. Buffett would be wary of the years of negative cash flow, as that indicates the business required external capital historically. But the trajectory is positive now. - **Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:** This measures how effectively a company uses the earnings it keeps (retains) to generate further returns (often measured by increase in market value or earnings). One way Buffett measures it is: the incremental market value created for each dollar of retained earnings.
*AMD's status:* **Mostly Pass (in recent period).** If we consider roughly 2017 onwards, AMD retained all its earnings (no dividends) and its market cap increased enormously - far more than earnings retained. For example, from 2017 to 2021, AMD's cumulative retained earnings (net income) was \~$6.3B, and market cap increased by over $150B. That's an extremely high return on retained earnings - showing that reinvesting in R&D, etc., paid off in market appreciation. However, part of market cap rise was multiple expansion (speculative). Another approach: AMD's EPS grew from $0.04 in 2017 to $2.57 in 2021, an increase of $2.53 EPS achieved with about $2.70 of earnings retained per share - roughly a 93% "return" on those retained earnings (very high). By these measures, AMD blew past 12% in the growth phase. That said, using Buffett's exact formula over a full decade (including the early losses) would be messy - AMD had negative retained earnings for a while (accumulated deficit). Since turning profitable, it has utilized retained earnings exceptionally well. So on this criterion, *in recent years* AMD looks great, though earlier it would fail. Buffett might acknowledge the strong management of retained earnings since 2017 but be concerned about the unstable history before that.

**Growth & Predictability:** - **EPS Record: Positive & rising every year for 10 years:** Buffett values a steady upward trend in earnings per share over a long period.
*AMD's status:* **Fail.** AMD does not have 10 years of uninterrupted EPS growth. In fact, it had **losses** for about half of the past decade. EPS was negative from 2012 through 2016. Even after turning positive, EPS has not risen every year: 2022's EPS ($0.84) was down sharply from 2021 ($2.57). The volatility and negative years mean AMD fails the consistency test. It's the opposite of the kind of stable earnings growth company (like a Coca-Cola) that Buffett prefers for core holdings. - **Shareholder Equity 10-yr CAGR ≥ 10% and no year of decline:** Buffett likes to see book value (as a proxy for intrinsic value) compounding at a good rate without setbacks.
*AMD's status:* **Fail (historically).** AMD's book value was nearly wiped out in mid-2010s due to losses, then recovered. There were declines: Equity dropped from $0.45B in 2014 to $0.29B in 2016 (negative CAGR, and absolute drops in those years). Only after 2017 did equity grow consistently - and then it exploded in 2022 due to new shares (not purely retained earnings). If we measure 2013 to 2023, the CAGR of book value is extremely high (because it went from near-zero to $57B, largely via the $49B Xilinx equity addition). But that's not organic growth from earnings alone; it's an acquisition. Buffett would likely not count that as meeting the spirit of the criterion (which assumes growth from within and no losses). Also, AMD had multiple years where equity decreased (2014→2015, 2015→2016). Thus it fails the "no single year of contraction" rule. - **Owner's Earnings positive every year, OE/Market Cap ≥ 10%:** Owner's earnings is Buffett's version of FCF. He wants consistently positive owner earnings, and a high yield on market price.
*AMD's status:* **Fail for consistency, Fail for current yield.** Owner's earnings (similar to FCF here) was negative in the first half of the decade, so not positive every year. It became strongly positive in recent years, but not a perfect 10-year record. As for OE/Market Cap (which is effectively FCF yield), AMD's current OE/Market Cap is low: e.g., in 2021, OE \~$3.3B vs market cap \~$170B = \~1.9%; in 2022, \~$2.9B vs $105B = \~2.8%; in 2023 maybe \~$2.2B vs $160B = \~1.4%. These are far below 10%. Even at trough stock prices, say end of 2022 ($64/share, $105B cap), the OE yield \~2.8% - nowhere near 10%. So AMD doesn't provide the high cash yield Buffett would like for a new purchase.

**Balance-Sheet Safety:** - **Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:** Low leverage is a Buffett preference to ensure safety.
*AMD's status:* **Pass (now), Fail (earlier).** Today AMD's D/E is \~0.04 (virtually no debt relative to equity) - a **pass**. However, earlier in the decade, D/E was sky-high (because equity was tiny or negative). From 2017 onward, AMD's D/E fell under 0.5, and since 2019 it's been well under 0.5. So in current terms, AMD passes easily (it's conservatively financed). Buffett might give credit for the current state, but the historical risk was high. Still, one major risk factor (debt) is now largely mitigated. - **Interest Coverage ≥ 3×:** EBIT at least 3 times interest expense for safety.
*AMD's status:* **Pass (recently), Fail (earlier).** In 2014-2016, AMD had negative EBIT, so coverage was &lt;1 (fail). From 2017 onward, once operating profits resumed and debt was reduced, coverage improved above 3× by 2018 and then went extremely high when debt was near zero. Currently, AMD easily covers interest (in fact interest expense is very low at \~$100M/year vs EBIT of $1B+, so \~10× coverage in 2023). So AMD now passes this criterion by a wide margin. The question for Buffett would be the consistency; early decade it did not, but since at least 2019 it has. - **Interest Exp/Operating Income ≤ 15%:** This is similar to coverage; essentially interest eats no more than 15% of op income.
*AMD's status:* **Pass (now).** In 2020-2021, interest expense was under 1-3% of operating income - negligible. In 2022, it rose with new debt but was \~15% ($190M interest vs $1.26B op income). That's right at the threshold of 15%. In 2023, with slightly better op income and stable interest, it's maybe \~10%. Earlier in the decade, this ratio was meaningless due to negative op income. But focusing on the stable period, AMD meets this comfortably now. No concern here given AMD's low debt. - **Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:** A strong liquidity position to handle short-term obligations.
*AMD's status:* **Pass.** AMD consistently maintained a current ratio around 1.8-2.5 each year. Even at its weakest, it was \~1.5-1.8, which meets the criterion. Currently \~2.1, which is healthy. So AMD has always had sufficient current assets to cover liabilities - likely because it kept cash reserves (and maybe because it had significant inventory and A/R relative to A/P). This would please Buffett, as it indicates no acute liquidity crunch risk.

**Profitability & Efficiency:** - **Gross Margin ≳ 40%:** High gross margins indicate a strong business moat or pricing power.
*AMD's status:* **Pass (recently), Fail (historically).** AMD's gross margin was below 40% for much of the decade. It hovered \~30-35% before 2018. Starting 2019, it crossed 40% and reached \~48% in 2021. After adding Xilinx, GM \~45%. So currently AMD is at \~50% gross margin which is above 40% - a **pass now**. But historically it wouldn't have passed. If Buffett looked at the trend, he'd see improvement into the desired range. Today, AMD's margins are comparable to or better than Intel's (\~42% in 2022) and approaching NVIDIA's (gross \~60%). That suggests AMD has achieved a degree of pricing power and product differentiation. - **Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:** Consistently high net margins show a durable competitive advantage.
*AMD's status:* **Fail.** AMD's net margin exceeded 20% only in 2020 (thanks to that one-time tax gain) at \~25%, and was \~19% in 2021, just shy of 20%. All other years it was far below (often negative or single digits). So it doesn't meet the ≥20% rule on a sustained basis. Even looking ahead, AMD's net margins might improve but are unlikely to average 20% consistently in the near term (due to competitive pressures and high R&D). NVIDIA, by contrast, has net margins &gt;30% in good years - something AMD hasn't achieved. Thus, AMD doesn't have the long-term record of high net margins Buffett would deem evidence of a strong moat. - **Return on Equity (ROE) ≥ 15% average for 10 yrs:** A high ROE sustained indicates efficient use of capital and competitive advantage.
*AMD's status:* **Fail for 10-yr avg.** AMD's ROE is all over the map: negative in loss years, then very high in 2018 (35%) and 2020 (near 50%), then dropping to \~2.5% in 2022 due to a swollen equity base. The 10-year average ROE wouldn't be meaningful given several negative years. If we average 2018-2022, ROE might be around 16% (thanks to two very high years skewing it). But Buffett would likely exclude the unusual leaps and focus on consistency. AMD doesn't have a stable high ROE track record. Only recently did it achieve strong ROE, and then the Xilinx merger diluted it down. Thus, on average it doesn't clear 15% across the full period (with losses included, average ROE is actually negative). So as a long-term measure, AMD fails. - **ROIC (or ROTC) ≥ 12% (10-yr avg):** Return on invested capital measures true business return ignoring capital structure. Buffett looks for businesses that earn high returns on the capital they deploy.
*AMD's status:* **Fail for 10-yr avg, but improving.** During 2014-2016, ROIC was negative. From 2017-2021, AMD's ROIC was rising dramatically (since it had little debt, ROIC \~ ROE): by 2020-2021 ROIC was well above 12% (in 2021, ROIC \~35%). However 2022 ROIC fell to \~2% with the big equity addition. The 10-year average would be pulled way down by negative and low earlier years. So AMD doesn't have a decade of &gt;12% ROIC. We can say in its sweet spot (2018-2021) AMD did exhibit high ROIC (evidence of a potential moat forming in CPUs). But it's not a consistent long-term average yet. Most value investors would want to see a stable 10-year history of strong ROIC, which AMD lacks. - **Return on Assets (ROA) ≥ 6%:** Measures how efficiently profits are generated from total assets.
*AMD's status:* **Mixed.** AMD's ROA was negative for many years. It became high in 2020-2021 (e.g., 2021 ROA = 3.16B/12.4B \~25%). After Xilinx, 2022 ROA = 1.32/67.6 \~2%. So average might be around 5% or less over 10 years (considering big losses earlier). Currently it's probably \~0.7% (0.5B on 72B assets) in 2023 - quite low because of goodwill/intangibles from Xilinx bloating assets. So AMD does not consistently have ≥6% ROA over the decade. Only in the few boom years did it far exceed that. This inconsistency would be a red flag for Buffett historically, although he might consider the intangible asset factor post-merger (a lot of assets are goodwill that don't require reinvestment). - **Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:** Buffett sometimes examines capital intensity. A low ratio means not much of gross profit is eaten by depreciation (implying a lighter capital business).
*AMD's status:* **Pass (approx).** AMD is fabless, so it has relatively low depreciation & amortization on its books relative to gross profit. For example, in 2021, D&A was around $300M (mostly amortization of IP and some depreciation) vs gross profit $7.87B, so \~3.8%. Even including 2022's big jump in amortization (roughly $1.5B amortization for Xilinx intangibles plus $150M depreciation = \~$1.65B) vs gross profit $10.6B, that's \~15.5%. If strictly counting depreciation of tangible assets only, it's even smaller (&lt;1% of gross profit, since AMD's capex is minimal). Even including amortization, 15% is slightly above the 10% threshold. But prior to the acquisition, AMD's Depreciation/GP was far below 10%. With Xilinx intangibles, it temporarily spiked above 10%. Over 10 years, on average, AMD would pass this since for many years it had low capex and depreciation (outsourcing manufacturing keeps this ratio low). So *excluding the accounting amortization*, AMD passes. If including amortization as a form of "depreciation" of acquired IP, it's a borderline pass/fail in 2022-2023 (\~15%) but likely will improve as amortization is fixed and gross profit grows.

**Summary of Buffett Criteria Assessment:**
- *Valuation:* AMD does not meet the classic undervaluation metrics (P/E, P/B) in general. It's usually been priced for growth, not at a bargain low multiple. So it **fails Buffett's strict value filters**. - *Consistent earnings/cash:* AMD's history of losses and volatility is a **fail** on consistent growth and cash flow stability. Only in the last few years did it show strong earnings - and even those swung down in 2022. Buffett typically avoids companies with such erratic pasts. - *High returns on capital:* AMD's recent performance (2018-2021) showed high returns, but the full 10-year picture is mixed. It doesn't have the **long-term unbroken record** of high ROE/ROIC that Buffett loves in consumer monopolies, for instance. This inconsistency would be a **concern** under Buffett's criteria, though he'd acknowledge the positive trajectory. - *Financial strength:* On the bright side, AMD's **balance sheet is now very strong** (debt low, lots of cash, good interest coverage). It went from financially fragile to robust. This portion **passes** today, though earlier it wouldn't have. - *Moat (profitability):* Gross margins climbing to \~50% and the ability to take share from Intel might suggest a developing competitive advantage in design. But net margins are not consistently high enough yet to declare a strong moat. Buffett might view AMD as still in a competitive industry with razor-thin margins historically (semi is tough). So on the **sustainable moat metrics (high margins, stable earnings)** - AMD **falls short** in a Buffettian sense.

In conclusion, AMD would likely not meet Buffett's traditional standards for a "great company" **over the past 10 years**. It was too unpredictable and required a corporate turnaround, something Buffett typically avoids until results are clearly durable. However, the **recent trend (last \~5 years)** is much more favorable: high growth, improving margins, strong cash generation, fortress balance sheet. If these trends persist for another decade, AMD's profile could evolve to fit more of Buffett's criteria (e.g., consistently high ROIC, robust free cash flow, etc.). But at present, on a strict interpretation: - AMD **fails** on valuation (not cheap by Buffett's yardstick). - **Fails** on 10-year consistent growth and profitability. - **Passes** on current financial strength and acceptable (recent) returns in good years. - **Fails** on demonstrating a long-term moat via steady high margins and earnings.

Buffett might admire AMD's turnaround and future potential but likely would not invest, chiefly because the industry is fiercely competitive and the company's historical record is too uneven relative to his beloved "steady compounders."

### 4. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis of AMD's Stock Value

To estimate AMD's intrinsic value, we perform a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis** under three scenarios - **Conservative, Nominal, and Optimistic** - based on different free cash flow (FCF) growth assumptions. We'll use AMD's recent free cash flow as a starting point and apply growth rates derived from the company's historical FCF trajectory over the past decade, then discount those cash flows to present value. Finally, we apply a margin of safety.

**DCF Assumptions Common to All Cases:** - **Base Year FCF:** We use approximately **$2.5 billion** as the current annual free cash flow (around the trailing twelve-month FCF for AMD in mid-2025, in line with 2022's \~$2.85B and 2023's expected \~$2.2B). This will serve as FCF in Year 1 of our model. - **Growth Period:** 10-year explicit forecast of FCF growth, followed by a terminal value. - **Discount Rate:** \~**10%** (reflecting a typical equity hurdle rate for a company of AMD's risk - somewhat higher than for a very stable firm, given cyclical and competitive risk). - **Terminal Growth Rate:** **2%** per year (assuming AMD's FCF growth tapers to a long-term economy-like growth rate after 10 years). - **Shares Outstanding:** \~1.60 billion (for per-share value calculation). - **Net Cash:** AMD has roughly $3.4B net cash (cash minus debt). We will add this to the DCF enterprise value at the end.

Now, the three scenarios differ by FCF growth rate: - **Conservative Case:** Use the **lowest FCF growth rate** AMD has seen in the past 10 years (excluding negative declines, we choose a modest positive growth or even 0%). AMD had some years of FCF decline (negative growth) - a truly conservative approach might assume essentially **0% FCF growth** (flat FCF) over the next decade, to be safe. We'll use **+5%** annually as a conservative positive growth (which is around the low end of AMD's non-decline years - e.g., 2019's FCF grew only slightly from 2018's near-zero). This captures a scenario where AMD grows barely above inflation. - **Nominal (Base) Case:** Use the **average FCF growth** rate of the past years. FCF went from \~$0 in 2017 to \~$3B in 2021 - an enormous rise, not likely to continue. Taking a longer average is tricky due to negatives, but focusing on the recent growth phase: 2018-2022 FCF CAGR was \~100% (because of near-zero baseline). That's too high to assume. Let's assume **15% annual FCF growth**, which is in line with a scenario where AMD continues to gain market share and expand in data center/AI, but also faces maturing in some segments. 15% is roughly AMD's expected revenue CAGR in a bullish analyst view for the next few years (excluding huge AI upside). This optimistic case assumes AMD strongly outperforms current expectations.

With these growth rates, we project FCF for 10 years and then compute a terminal value at year 10 using the 2% growth perpetuity, and discount everything to present at 10%.
