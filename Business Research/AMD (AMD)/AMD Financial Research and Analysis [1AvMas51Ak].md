## Business Model

The business model of Advanced Micro Devices has undergone a profound evolution over the last decade, transitioning from a fabless design shop struggling for relevance to a global leader in high-performance and adaptive computing. The company operates as a fabless semiconductor entity, meaning it focuses entirely on the architecture, design, and software integration of its chips while outsourcing the capital-intensive manufacturing process to specialized foundries, primarily TSMC.<sup>8</sup> This model allows AMD to maintain a lean balance sheet and allocate its capital toward research and development (R&D), which is the lifeblood of its competitive strategy.

### Revenue Streams and Segment Analysis

AMD categorizes its operations into four primary segments: Data Center, Client, Gaming, and Embedded. The revenue distribution as of the 2024 fiscal year reveals the successful execution of its "data-center-first" strategy, with the high-margin infrastructure business now accounting for nearly half of total sales.

The Data Center segment is the crown jewel of the modern AMD, providing high-performance computing (HPC) solutions for cloud giants and enterprise data centers. The Client segment serves the personal computer market, providing the "Zen" architecture-based Ryzen processors that have consistently gained market share from Intel.<sup>8</sup> The Gaming segment remains a stable contributor, supplying the "brains" for the Sony PlayStation and Microsoft Xbox ecosystems, alongside discrete Radeon graphics cards.<sup>8</sup> The Embedded segment, bolstered significantly by the <img src="media/image54.png" style="width:0.30257in;height:0.23997in" /> billion acquisition of Xilinx, provides critical components for the automotive, industrial, and telecommunications sectors, offering high-margin, long-lifecycle products that provide a counter-cyclical hedge to the consumer-driven segments.<sup>8</sup>

### Customer Dynamics and Market Penetration

The company's customer base is characterized by a high degree of concentration, common in the semiconductor industry. A small number of large-scale clients, primarily the "Hyperscalers" (Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, and Oracle Cloud), account for a substantial portion of total revenue.<sup>2</sup> In the third quarter of 2025, the company highlighted a surge in enterprise adoption, with Ryzen PC sell-through increasing by <img src="media/image48.png" style="width:0.36979in;height:0.23626in" /> year-over-year, supported by large-scale corporate deployments in healthcare and manufacturing.<sup>4</sup>

AMD does not typically disclose the exact number of units sold for every SKU, but the scale of its operations is evidenced by its presence in 172 of the world's Top500 supercomputers.<sup>10</sup> In the GPU market, while NVIDIA remains the dominant leader with a <img src="media/image68.png" style="width:0.36979in;height:0.23626in" /> to <img src="media/image74.png" style="width:0.36979in;height:0.23626in" /> share of discrete GPU shipments, AMD has maintained a presence around <img src="media/image65.png" style="width:0.26888in;height:0.23786in" /> to <img src="media/image70.png" style="width:0.26888in;height:0.23786in" />, positioning itself as the primary alternative and "second source" for large-scale deployments that require competitive pricing and open-source software flexibility.<sup>11</sup>

### The Moat: Architectural Leadership and Open Ecosystems

AMD's competitive moat is constructed around three structural advantages: architectural innovation, chiplet-based packaging, and an open software strategy. The "Zen" CPU architecture allowed AMD to regain technical parity and eventually leadership over Intel in performance-per-watt metrics, a critical factor for data center operators looking to minimize electricity costs.<sup>4</sup> By pioneering the use of chiplets-smaller, modular pieces of silicon interconnected via advanced packaging-AMD achieved higher manufacturing yields and lower costs than competitors who relied on monolithic designs.<sup>12</sup>

Furthermore, the company's software strategy serves as a strategic differentiator. While NVIDIA's CUDA is a closed, proprietary system that locks users into their hardware, AMD's ROCm (Radeon Open Compute) is an open-source platform. This openness is increasingly attractive to large-scale customers who want to avoid vendor lock-in and contribute to a communal software ecosystem, as evidenced by the OpenAI and Meta partnerships.<sup>2</sup>

## Headwinds and Risks

The semiconductor industry is defined by high capital intensity, rapid product cycles, and extreme sensitivity to macroeconomic and geopolitical shifts. AMD, despite its recent success, operates under several significant risk umbrellas that could impact its performance in both the short and long term.

### Economic and Industry Risks

In the short term, the company is vulnerable to the cyclicality of consumer spending. The Client and Gaming segments, while currently rebounding, were severely depressed in 2023 following the post-pandemic hardware glut.<sup>13</sup> A global memory shortage currently poses a risk to PC makers, who may be forced to raise laptop and desktop prices, thereby slowing the adoption of AMD's latest Ryzen 9000 series processors.<sup>3</sup> Additionally, there is a pervasive "AI bubble" concern; if the massive capital expenditures by hyperscalers do not result in proportional revenue generation from AI services, a significant correction in data center spending could follow, impacting AMD's most profitable segment.<sup>3</sup>

Inventory management also remains a persistent challenge. In Q3 2025, AMD's inventory-to-COGS ratio rose to 1.74, a level that suggests potential inventory pressure if the anticipated holiday and AI upgrade cycles do not meet volume expectations.<sup>4</sup> The reliance on a small number of customers means the loss or even a moderate spending reduction by a single client like Microsoft or Sony could have a disproportionate impact on the quarterly P&L.<sup>8</sup>

### Political and Geopolitical Risks

Geopolitical tensions represent perhaps the most significant tail risk for AMD. As a fabless company, it is entirely dependent on third-party foundries, primarily TSMC in Taiwan, for its leading-edge production.<sup>8</sup> Any escalation of conflict in the Taiwan Strait would likely lead to a permanent disruption of the global semiconductor supply chain, for which there is currently no viable alternative for high-end 4nm and 5nm nodes.

Furthermore, the U.S.-China trade war has already had a tangible impact. The imposition of export restrictions on high-end AI chips led to the total cancellation of Instinct MI308 shipments to China, a market that previously represented a massive growth opportunity.<sup>2</sup> These regulatory barriers are often permanent or semi-permanent, forcing the company to reconfigure its product roadmap and seek growth in alternative geographic regions. The company also faces increased scrutiny regarding AI safety and responsible use, which could lead to future regulatory constraints on the design and deployment of large-scale GPU clusters.<sup>15</sup>

### Competitive Threats

The competitive landscape remains aggressive. Intel, through its "IDM 2.0" strategy, is investing hundreds of billions to regain manufacturing leadership and is becoming a direct competitor in the foundry space as well as the design space.<sup>8</sup> NVIDIA's dominance in the AI software ecosystem (CUDA) remains a formidable barrier to entry for AMD's Instinct GPUs in certain deep-learning workloads.<sup>11</sup> Failure to continue the rapid cadence of architectural innovation (e.g., if "Zen 6" or the MI400 series misses performance targets) would lead to a rapid loss of market share, given the lack of switching costs for hyperscale customers who can easily pivot to alternative silicon.<sup>1</sup>

## Cost Structure

AMD's cost structure reflects its status as a high-technology R&D powerhouse that operates through a variable-cost manufacturing model. Understanding the interplay between fixed innovation costs and variable production costs is essential for assessing its margin sustainability.

### Fixed and Variable Costs

The company's most significant variable cost is the Cost of Goods Sold (COGS). For fiscal year 2024, COGS reached <img src="media/image71.png" style="width:0.56038in;height:0.23868in" /> billion against <img src="media/image73.png" style="width:0.56038in;height:0.23868in" /> billion in revenue.<sup>16</sup> This includes the price paid to TSMC for wafers, the cost of substrates, and the expenses associated with outsourced assembly and test (OSAT) services. Because AMD is fabless, it does not carry the massive fixed costs of owning and maintaining a semiconductor fabrication plant (a "fab"), which allows it to scale its costs more effectively with revenue volume.<sup>8</sup>

However, the company's fixed costs are substantial, primarily driven by Research and Development (R&D). In the semiconductor world, R&D is not discretionary; it is a prerequisite for survival. For the twelve months ending September 2025, R&D expenses were a record <img src="media/image60.png" style="width:0.56038in;height:0.23868in" /> billion, representing nearly <img src="media/image49.png" style="width:0.36979in;height:0.23626in" /> of revenue.<sup>17</sup> These costs have trended sharply upward as the complexity of chip design increases at advanced nodes.

### Labor and Marketing Expenses

Labor is a major component of both R&D and SG&A. AMD's workforce has expanded from approximately 8,900 in 2017 to 28,000 by late 2024.<sup>20</sup> This rapid hiring has been necessary to build out the software teams required for ROCm and to support the integration of Xilinx and ZT Systems. The average revenue per employee stands at approximately <img src="media/image39.png" style="width:0.45947in;height:0.24018in" /> million, a high figure that demonstrates the scalability of the designer-talent model.<sup>21</sup>

Marketing and advertising expenses, encapsulated within SG&A, have also seen a marked increase. SG&A for the TTM period ending September 2025 was <img src="media/image56.png" style="width:0.56038in;height:0.23868in" /> billion, a <img src="media/image42.png" style="width:0.52669in;height:0.23753in" /> increase year-over-year.<sup>22</sup> This surge is driven by branding efforts for AI PC processors (Ryzen AI) and discrete graphics, as well as the sales teams required to support massive data center contracts.

### Cost Trends and Economic Conditions

During periods of economic expansion, AMD's margins typically expand as it achieves better absorption of its fixed R&D costs and benefits from a "richer" product mix (selling more high-margin server chips vs. low-margin budget CPUs). Conversely, in economic downturns, the high fixed R&D load remains constant even as revenue declines, which can lead to rapid margin contraction. This was evident in 2023, where operating income fell significantly during the PC market slump.<sup>13</sup> However, the company's shift toward the Data Center segment has made its cost structure more resilient, as enterprise infrastructure spending tends to be less volatile than consumer discretionary spending.

## Management and Governance

The modern history of AMD is synonymous with the tenure of Dr. Lisa Su, who assumed the role of President and CEO in October 2014. Her leadership is widely cited as the catalyst for the company's resurgence from the brink of insolvency to its current status as a trillion-dollar-potential technology giant.

### Current Executive Team and Board

The executive leadership team is characterized by stability and technical expertise.

-   **Dr. Lisa T. Su (Chair and CEO)**: An electrical engineer by training, Dr. Su's "best products" strategy prioritized technical excellence over short-term market share. She holds over 4.7 million shares of the company, valued at more than <img src="media/image21.png" style="width:0.20182in;height:0.24431in" /> billion, ensuring deep alignment with shareholders.<sup>23</sup>

-   **Jean Hu (Executive VP, CFO, and Treasurer)**: Formerly the CFO of Marvell, Hu has brought a rigorous focus on capital allocation and free cash flow generation.<sup>6</sup>

-   **Mark Papermaster (Executive VP and CTO)**: A veteran of Apple and IBM, Papermaster has overseen the development of the "Zen" architecture from its inception.<sup>24</sup>

The Board of Directors consists of eight nominees, seven of whom are independent.<sup>23</sup> To ensure robust oversight, given that Dr. Su serves as both Chair and CEO, the board has elected Nora M. Denzel as the Lead Independent Director.<sup>23</sup> The board includes individuals with deep experience in finance, semiconductor manufacturing, and software, such as Mark Durcan (former CEO of Micron) and Abhi Talwalkar (former CEO of LSI).<sup>23</sup>

### Performance and Integrity

Management has established a culture of transparency and execution. During earnings calls, Dr. Su is known for providing realistic, often conservative guidance and addressing analyst questions with technical specificity rather than marketing fluff.<sup>1</sup> The company has successfully navigated complex integrations, notably the <img src="media/image54.png" style="width:0.30257in;height:0.23997in" /> billion Xilinx acquisition, which was completed without major operational disruptions-a rarity for a deal of that size.<sup>8</sup>

There have been no significant scandals or integrity issues raised against the current management team. The company maintains a "Worldwide Standards of Business Conduct" and a specific Code of Ethics for senior finance executives to ensure accurate reporting.<sup>23</sup> Furthermore, a "clawback" policy is in place to recover executive compensation in the event of financial restatements.<sup>23</sup>

### Value Alignment

AMD uses a pay-for-performance compensation model. A significant portion of executive wealth is tied to stock-based awards that vest based on meeting long-term strategic and financial goals. For instance, Dr. Su does not receive additional compensation for her service on the board, and her primary motivation is derived from the long-term appreciation of her equity stake.<sup>23</sup> The company also mandates minimum stock ownership requirements for all non-employee directors, further aligning the board's interests with those of the stockholders.<sup>23</sup>

## Shareholders and Capital Returns

AMD's ownership structure is dominated by large institutional investment firms, a testament to the stock's inclusion in major indices like the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq-100.

### Top Shareholders and Voting Rights

As of March 2025, the primary institutional holders are:

1.  **The Vanguard Group**: 142,442,745 shares (8.8% of class).<sup>23</sup>

2.  **BlackRock, Inc.**: 124,945,206 shares (7.7% of class).<sup>23</sup>

These institutions regularly exercise their voting rights on critical corporate matters. Institutional investors like BlackRock and Vanguard generally support management on these "good governance" and structural proposals, provided they are tied to long-term value creation.<sup>26</sup>

### Share Buyback History vs. Former 10 Years

AMD's approach to capital returns has shifted from preservation to distribution. Between 2011 and 2018, the company performed virtually no buybacks as it was focused on servicing debt and funding R&D during its turnaround phase.<sup>28</sup>

As the company became FCF-positive, it initiated aggressive buyback programs. In May 2025, the board authorized a new <img src="media/image52.png" style="width:0.20182in;height:0.24431in" /> billion share repurchase program, supplementing an existing <img src="media/image50.png" style="width:0.20182in;height:0.24431in" /> billion balance for a total authority of <img src="media/image61.png" style="width:0.30257in;height:0.23997in" /> billion.<sup>29</sup> This compares to 2022, when buybacks peaked at <img src="media/image3.png" style="width:0.35872in;height:0.24267in" /> billion following the Xilinx merger to offset share dilution.<sup>28</sup> In the first quarter of 2025 alone, the company repurchased 7 million shares for <img src="media/image13.png" style="width:0.40348in;height:0.23795in" /> million.<sup>31</sup> Management has explicitly stated that they do not expect to pay dividends in the near future, preferring to use excess cash for buybacks and strategic M&A.<sup>8</sup>

## Key Metrics Trends and Insights

A decade-long analysis of AMD's financial trajectory reveals a classic "S-curve" growth profile. In the 2015-2016 period, the company was a distressed asset, with gross margins struggling below <img src="media/image48.png" style="width:0.36979in;height:0.23626in" /> and a negative net income.<sup>14</sup> The 2017 introduction of the Zen architecture caused a fundamental shift, initiating a period of triple-digit revenue growth in the Data Center segment.

The acquisition of Xilinx in 2022 fundamentally changed the balance sheet, increasing total assets from <img src="media/image30.png" style="width:0.30257in;height:0.23997in" /> billion to <img src="media/image31.png" style="width:0.30257in;height:0.23997in" /> billion and providing a stable foundation of "Embedded" revenue that mitigated the volatility of the consumer PC cycle.<sup>9</sup> However, this deal also introduced significant non-cash amortization charges, which have historically suppressed GAAP net income while FCF remained strong.<sup>2</sup>

Looking forward to the 2026-2028 horizon, the primary driver will be the conversion of "AI pipeline" to "AI revenue." With strategic deals like the 6GW OpenAI partnership and the "Helios" rack deployments, AMD is successfully carving out a role as the essential alternative to NVIDIA.<sup>2</sup> The financial success of this period will be measured by the company's ability to drive non-GAAP gross margins toward the <img src="media/image35.png" style="width:0.36979in;height:0.23626in" /> threshold, a target that appears achievable if data center revenues continue to comprise more than <img src="media/image27.png" style="width:0.36979in;height:0.23626in" /> of the total mix.
