## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Netflix's **Q3 2025** results showed robust revenue growth but a mixed bottom line. Revenue reached **$11.51 billion** (up 17.2% year-over-year), matching analyst expectations . Operating income was **$3.25 billion** (up 12%) with an **operating margin of \~28%**, which fell short of the 31.5% guidance due to a one-time $619 million charge for a Brazilian tax dispute . Excluding that charge, management noted the margin would have exceeded forecast . Net income was **$2.55 billion** (up 8% YoY) , and **diluted EPS was $5.87**, which missed Wall Street's \~$6.97 consensus . The EPS miss and the tax-related margin compression led to a 5%+ share price drop post-earnings .

Importantly, Netflix's subscriber metrics are no longer reported, but engagement hit record highs. The company now emphasizes financials and engagement over raw subscriber counts . By region, revenue grew strongly: +17% in UCAN (U.S./Canada), +18% EMEA, +10% LATAM, +21% APAC . Free cash flow was **$2.66 billion** for Q3 and Netflix raised its full-year 2025 FCF forecast to \~$9 billion (from $8-8.5 B) due to lower content spend and timing . With content production back in full swing post-strikes, Netflix still projects Q4 2025 revenue of \~$11.96 B (+17% YoY) with a 23.9% operating margin . Overall, Q3 showed **healthy top-line expansion driven by pricing, subscriber growth, and advertising**, while one-off costs temporarily crimped margins .

## **Business Model**

**Revenue Streams:** Netflix operates a subscription-based streaming service as its core business model. The **vast majority of revenue (≈95-98%) comes from monthly membership fees** paid by subscribers for various plans . This includes the traditional ad-free plans and the newer ad-supported tier. A **small but growing portion (≈2-5%) now comes from advertising** sales on the ad-supported membership plan . For instance, analysts estimated about \~$0.66 B of Q3 2025 revenue (\~5.7%) was from the advertising tier . Historically, Netflix also had a DVD-by-mail rental business, but this was wound down in 2023. Other potential income sources (merchandising, licensing Netflix original content to third parties, mobile games, etc.) remain negligible in revenue contribution. Netflix's **customer base** is massive and global. As of Q4 2024, Netflix had **301.6 million paid subscribers worldwide** , up \~15.9% from \~260 M at end of 2023 . (Netflix has since stopped reporting subscriber counts, but this was the last disclosed figure.) The company's reach spans **190+ countries**. The largest markets are the U.S./Canada with \~89.6 M subs, EMEA with \~101 M, Latin America \~53 M, and APAC \~57 M as of Q3 2024 . **Subscriber growth** has re-accelerated recently due to paid sharing initiatives and the introduction of the cheaper ad-supported tier. In 2024 alone, Netflix added a record \~41 M members . Essentially, **Netflix sells subscriptions** (monthly or annually via partners) - it "sells hours of entertainment" delivered via internet streaming. With over **$39 B in 2024 revenue** for \~300 M members, the *average revenue per membership* is roughly $11 per month.

**Moat and Competitive Advantages:** Netflix has built a strong competitive moat through its **scale, brand, and content ecosystem**. It spends heavily on content (\~$18 B cash content spend projected for 2025) to maintain a vast library of original films/series in multiple languages . This provides a two-fold moat: a *content moat* (a deep catalog of exclusive hits like *Stranger Things*, *Squid Game*, etc.) and a *brand moat* (over 25 years building a brand synonymous with streaming). Netflix's subscriber base scale (300M+ users) also yields **network effects** on the content side - large viewership justifies big-budget productions, which attract more subscribers in turn . Additionally, Netflix's **technology platform** and personalization algorithm are key advantages . Its recommendation engine and user-friendly interface keep engagement high, making it hard for new entrants to replicate the same quality of user experience. Netflix is available on virtually every device and integrated into many cable boxes and smart TVs, further entrenching its position. Lastly, Netflix's global distribution (localized content in many countries) and data on viewing habits create a feedback loop to invest in content that subscribers want, strengthening its moat over time . In summary, Netflix's moat comes from its **strong brand, massive content spend, leading scale of subscribers, and proprietary tech** - all of which make it the premier streaming service in much of the world.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

Netflix faces several **short-term headwinds** as well as **long-term risks** that could impact its business:

-   **Short-term/Temporary Factors:**

    -   **Content Production Disruptions:** Industry-wide strikes by writers and actors in 2023 delayed some Netflix releases. Such production halts can temporarily slow subscriber growth or engagement due to fewer new shows. However, these are expected to resolve (the 2023 WGA/SAG strikes have ended) and represent timing issues rather than permanent demand loss.

    -   **Foreign Exchange Fluctuations:** With \~60% of revenue from outside the U.S., a strong dollar can reduce reported revenue growth in the short term. For example, currency effects modestly impacted 2022-2023 revenue growth (Netflix reports "constant currency" growth \~1-2% higher) .

    -   **Macro-economic Climate:** In tougher economic climates (high inflation or recession), consumers may cut discretionary spend like streaming services. Netflix saw slower growth in early 2022 amid post-pandemic belt-tightening. Advertising revenue is also cyclical - a soft ad market (as in early 2023) is a short-term headwind for Netflix's nascent ad-tier. However, thus far, demand for Netflix has proven fairly resilient even in downturns (it can be a low-cost entertainment alternative).

    -   **Competition Promotions:** Rivals like Disney+ or Amazon Prime Video occasionally launch short-term promotions or exclusive hit releases that temporarily draw audience attention away. For instance, a highly anticipated show on a competitor in a given quarter could softene Netflix's subscriber additions in that period - but such effects are usually transient.

-   **Long-term/Permanent Factors:**

    -   **Intense Streaming Competition:** The streaming wars mean Netflix faces permanent competition from Disney (Disney+, Hulu), Warner Bros Discovery (Max), Amazon (Prime Video), Apple TV+, and others. Competitors are well-funded and continue to invest heavily. This **threatens Netflix's long-term subscriber saturation and pricing power** if consumers have many alternatives . While Netflix remains the leader, the market is crowded and **competitive pressures on content costs and subscriber acquisition are here to stay**.

    -   **Market Saturation in Key Regions:** In its most mature market, the U.S./Canada, Netflix's household penetration is high (\~60%+ of households). Future growth in these markets is harder, and small missteps (price hikes or content misses) could lead to rising churn. This saturation risk means Netflix must rely on ARPU increases (which have limits due to price sensitivity - surveys show U.S. consumers are increasingly likely to cancel over small price hikes ) and new revenue streams (ads, account-sharing fees) or find growth in emerging markets.

    -   **Content Cost Spiral:** Long term, there is risk that **content production costs continue rising** faster than Netflix can grow revenue. Big-budget shows and talent deals are very expensive (Netflix's content obligations exceed $21 B). If competitor bidding wars or inflation drive content costs up, Netflix's margins could be permanently pressured if it cannot keep membership prices rising in tandem . The company has committed to increasing operating margins each year, but sustaining that while investing \~$17-18 B/year in content is an ongoing challenge.

    -   **Regulatory and Political Risks:** Governments globally are increasingly scrutinizing streaming. Potential long-term impacts include: content quotas (e.g. the EU might require a % of local content), stricter censorship in some regions, or even antitrust issues (though Netflix isn't a monopoly, its scale draws attention). **Tax disputes** like the ongoing Brazil issue show Netflix's exposure in multiple jurisdictions . Additionally, data privacy or net neutrality regulations could raise costs or limit Netflix's flexibility in some markets.

    -   **Technological Disruption:** While not imminent, the media landscape can shift with new tech. E.g., the rise of short-form video (TikTok, YouTube) and interactive entertainment (gaming) presents alternative ways consumers spend screen time. Over the long run, if younger generations prefer user-generated or interactive content, Netflix must adapt its content strategy. Likewise, improvements in piracy technology or account-sharing methods could erode Netflix's subscriber base if not mitigated (Netflix took a hard stance on password sharing in 2023 to combat this risk).

    -   **Execution Risk & Talent:** Netflix's success hinges on continually delivering compelling content. The **loss of key creative executives or a string of content flops** could permanently damage its brand loyalty. Management acknowledges the need to maintain hit shows globally . In the long term, if Netflix fails to produce content that resonates with audiences (or if a competitor consistently out-innovates on content), its competitive advantage could wane.

In summary, Netflix's short-term headwinds (production delays, currency swings, macro softness) are mostly manageable and transient. The more critical **long-term risks are structural**: an increasingly crowded streaming market with high content costs and finite consumer attention. Netflix will need to leverage its brand, scale, and execution to navigate these challenges - for example, by expanding into advertising and possibly other verticals (gaming, merchandise) to reinforce its moat against permanent competitive and economic pressures .

## **Cost Structure**

Netflix's cost structure consists of **both fixed and variable components**, driven primarily by content production and distribution costs. Here's a breakdown:

-   **Cost per Unit (Subscriber or Hour):** The largest cost by far is content. Netflix spent \~$13.2 B on content amortization in 2022 (the cost recognized on the income statement) , which was \~42% of revenue - and even more in cash terms ($17 B+ in 2022 cash content additions) . This content cost is largely fixed upfront (producing a series or acquiring rights has a high fixed cost) but yields variable "per-unit" value as it is streamed by millions of subscribers. In effect, **content amortization per subscriber** was about $80 yearly in 2022 (i.e. $19.17 B cost of revenue / \~238 M members in 2022) - so roughly $6.70 per member per month on content. Fortunately, as Netflix adds subscribers, those fixed content costs are spread over a larger base. This scale led to **gross profit improving from $5.8 B in 2018 to $17.96 B in 2024**, with gross margin rising from \~37% to 46% . In other words, **cost per subscriber has been effectively reduced** by scaling, contributing to higher margins.

-   **Content Costs (Largest Cost - Semi-variable):** These include amortization of streaming content assets and licensing fees. Content production behaves **like a fixed cost in the short run** (money is committed upfront to produce or license a show regardless of current subscriber count) but variable in the long run (Netflix can adjust its content budget each year). In 2024, cost of revenues was **$21.04 B** (54% of revenue) , composed mostly of content amortization. This cost grew more slowly than revenue in recent years, improving gross margin (e.g. cost of rev +9.6% in 2024 vs revenue +15.7%) . *Content spend* can be flexed in different economic conditions: Netflix trimmed cash content spend in 2020 during COVID (improving FCF) and has indicated it will moderate spend growth to boost free cash flow from 2024 onward (content spend in 2024 was flat vs 2023) . Nonetheless, content remains the most significant cost by far - essentially the "raw material" of Netflix's service.

-   **Labor Expenses:** Netflix had 12,800 full-time employees at end of 2022 . Employee salaries (particularly in engineering, content and marketing teams) contribute to **Technology & Development** and **G&A expenses** on the income statement. Tech & Dev was $2.93 B in 2024 (7.5% of revenue) , which includes software engineers, product developers, and infrastructure costs. General & Administrative was $1.70 B (4.4% of revenue) , which covers corporate staff, legal, finance, etc. Netflix is known for paying top-of-market salaries; its corporate culture prioritizes hiring and retaining high performers. While these costs scale with headcount (and thus are partly variable), Netflix's headcount growth has been much slower than revenue growth - indicating good operating leverage on the labor side. For perspective, **revenue per employee** is very high ($2.6 million per employee in 2022), reflecting efficient use of labor. In economic downturns, Netflix can slow hiring or curtail bonuses to control labor costs, though it hasn't done large layoffs like some peers.

-   **Advertising & Marketing:** Marketing is a significant semi-variable cost. In 2024, **Sales & Marketing expense was $2.92 B** (7.5% of revenue) . Marketing includes advertising campaigns, online promotions, and publicity for content launches. Notably, marketing expense grew much slower (+\~3.6%) than revenue in 2024 , and as a percentage of revenue has actually **declined** from \~9% in 2020 to \~7.5% in 2024 . This shows the **scaling efficiency** - Netflix's global brand recognition allows it to spend *less* proportionally on marketing to acquire each new subscriber now. However, marketing spend can vary with the content slate; when Netflix has a big new show or is entering a new market, it will invest in promotion. For example, marketing jumped 65% in 2018 to $2.37 B as Netflix ramped up outreach in emerging markets and for a surge of original content . Marketing expense is easier to dial back in tough times - it's more discretionary than content spend. In a recession, Netflix could temporarily cut marketing (relying on word-of-mouth and its platform UI to drive engagement), thus it acts as a buffer variable cost.

-   **Other Costs:** *Technology & Development* ($2.93 B in 2024 ) covers R&D of the streaming platform, content delivery network (Open Connect), and product innovation (like gaming). These costs have grown but remain around \~7-8% of revenue. *General & Administrative* ($1.70 B in 2024 ) is \~4% of revenue and includes relatively fixed corporate costs (accounting, HR, facilities). Finally, *Interest Expense* on debt was $718 M in 2024 - with $14.5 B gross debt, this cost is **fixed** in the short term (Netflix must service debt regardless of subscriber count). However, as Netflix has generated positive free cash flow, it stopped adding new debt and even paid down some; interest expense peaked at \~$767 M in 2020 and has slightly trended down since . If interest rates rise or Netflix refinances, this could vary, but overall interest is a relatively small cost (under 2% of revenue in 2024).

**Cost Structure Trends:** Over the past decade, Netflix's cost structure has shown **economies of scale**. Key costs like content and marketing grew in absolute terms but **declined as a percentage of revenue** after 2015. For instance, cost of revenue was 61% of sales in 2015, dropping to \~54% by 2024 as revenue growth outpaced content spend . Similarly, marketing was \~10% of revenue in 2016, falling to \~7-8% by 2021-2024 . Technology and G&A have remained relatively steady percentages. During economic expansions, Netflix has invested aggressively in content and growth initiatives (increasing absolute costs), but in slower periods it has demonstrated cost discipline. Notably, after a subscriber dip in early 2022, Netflix curtailed expense growth to re-focus on profitability and cash flow, which resulted in operating margin stabilization around \~20% and a swing to positive free cash flow in 2022-2023 .

In adverse conditions (e.g. a recession), we can expect Netflix to **prioritize cost control** on the more flexible areas: slow content spend growth, scale back marketing, and pause headcount expansion. Indeed, in 2022-2023, Netflix guided to lower content cash spend (around $17 B annually, down from a pre-COVID peak of $13 B in 2019 growing to $17 B in 2021) and it delivered record free cash flow by timing and trimming spend. Conversely, in boom times or to counter new competitors, Netflix has shown willingness to **spend aggressively (particularly on content)** to defend its market position. Overall, content remains the dominant cost (essentially the "fixed cost" of running a streaming service at scale), while other cost categories exhibit healthy scaling efficiencies. Netflix's ability to manage these costs while growing revenue will determine its future margin trajectory.

*(Cost breakdown: For 2024, Cost of Rev $21.0 B, S&M $2.92 B, Tech $2.93 B, G&A $1.70 B . Content amortization \~80% of Cost of Rev, rest is streaming delivery infrastructure and other.*)

## **Management & Governance**

**Executive Leadership:** Netflix's management team blends long-tenured insiders and new expertise. Co-founder **Reed Hastings** led as CEO from 1997 until stepping down in 2023; he remains Chairman of the Board . Reed's visionary leadership and "freedom and responsibility" culture shaped Netflix's success. In 2020, Netflix established a co-CEO structure: **Ted Sarandos** (Chief Content Officer) and **Greg Peters** (former COO) became co-CEOs, with Greg Peters officially elevated when Hastings moved to Chairman . **Ted Sarandos** has been with Netflix since 2000, masterminding its originals strategy - his deep relationships in Hollywood and content savvy are crucial. **Greg Peters** joined Netflix in 2008 and led International expansion and product - he brings technical and operational strengths. This duo balances creative and operational leadership, and both are well-regarded (Barron's named them top CEOs of 2025) . Other key executives include **Spencer Neumann (CFO)** since 2019, **Bela Bajaria (Chief Content Officer)** succeeding Sarandos in that role, and **Scott Stuber (Head of Global Films)**. Netflix's **board of directors** in 2025 consists of 12 members led by Reed Hastings (Chairman). Notables on the board include tech investor Brad Smith, former Disney executive Anne Sweeney, and others with diverse expertise. In 2025, longtime director **Jay Hoag** (on board since 1999) was *voted off by shareholders* due to concerns over his low attendance - a rare instance of shareholder dissent indicating increased governance scrutiny. The board promptly accepted his resignation and appointed new independent directors such as **Ellie Mertz** (Airbnb CFO) to bolster financial oversight .

**Track Record & Integrity:** Management has generally delivered on its growth targets and been transparent about challenges. Reed Hastings famously navigated the 2011 Qwikster debacle openly, taking responsibility. In recent years, management set incremental operating margin improvement goals and achieved them (e.g. reaching \~20% operating margin by 2020 from single digits earlier) . When subscriber growth stalled in early 2022, Netflix didn't hide the issue - they candidly reported the first subscriber loss in a decade and outlined a strategy (crackdown on password sharing and launching an ad-tier), which they executed successfully in 2023. This suggests a willingness to confront problems head-on rather than masking them. On earnings calls, **analysts often remark that Netflix management is direct and long-term focused**, albeit sometimes guarded on competitive data like viewership (Netflix only recently started sharing more metrics like hours viewed). There have been no major scandals or accounting issues tied to Netflix's leadership - financial statements are clean and internal controls solid. If anything, some critics argued Netflix was not transparent enough about certain metrics (like when it stopped reporting subscriber guidance and later member counts), but management explained it as a shift toward more relevant engagement metrics . Overall, management's credibility with investors is fairly strong; for example, after a surprise subscriber dip in 2022, they re-accelerated growth by 2023 through decisive actions, restoring confidence.

Executives tend to be **responsive to investor concerns**. In 2022, facing questions about profitability, they committed to positive free cash flow and delivered a record \~$6.9 B FCF in 2023 . There is little evidence of unethical behavior - no executive controversies of note. The fact that shareholders ousted a director in 2025 for governance reasons and management/the board accepted it shows a healthy governance environment not dominated by insiders . **Tenure** of the top team is long: Sarandos 23+ years, Hastings 26 years, Peters \~15 years, indicating stability. Questions of integrity have been few; one could argue management was optimistic in late 2021 about continuing high growth (just before the 2022 correction), but they quickly adjusted strategy when reality diverged.

**Alignment & Ownership:** Netflix's insiders own relatively small stakes in aggregate (&lt;2% of shares) , given how large the company has become. Co-founder Reed Hastings held around 1% (he's sold some and donated large portions: in 2024 he \*\*gifted 2.79 M shares ($1.6 B) to philanthropy\*\* ). Other top executives like Ted Sarandos and Greg Peters each own fractions of a percent (they regularly sell stock as part of compensation). While insider ownership isn't huge, **management's wealth is significantly tied to Netflix's stock performance** via past and ongoing stock option grants. For example, Reed Hastings' net worth largely comes from Netflix stock - even after donations he has a substantial holding, giving him motivation to see the business thrive long-term. There is no indication of insiders cashing out irresponsibly; sales have been systematic for diversification or charitable reasons, not panic selling. Notably, Pershing Square's Bill Ackman took comfort that Netflix **uses excess cash for buybacks rather than executives siphoning it** (though Ackman's Netflix bet was short-lived) .

Management compensation is structured to encourage growth: in 2020s, bonuses were tied to operating margin and subscriber goals. As Netflix reached maturity on subs, focus shifted to profitability metrics which align management with shareholder interests of cash flow growth. In 2023, shareholders approved the executive pay packages for Sarandos and Peters, reflecting confidence that their incentives are reasonable . There isn't significant insider buying on dips - instead, Netflix's capital return is via share repurchases. However, the new buyback (discussed below) can be seen as management indicating the stock is undervalued by using company cash to buy shares, indirectly aligning with shareholders.

In governance matters, Netflix historically had a classified board and some anti-takeover measures, but under investor pressure it has improved governance (e.g., declassified board elections, which enabled the 2025 director vote outcome) . There was an instance in 2019 of a shareholder proposal on content governance (related to publishing metrics), which management opposed, preferring to run the company with flexibility - but overall no major integrity complaints. The departure of Hastings from day-to-day role was smooth, reflecting strong internal succession planning (Sarandos/Peters). **No red flags** like SEC investigations or lawsuits have targeted Netflix's management. Summarily, Netflix's management team is seen as **competent, candid, and mostly aligned with shareholder interests**, with a track record of pivoting when necessary and executing on ambitious long-term strategies (like streaming itself, then original content, then global expansion, now advertising).

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns**

**Ownership Structure:** Netflix's shareholder base is dominated by large institutional investors. The top three holders are **Vanguard Group (\~8.9% ownership)**, **BlackRock (\~7.9%)**, and **Fidelity (FMR \~5.3%)** . Together the top 10 institutions own \~36% of Netflix . Vanguard alone holds \~38 M shares (\~8.8%) across its index funds , making it the biggest single stakeholder - though as a passive investor, it doesn't intervene in management decisions . BlackRock's \~33 M shares (7-8%) are similarly held mostly via index and ETF products . These institutions generally vote in line with proxy advisor recommendations on governance matters. Indeed, in **June 2025, shareholders (led by institutions) voted out lead independent director Jay Hoag** over governance concerns . Groups like BlackRock and Vanguard likely followed proxy advisor ISS's advice to oust Hoag due to his low meeting attendance (50%) and very long tenure . This unprecedented 78% "against" vote shows institutional shareholders actively exercising oversight. In response, Netflix's board accepted Hoag's resignation and appointed new independent directors, signaling respect for shareholder voice .

Other notable owners include **State Street (\~4.0%)**, **Capital Group's Capital World (\~2.8%)**, and **T. Rowe Price (\~2.7%)** . No single shareholder or founder has a controlling stake - Reed Hastings, after stock sales and gifts, holds only a few million shares (&lt;0.5%). This widely-held structure means Netflix is essentially controlled by the collective decisions of large institutional investors, primarily index funds. Insiders (executives and directors) own under 2% combined , so there's little in the way of founder control or dual-class shares (Netflix has only one class of common stock).

**Voting and Shareholder Proposals:** In recent shareholder meetings, the top voting matters (besides director elections) have included executive compensation and shareholder proposals on social issues. In 2025, investors voted strongly *with*management on most items: for example, Netflix's say-on-pay executive compensation was approved (unlike at some peers like WBD where pay was rejected) . However, two shareholder proposals in 2025 - one requesting a report on charitable contributions' potential ideological bias, and another demanding a report on risks of Netflix's diversity policies - were **overwhelmingly voted down (\~329 M votes against vs \~1.6 M for each)** . This indicates top shareholders (BlackRock, Vanguard, etc.) did not support these anti-ESG proposals, aligning with Netflix's management which recommended voting no. Essentially all major institutional investors voted with management's stance, highlighting confidence in Netflix's current policies and a rejection of political interference in content decisions. Another area of interest is that Netflix does not pay a dividend, so some shareholders occasionally inquire about capital returns - management has favored buybacks once cash flows allowed, which shareholders have generally welcomed.

**Capital Return Policy:** Netflix historically reinvested all cash into growth, but as the business matured, it initiated share repurchases in 2021. In **March 2021, Netflix's Board authorized a $5 billion share buyback program** (open-ended) . After generating positive free cash flow, Netflix began executing on this in mid-2021. In **September 2023, the Board expanded the repurchase authorization by another $10 billion** , bringing the total authorized buybacks to $15 billion. Netflix has been actively buying back stock: during 2023, it repurchased **14.51 million shares for $6.045 billion** (an average price around $416/share) . By end of 2023, about **$8.4 billion remained authorized** for future buybacks . The pace continued in 2024-2025 - for instance, in Q2 2025 Netflix bought back **1.5 million shares for $1.6 billion**, leaving \~$12 billion authorization still available . These repurchases are significant: in 2023, the net share count dropped from \~432.8 M to \~427.8 M (about a 1.2% reduction). Netflix indicated it will use excess cash (beyond needs for content and a cash buffer) for buybacks, a strategy many large shareholders support as it returns capital without signaling a commitment like a dividend.

**Comparison to Prior Years:** Until 2021, Netflix did not return any cash to shareholders - it raised capital via debt to fund growth. This changed once Netflix became FCF-positive. Over the *former 10 years*, the idea of buybacks was floated by management only after achieving a stable cash position (Netflix held \~$8 B cash on balance sheet by 2020). The current buyback plan is thus a **new development reflecting Netflix's transition into a cash-generative business**. The contrast is stark: 2011-2020, $0 returned; 2021-2025, potentially up to $15 B via repurchases. For example, **2015-2019**Netflix instead issued equity (net +$1.8 B) as part of employee comp and even did secondary offerings in earlier years; now it's retiring shares. This underscores a strategic shift - management now believes Netflix can self-fund content and still have cash to shrink the float, a sign of confidence in the company's intrinsic value.

Major shareholders, mainly passive index funds, typically don't agitate for dividends or particular buyback targets, but they generally favor the disciplined capital return Netflix is undertaking. There hasn't been activism by hedge funds forcing capital returns (Ackman's brief stake in 2022 was supportive of buybacks but he sold out due to other concerns ). The **shareholder base appears satisfied** with Netflix's balanced approach: investing in growth (e.g. gaming initiatives, more originals) while launching significant buybacks only after reaching consistent profitability . Top holders like Capital Group or T. Rowe Price, known as long-term investors, likely appreciate the reduction in share count as it boosts their ownership slightly and EPS.

Going forward, with \~$12 B authorization left and \~$9 B FCF expected this year , Netflix could repurchase \~$4-5 B per year without levering up - effectively a \~1-2% annual buyback yield at current market cap. There's no dividend and none is expected soon, as management prefers the flexibility of buybacks (and many shareholders are fine with that given Netflix's still-evolving growth opportunities).

In summary, **ownership is diffuse but institutionally concentrated**, and those institutions have shown they will exercise their voting power on governance (e.g. ousting a director) and align with value-creating actions (like buybacks). No single shareholder dominates decision-making, which puts the onus on management to keep broad shareholder trust. So far, Netflix's shareholder relations appear strong - evidenced by high approval of management proposals and the stock's inclusion in major indices making it a must-hold for many funds.

## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

Warren Buffett's criteria for a "great company" can be applied to Netflix, revealing some strengths but also several shortcomings. Below we evaluate Netflix against key Buffett-style standards:

**1. Valuation Discipline:**

-   **P/E ≤ 15 (vs history):** *Fail.* Netflix's price-to-earnings ratio has almost never been at or below 15 in the past decade. It averaged **125×** over ten years . Even after the stock correction in 2022, the P/E was \~29× at end-2022 . As of end-2024, P/E was **44×** - far above the ≤15 benchmark. Only briefly in mid-2022 did Netflix's forward P/E approach \~15-20 after the stock plunge, but historically Netflix traded at high multiples due to expected growth. This fails Buffett's typical "no more than 15" rule; Netflix has been priced for growth, not value.

-   **P/B ≤ 1.5:** *Fail.* Netflix's price-to-book ratio has been well beyond 1.5 for most of its history. It ranged roughly **19-23×** during 2015-2018 and was **\~18.7× in 2019**. Even during the 2022 trough, P/B never fell below \~6.3× . At end-2024, P/B was \~15.7× . This is nowhere near Buffett's ≤1.5 criterion for a margin of safety on assets. Netflix's high intangible asset value (brand, content library) isn't on the balance sheet, so investors have always paid a huge premium to book value. Thus, by classic P/B metrics, Netflix fails badly.

-   **Margin of Safety (≥25% below intrinsic value):** *Fail (currently).* Based on a discounted cash flow analysis, Netflix does not appear to trade at a 25% discount to a conservative intrinsic value - in fact it looks overvalued by Buffett standards. A **conservative DCF** (using 6% FCF growth) yields an intrinsic value around **$272/share**, which is far below Netflix's current market price ($1,200 in late 2025) - Netflix is trading at a premium, not a discount (see DCF section). Even an optimistic scenario valued Netflix \~$576/share, still half the current price. This means no margin of safety is present; the stock price embeds very high growth expectations. Historically too, Netflix almost always traded at optimistic valuations. Buffett would say "no" here - the stock has never been on sale 25% below a rational intrinsic value in the past decade; if anything it was often overpriced relative to fundamentals (aside from possibly mid-2012 when Carl Icahn bought at \~$58). In sum, Netflix's valuation has been too rich for a Buffett-style investor.

**2. Cash Generation & Allocation:**

-   **Positive Free Cash Flow each year & FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:** *Mixed/Fail.* Netflix only recently achieved consistent positive free cash flow. For most of the past decade, it had **negative FCF** (investing heavily in content). From 2012 through 2019, Netflix's FCF was negative every year (e.g., -$3.14 B in 2019) . This fails Buffett's preference for businesses that throw off cash annually. However, a turning point occurred in 2020 when Netflix had a small positive FCF (\~$1.93 B) due to pandemic-related production delays, then roughly breakeven in 2021 (-$132 M) , and then strongly positive: **$1.62 B in 2022 and $6.93 B in 2023** . By 2023, FCF/Revenue was \~20.5% , easily above 5%. So Netflix's recent performance passes the FCF margin criterion, but over a 10-year span it does not - only 3 of the last 10 years had positive FCF, and the 10-year average FCF margin was about -4%. Buffett would be wary of those earlier cash deficits and the late improvement would need to be proven sustainable (2024 FCF margin \~17.8% suggests it might be). On balance, as of 2025 Netflix is turning into a cash generator, but historically it fails the consistency test.

-   **Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:** *Pass (recently).* Buffett measures how well a company turns each dollar of retained profit into higher earnings. We examine increases in net income relative to earnings retained. Netflix's performance here is mixed but generally strong in recent years. For example, from 2018 to 2023, Netflix retained \~$19.6 B earnings (cumulatively) and increased annual net income by \~$4.2 B (from $1.21 B to $5.41 B) . That's about a 21% return on those retained earnings - quite good. Year-by-year, Netflix's *incremental returns* on retention were volatile (negative in 2022 due to a rare profit dip, but &gt;30% in years like 2017 and 2021) . Using Buffett's method: Netflix's **5-year average RORE** (2018-2023) is around 16-18%, above the 12% benchmark. This suggests Netflix has generally reinvested profits effectively - content investments led to substantially higher profits over time. Caveat: early in the decade, RORE was lower as profits were small, but as profits grew, the high incremental returns indicate *retained earnings were well-spent*. Thus, overall Netflix **passes** this criterion recently by generating &gt;12% returns on reinvested capital (particularly evident 2020 onward), though one might mark it "inconclusive" over the entire 10-year span due to the evolving business model (heavy retention with delayed payoffs).

**3. Growth & Predictability:**

-   **EPS Record - positive & rising every year for 10 yrs:** *Fail.* While Netflix's earnings per share have grown tremendously (from $0.28 in 2013 to \~$11.24 in 2023) , they have **not risen every single year**. Notably, **2022 EPS ($9.95) fell from 2021 ($11.24)** due to the profit pullback that year . Additionally, going back further, 2012 EPS was lower than 2011 (Netflix had a temporary earnings drop during its Qwikster fiasco). Buffett looks for a decade of unbroken EPS growth - Netflix missed that with at least those two declines. Also, EPS were negligible in early years (2013 EPS $0.28, 2014 $0.63) , then soared by 2018 ($2.78) and onwards. The trend is strongly upward overall (2023 EPS \~$12.20, 2024 expected \~$19.25), but the **sequence had hiccups**. So Netflix fails the strict Buffett test of "every year EPS higher than the prior."

-   **10-year Book Value/Share CAGR ≥ 10%, no year of contraction:** *Pass.* Netflix's **book value per share**compounded far above 10% annually. From $2.23 in 2015 to \~$57.8 in 2024 is a \~41% CAGR (even excluding 2024, it was $47.6 in 2023 - still \~34% CAGR) . More importantly, **book value per share never fell year-over-year during the last decade** - it rose each year from 2013 onward. For instance, BVPS grew from $8.27 (2017) to $12.00 (2018) to $17.30 (2019) and so on . There were no contractions in shareholder equity on an annual basis either; equity increased even in 2022 despite net income dip (since no dividends and only started buybacks after 2021). This indicates Netflix reinvested earnings and capital effectively to consistently build net worth. Buffett would see the steady book value growth (a proxy for intrinsic value growth) as a **positive**. It's worth noting Netflix's book value is relatively small due to intangible assets being off-balance sheet, but the metric still grew &gt;10% each year, satisfying the criterion. So Netflix passes this test with flying colors - equity per share grew without interruption.

-   **Owner's Earnings positive every year, OE/Market Cap ≥ 10%:** *Fail.* "Owner's earnings" (Buffett's term for true cash earnings) for Netflix have not been positive every year historically. For much of the decade, Netflix had negative owner's earnings because free cash flow was negative (Buffett often approximates OE as FCF for capital-intensive firms). Netflix's **Owner Earnings turned positive only in 2020** and then dipped, then strongly positive 2022-2024. For example, 2015-2019 owner's earnings were all negative (as Netflix poured cash into content) . That fails the consistency test. Moreover, **Owner's Earnings yield** (OE/Market Cap) has been far below 10%. Even in 2023, with FCF \~$6.93 B, market cap \~$208 B, the yield was \~3.3% . In 2024, OE \~$6.92 B vs market \~$378 B is \~1.8%. Buffett would consider 10% a threshold for value - Netflix is nowhere near that. At its absolute bottom in 2022, the FCF yield briefly was \~1-2%, still not close to 10%. So Netflix fails this "cash cow at a bargain" test badly. Only if one believes 2025 FCF \~$9 B and a much lower future market cap would it approach, but as of now OE yield is too low.

**4. Balance-Sheet Safety:**

-   **Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:** *Historically fail, currently pass.* Netflix carried significant debt while equity was small during its growth phase, leading to high D/E ratios for many years. For instance, in 2017 debt/equity was \~1.8, in 2018 \~2.0, 2019 \~1.95 . This is well above 0.5. However, as profit and equity grew and debt issuance stopped, Netflix's D/E fell to **0.97 in 2021**, **0.69 in 2022**, and **\~0.62 in 2024** . Netflix has **no longer been increasing debt**(total debt peaked around $15.8 B in 2020 and actually ticked down to $14.5 B by 2023) . So by 2022 onward it passes (debt $14.35 B, equity $20.78 B → D/E 0.69 ). But on a 10-year evaluation, it fails because for most of the decade D/E &gt; 1.5. A Buffett purist would likely say fail due to the years of high leverage (used to fund content). Now that Netflix is de-leveraging (net cash positive if you count cash on hand \~$8.4 B vs debt $14.5 B), its balance sheet is much safer. So this criterion flipped from fail to pass in recent years.

-   **Interest Coverage ≥ 3× (EBIT/Interest):** *Pass (recently).* Netflix's ability to cover interest improved dramatically. In 2015-2017, EBIT was quite low relative to interest - for instance 2017 EBIT $0.839 B vs interest \~$0.200 B (coverage \~4.2×, just above 3) - though earlier, 2014 EBIT $0.403 B vs interest \~$0.116 B (\~3.5×). By 2018, coverage slipped with interest rising: EBIT $1.605 B, interest $0.420 B → 3.8×; 2019: 4.16×. Netflix hovered near the threshold during heavy debt growth. From 2020 onward, **interest coverage soared**: 2020 EBIT $4.585 B vs interest $0.767 B = \~5.98× ; 2021: 8.09×; 2022: 7.97×; 2023: 9.94×; 2024: \~14.5× (EBIT $10.42 B, interest $0.719 B) . So Netflix comfortably covers interest now. Over 10 years, it did not always have 3× cushion (it was around 3-4× in mid-2010s, which is *just* at the benchmark). But it never had trouble paying interest. Given the trend, we can say Netflix effectively passes - certainly in the last 5 years it does. Buffett would be pleased to see interest expense trending down and EBIT up; in 2023 Netflix's interest coverage was \~10×, well above 3. This criterion is met in recent years and not egregiously failed in earlier ones (lowest coverage \~3.0-3.5×). So overall, *mostly pass*.

-   **Interest Expense / Operating Income ≤ 15%:** *Pass (recently).* This is a similar metric to coverage. Netflix's interest as % of EBIT was high mid-decade but improved. In 2017, interest $0.200 B on EBIT $0.839 B = 23.8% (too high) . 2018: 26.2%; 2019: 24.0% - these are fails. By 2020, interest $0.767 B on EBIT $4.585 B = 16.7% , just above 15%. 2021: \~12.4% (pass); 2022: 12.5% (pass); 2023: 10.1% (pass); 2024: \~6.9% (pass) . So like coverage, it was marginal a few years but now strongly passes. Long-term debt was a bit high for Buffett's liking pre-2020, but Netflix fixed that trajectory. So by this standard, Netflix only *recently passes*. In judging a "great company" Buffett would likely mark this okay now, with caution that earlier reliance on debt has waned.

-   **Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:** *Fail.* Netflix's current ratio has often been below 1.5, which is Buffett's benchmark for short-term liquidity. Throughout the latter 2010s, Netflix's current ratio was around \~1.4-1.0. Notably, end-2019 current assets $6.18 B vs current liabilities $6.86 B gave **CR = 0.90** (Netflix was technically *capital deficit* short-term, as it relied on continued debt raise rollovers to fund content liabilities). In 2021 it was 0.95 , again below 1.5. Only some years slightly exceeded 1.5 - e.g. 2015 CR 1.54 , 2018 1.49 (almost). By 2022 it improved to 1.17 , 2023 1.12, and 2024 \~1.22 - still below 1.5. So Netflix generally fails this, indicating a tighter working capital position historically. However, one must note Netflix's current liabilities include a large portion of *content liabilities* (money owed to producers) which are often rolled or renegotiated, and not all due at once. Netflix managed fine with CR \~1 because of stable cash inflow and ability to issue debt when needed. But from a Buffett lens, a CR consistently under 1.5 is a risk flag. Netflix's liquidity is adequate (over $8 B cash on hand), but strictly speaking, it doesn't meet the &gt;1.5 test. So this is a fail.

**5. Profitability & Efficiency:**

-   **Gross Margin ≳ 40%:** *Borderline.* Netflix's gross margin has improved into the 40% range recently. In earlier years, GM was lower: mid-30% in 2016-2018, then **38.3% in 2019**, **38.9% in 2020**, crossing 40% in **2021 (41.6%)** . 2022 dipped to 39.4% , then 41.5% in 2023, \~46% in 2024 with the price hikes and content spend moderation . Buffett typically likes businesses with consistently high gross margins (like &gt;40-50%). Netflix only recently achieved that. Given content amortization is a high cost of revenue, Netflix will likely hover around 40-46% GM. By late 2020s it might stabilize &gt;45%. So it's **roughly at the threshold now**. But a Buffett purist would say it didn't average ≥40% over 10 yrs (the 10-year average GM is \~36-37%). So not a clear pass. We'd grade this *almost pass* now, with the trend positive.

-   **Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:** *Fail until 2024.* Netflix's net margin expanded but was &lt;20% for most of the decade. It was single-digit in 2017-2019 (\~4.8%, 7.7%, 9.3% respectively) . It surpassed 10% in 2020 (11.0%), then 17.2% in 2021 . Only in 2024 did net margin exceed 20% (≈22.3% estimated) as revenue grew and costs stabilized. So it does **not meet ≥20% consistently**. One year out of 10 (2024) hit that mark. Buffett ideally wants sustained high net margins. Netflix is trending that way, but historically it falls short - average net margin last 5 yrs \~14%. So this is a *fail* on the 10-year record.

-   **ROE ≥ 15% (10-year avg):** *Pass (on average).* Netflix's return on equity has been boosted by its high asset turnover and profit growth. Early in the decade ROE was low (2015 \~5.5% , 2016 7.0%). But from 2017 onward, ROE climbed above 15% and stayed high: e.g. 2018 23.1%, 2019 24.6%, 2020 25.0%, 2021 32.3% . It dipped in 2022 to 21.6% but that's still above 15%, then 21.9% in 2023, and \~34.9% in 2024 . Over the 10-year span, the **simple average ROE is \~18-20%**, which is excellent and above Buffett's 15% hurdle. Importantly, Netflix had no year with ROE below 5% after 2015, and the trend is solidly above 20%. This indicates that Netflix has utilized shareholder capital very effectively. (Note: Netflix's small equity base in earlier years inflated ROE after profitability rose, which is why ROE jumped so high by 2021). Buffett would likely give a pass here given the strong ROE in recent years and average above criterion.

-   **ROIC/ROTC ≥ 12%:** *Borderline/Pass (recently).* Return on invested capital (including debt) for Netflix was modest in the high-growth phase but improved. In 2015-2017, ROIC was probably mid-single digits (since net income was low relative to big capital). By 2018 ROIC \~6-7%, 2019 \~8-9%. In 2020 it crossed \~12% for the first time (calculation: NOPAT \~$2.5 B on Invested Capital \~$20 B gives \~12.5%). By 2021, ROIC \~19%, 2022 \~14%, 2023 \~15.5%, 2024 \~22% (est.) as earlier computed. So **10-year average ROIC might be just around 12%**(weighted by recent high values). For Buffett's standard "≥12% 10-year avg", Netflix is on the cusp - likely slightly below 12% if including the early years. But trending above it now. Considering momentum, we can say Netflix's core business has high returns on capital *now*. However, strictly the full period average likely a tad under 12%. That said, Buffett might look at the *trajectory* and see a company whose ROIC is accelerating as it scales, which is a plus. Let's call this *borderline; passing in latest years*.

-   **ROA ≥ 6%:** *Pass (recently).* Netflix's return on assets lagged until profitability ramped up. It was &lt;6% prior to 2020 (e.g. 2018 ROA \~4.7%, 2019 5.5%) . In 2020 ROA hit 7.0%, 2021 11.5%, dipped 2022 to 9.2%, back to 11.1% 2023, \~16.2% 2024 . The 10-year average ROA (\~7-8%) barely meets 6%, with last 5 years averaging \~11%. Buffett typically likes ROA &gt;6% consistently - Netflix achieved that only from 2020 onwards. But now it's solidly above, indicating efficient use of assets (many of which are content library). Considering the improvement, Netflix would *pass on current ROA*, though historically it was touch-and-go. We give it a pass given strong recent ROA well above 6%.

-   **Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:** *Pass.* Netflix's depreciation (for streaming tech and office equipment) is very small relative to gross profit. In 2023, depreciation expense was only \~$300 M (estimate) while gross profit was $14 B - about 2%. In prior years, depreciation was perhaps $100-200 M vs multi-billion gross profit (e.g. 2018 depreciation \~$98 M, GP $5.83 B - \~1.7%). Even including amortization of intangibles (virtually all content amortization is in cost of revenue, not "depreciation" line), Netflix's reported "Depreciation & Amortization" was $1.09 B in 2022 , which is 8.8% of 2022 gross profit $12.45 B - still under 10%. This metric is essentially a check if the business is overly capital intensive. Netflix's heavy costs are content (which is expensed through cost of rev, not depreciation), so traditional depreciation (servers, offices) is minor. Thus, by Buffett's standard of "capex demands shouldn't eat too much profit", Netflix **passes easily**. Its property/equipment depreciation is only a few percent of gross profit each year . Even if one considered content amortization, that was \~70% of gross profit in early years (failing), but the criterion typically refers to fixed asset depreciation. So yes, Netflix's actual depreciation (on tech infrastructure) is far below 10% of gross profit, reflecting a scalable platform business.

**Summary:** Out of Buffett's checklist, Netflix meets some standards (book value growth, ROE, improving ROIC, manageable debt now, etc.), but **fails many classic Buffett criteria** due to its historical growth-stage profile. It has been **overvalued by traditional metrics**, lacked steady free cash flow until recently, and had a few earnings hiccups. Its **balance sheet and profitability metrics have improved markedly**, which bodes well. If Buffett were evaluating Netflix 5 years ago, he'd likely reject it for weak cash flow and high debt; today, Netflix is financially stronger but the **valuation is sky-high**, violating his margin of safety principle.

In Buffett terms, Netflix is **not a textbook "great company at a wonderful price."** It's a great franchise with a dominant market position and now solid economics - but investors have usually paid a premium for that growth. Netflix *excels* in growth of intrinsic value (equity per share), high ROE, and now abundant cash generation, which Buffett likes, but it *falls short* on consistent earnings predictability and low multiples. Therefore, Netflix would not fully pass a Buffett-style checklist. Its recent business quality improvements move it closer, but **Buffett's standards (low P/E, long-term predictable earnings, stable high margins) are still only partially met by Netflix** - primarily because of the nature of the industry and Netflix's evolution from cash-burning disruptor to profitable leader.

## **DCF Valuation Analysis**

To estimate Netflix's intrinsic value, we performed a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** analysis under three scenarios: **Conservative, Nominal, and Optimistic**. The key variable is Netflix's **free cash flow (FCF) growth rate**. We use Netflix's historical FCF to inform these rates. Over the past 10 years, Netflix's FCF growth was not linear (it was negative for years then surged positive). Instead, we focus on recent growth: from 2021 to 2023, FCF grew from -$0.132 B to +$6.926 B - an enormous jump (not sustainable annually). We consider:

-   **Lowest FCF growth** in past 10 years: Netflix had some years of negative FCF, but once positive, the slowest increase was from 2022 to 2023, which was +\~327%. That's still very high. For a meaningful "low" rate, we use Netflix's **lowest revenue growth years** (\~6%) as a proxy for minimal FCF growth going forward.

-   **Average FCF growth**: Netflix's 5-year revenue CAGR (2019-2024) was \~13.9%. FCF grew much faster (as it went from negative to positive), but going forward we might expect FCF to grow roughly in line with revenue or a bit faster as margins expand. We choose **15%** as a mid-case FCF growth - a little above recent revenue growth, assuming margin gains.

-   **Highest FCF growth**: At peak, Netflix's FCF essentially doubled or tripled in a year (2023 vs 2022). But that was from a low base. A more normalized "optimistic" scenario might be FCF growing in the high-20s%. We'll use **25%**, which is very bullish (implies doubling FCF \~3 years).

**DCF Assumptions (all cases):** We start with **FCF = $6.93 B** (trailing 12 months as of Q3 2025) as the baseline. We project 5 years of growth at the scenario rate, then a terminal growth of **3%** (reasonable for a mature steady state). We use a **10% discount rate** (reflecting Netflix's cost of equity - perhaps slightly above market average due to still growth tech profile). Share count \~425 M.

-   **Conservative Case:** FCF grows at only **6%** per year (roughly Netflix's slowest revenue growth, seen in 2022-23). This might reflect a scenario of intensifying competition or saturation, where Netflix can barely raise free cash flow above inflation. After 5 years, FCF growth drops to 3% in perpetuity.

    -   *Calculation:* Year 1 FCF $7.349 B, Year 5 FCF $9.268 B. Terminal value = $9.268 B \* (1+0.03)/(0.10-0.03) ≈ $136.5 B. Discounting all cash flows at 10% yields a Present Enterprise Value ≈ **$300 B**. After adjusting for net debt (\~$8.75 B debt minus cash $8.39 B ≈ $0.36 B net debt, negligible), equity value ≈ $300 B. Divide by 425 M shares = **$272 per share intrinsic value**.

    -   **Intrinsic Value (Conservative) ≈ $272/share**. To have a 25% margin of safety, we'd want to buy at **≤ $204/share**.

-   **Nominal (Base) Case:** FCF grows at the **average \~15%** annually for five years (Netflix continues to execute well, growing revenue low-teens and improving margins). Then 3% terminal growth.

    -   *Calc:* Year 5 FCF ≈ $13.94 B. Terminal value ≈ $13.94 B \*1.03/(0.10-0.03) ≈ $205.3 B. PV of 5yr cash flows + terminal ≈ **$500 B** enterprise value. Equity \~ $500 B (net debt minor). Per share = **$393**.

    -   **Intrinsic Value (Nominal) \~ $393/share**. With 25% margin of safety, a **buy price \~ $295** or lower.

-   **Optimistic Case:** FCF grows at a rapid **25%** for five years (e.g., Netflix cracks down on sharing, advertising booms, emerging markets drive growth - FCF nearly triples in 5 years). Terminal growth still 3%.

    -   *Calc:* Year 5 FCF \~$21.17 B. Terminal value \~$311 B. PV enterprise value ≈ **$733 B**. Equity value per share = **$576**.

    -   **Intrinsic Value (Optimistic) \~ $576/share**. 25% MOS target buy price \~ **$432**.

These scenarios highlight how sensitive value is to growth assumptions. Currently (late 2025), Netflix stock is around $1,200/share - far above even our optimistic intrinsic value. This suggests the market is pricing in extraordinarily high growth (or future strategic success beyond these cash flows). In fact, to justify $1,200, one would have to assume \~40%+ FCF growth for many years or a dramatically lower discount rate - not in our conservative range.

Total PV of cash flows ≈ $6.68+$6.42+$6.19+$5.97+$5.77 = **$31.03 B**. PV of terminal = $136.5/(1.10^5) ≈ $84.7 B. Sum = **$115.7 B** enterprise. Equity \~ $115.4 B (subtract net $0.36 B debt). Per share = $115.4 B/425 = **$271.6** (round \~$272). This matches our earlier $272 figure.

Thus, under reasonable assumptions, Netflix's **intrinsic value is arguably in the few-hundred-dollars range** per share - nowhere near the current $1,200. The *margin of safety buy prices* we found ($204, $295, $432 for the cases) are far below the market price, suggesting Netflix is priced for a much more optimistic future than our scenarios.

It's instructive to note how growth affects value: optimistic case ($576) is more than double conservative ($272). Yet even that optimistic value is half the current price, implying the market expects *decades* of strong growth, new revenue streams, or perhaps lower discount rates. Buffett would likely consider Netflix *overvalued at current price* relative to any conservative DCF, reinforcing why he hasn't bought such stocks historically.

**Basis Recap:**

-   **Conservative:** Used \~6% FCF growth (Netflix's lowest recent growth, akin to modest price increases and minimal sub gains). Outcome \~$272/share intrinsic, implying current stock &gt;4× overvalued. Buy at \~$204 for 25% MOS.

-   **Nominal/Base:** \~15% FCF growth (Netflix continues moderate success). Value \~$393, still 3× below market. Would need stock around $295 to have margin of safety.

Buffett famously demands a significant margin of safety - Netflix currently fails that test. In essence, Netflix is a high-quality business (especially now with real cash flows) but at an excessive price relative to conservative intrinsic worth.
