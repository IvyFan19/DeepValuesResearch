# **Circle (CRCL) - Comprehensive Financial Analysis**

## **Background Research**

### **Business Model and Main Income Sources**

Circle Internet Group (NYSE: CRCL) is a fintech company best known as the issuer of the USD Coin (USDC) stablecoin - a cryptocurrency pegged 1:1 to the U.S. dollar. Circle's platform enables businesses to use digital currencies and blockchain for payments and commerce worldwide. The company's **business model** centers on **issuing and managing stablecoins** (primarily USDC, and a smaller Euro Coin EURC) and providing related financial infrastructure (APIs, wallets, and payment services) for developers and enterprises. Circle earns revenue mainly from the **interest income on the reserves** backing its stablecoins. Those reserves (equal to the amount of USDC in circulation) are invested in short-term U.S. Treasury bills and cash deposits to ensure the peg. In 2024, fully **99% of Circle's total revenue** came from interest earned on USDC reserve assets, while only about 1% came from transaction fees and other product revenue. This heavy reliance on reserve interest is illustrated in the revenue breakdown pie chart below.

-   *Figure: Circle's Revenue Mix (2024) - Interest on USDC Reserves (\~99%) vs Other Fees (\~1%)*

Circle essentially operates like a wholesale money-market fund: it invests USDC reserves in safe instruments and earns interest yield. It shares a portion of that yield with partners (notably Coinbase) as distribution costs, and retains the rest as income. The **other 1% of revenue** comes from fees on services such as **Circle Mint** (an institutional on/off-ramp for minting/redeeming USDC) which has \~1,800 customers and charges tiered redemption fees, and from new products (developer APIs, tokenization services, etc.) which are currently in early stages. Going forward, Circle is trying to diversify revenue by offering payment and treasury services (e.g. the Circle Payments Network launched in 2025) to reduce its dependence on interest income. Nonetheless, as of the latest data, interest on stablecoin reserves remains the dominant income source for Circle.

### **Customers, Usage, and Units in Circulation**

Circle's direct **customers** are largely businesses, fintechs, exchanges, and financial institutions that integrate USDC or use Circle's platform. As of Q1 2025, the company reported **over 1,800 institutional customers** for its Circle Mint and liquidity services. These include banks, exchanges, payment providers, and corporate treasuries that directly issue or redeem USDC through Circle. In addition, Circle caters to developers with APIs and digital wallet infrastructure (over 10 million on-chain wallets have been deployed using Circle's wallet services). It's worth noting that the end-users of USDC - i.e. traders, crypto users, and businesses transacting in USDC - number far more, but those are typically served via intermediaries like exchanges rather than directly by Circle.

In terms of **units in circulation**, Circle's flagship USDC stablecoin has grown dramatically. Over **$60 billion USDC** have been issued cumulatively, and as of mid-2025 the **current USDC supply is around $61 billion** - an all-time high, even surpassing its prior peak in 2021. (This figure reflects a resurgence after a dip in 2022-2023, when USDC's market cap fell to \~$25-30B following a crypto market downturn and the temporary depegging incident in March 2023.) USDC is the second-largest stablecoin with roughly **24-26% market share** of a \~$250B total stablecoin market, trailing only Tether (USDT). Circle also has a much smaller Euro-denominated stablecoin (EURC) and has introduced a tokenized money market fund (USYC), but those are nascent. For now, **USDC circulation** is the key unit metric: its growth drives Circle's revenue. In 2024, USDC average circulation was about $32B (up 36% YoY), and recent regulatory momentum has helped USDC supply rebound to over $60B. Each additional USDC in circulation contributes reserve interest income for Circle, so **growth in USDC units** is directly tied to revenue growth (as long as interest rates remain favorable).

### **Short-term and Long-term Headwinds & Risks**

Circle faces several **headwinds and risks** in both the short run and long run. In the **short term**, a key variable is the **interest rate environment** and macroeconomic conditions. Because \~99% of revenue comes from interest on reserves, **falling interest rates would sharply reduce Circle's income**. The U.S. Federal Reserve is widely expected to cut rates from recent highs over the next couple of years; if rates revert to near-zero levels, Circle's interest income (and thus revenue) could decline to a fraction of current levels, posing a significant temporary headwind. Additionally, crypto market volatility or **reduced demand for stablecoins** could temporarily shrink USDC circulation - for example, during the 2022-2023 crypto bear market and the Silicon Valley Bank incident, USDC's market cap dropped by tens of billions (users switched to rival stablecoins like USDT or cashed out). Such events can cause **short-term revenue dips** for Circle. Other short-term factors include **regulatory uncertainty** - while progress is being made on a U.S. stablecoin bill, any delays or unfavorable interim regulations could create uncertainty and limit USDC's growth in certain markets in the short run. Geopolitical events or credit stresses in banking (e.g. another bank holding Circle's reserves facing trouble) are additional transient risks that could momentarily impact Circle's business (e.g. the SVB collapse in March 2023 temporarily broke USDC's $1.00 peg and required swift intervention).

Looking at the **long term**, **regulatory and competitive factors** dominate the risk landscape. On the regulatory side, there is momentum for a U.S. federal stablecoin framework (e.g. the proposed **GENIUS Act** which would impose reserve transparency and licensing requirements). Circle has welcomed regulation and is even applying for a national digital currency bank charter to solidify compliance. However, a **permanent risk** is that new laws might **limit private stablecoin issuers** or introduce a central bank digital currency (CBDC) that could displace private stablecoins. If, for example, the Federal Reserve or U.S. Treasury were to issue a digital dollar, or if only banks were allowed to issue stablecoins, Circle's business model could be fundamentally challenged in the long run. Another long-term risk is **competitive pressure** from larger players: currently USDC's main rival is Tether's USDT (with \~60%+ stablecoin market share) which is less transparent but deeply entrenched. Circle's ability to overtake or coexist with Tether will influence its market share trajectory. Moreover, major tech or finance companies might issue their own stablecoins (for instance, **traditional banks** could launch stablecoins once clear regulations are in place). Such competition could **permanently cap Circle's growth** or erode its market share if not navigated well.

There are also industry-level secular factors: **blockchain adoption** in mainstream finance is still evolving. If crypto and stablecoins see broad acceptance in payments (a positive scenario for Circle), it could greatly expand Circle's long-term opportunity. Conversely, if stablecoins remain niche or are supplanted by alternative technologies (like instant payment systems or CBDCs), Circle's growth could stagnate long-term. Finally, Circle's extreme revenue concentration in one product (USDC) and one type of income (interest) is itself a risk - the company will need to diversify its business model over time to ensure resilience. Management is aware of this and is investing in payments networks, custody services, and tokenization, but these **new ventures may or may not gain traction** to offset potential declines in the core stablecoin revenue. In summary, **short-term risks** include interest rate swings, crypto market volatility, and regulatory delays, while **long-term risks** involve regulatory regime changes (or CBDCs), competitive displacement, and the need to diversify beyond a single revenue source.

### **Cost Structure and Expenses**

Circle's cost structure comprises both **variable costs** tied to USDC circulation and **fixed costs** related to operations and growth. The **most significant cost** by far is the **"distribution & transaction costs"** - primarily the revenue share paid out to distribution partners (chiefly Coinbase) for USDC issuance. Under a partnership agreement (the **Centre Consortium deal**), Coinbase historically received **50% of the interest income** on USDC that it helped distribute. In 2024, these distribution and transaction costs were about **$1.01 billion**, up \~40% from $720 million in 2023. This expense scales with the size of USDC reserves and interest rates - essentially it's a **variable cost proportional to revenue**. Indeed, in Q1 2025, as reserve revenue jumped 55% YoY, distribution costs jumped 68% YoY, reflecting increased payouts to Coinbase and other partners. Apart from partner revenue sharing, other variable costs include transaction processing fees and any interest Circle might pay on client balances (though currently USDC holders do not earn interest from Circle, some corporate clients might negotiate terms). Overall, **cost of revenue** (mostly distributions) was $1.28B in 2024, about 76% of revenue - indicating the high variable payout.

On the **fixed cost** side, **operating expenses** include personnel, product development (R&D), compliance, marketing, and general administrative costs. Circle's headcount is about 900 employees, and related **labor expenses** fall into SG&A and R&D. In 2024, **Selling, General & Administrative (SG&A)** expenses were \~$182 million, up from $157M in 2023. Circle has been growing its team (particularly compliance and engineering) as it expands globally and develops new services, so fixed costs have trended upward albeit at a much lower rate than revenue. **Advertising & marketing**spending specifically isn't broken out publicly, but it is likely a modest portion of SG&A - Circle's growth has been driven more by industry adoption than consumer advertising, though it does engage in business development and policy advocacy (which might be part of SG&A). Another component in operating expenses is **technology and development**("Other Operating Expenses"), which in 2024 was a net $-4.25M (possibly due to capitalization of software costs or one-time adjustments), whereas in 2022 it was +$57M (indicating heavy investment and perhaps some asset write-downs). This volatility suggests some **one-time charges** (e.g. impairments or restructuring) hit the books in earlier years.

**Cost per unit:** For each $1 of USDC issued, Circle must hold $1 in reserve assets. The interest earned on that $1 is split roughly 50/50 with Coinbase (under the old arrangement), meaning effectively **$0.50 of each $1 of revenue "unit" goes to distribution cost**. The remaining $0.50 covers operating expenses and profit. In 2024, Circle's **gross margin** was \~23.6%, down from 29.4% in 2023, reflecting higher distribution cost ratio as more USDC was distributed via partners. Fixed costs (SG&A, etc.) were about $228M in 2024, which is relatively small compared to revenue, giving Circle a high operating leverage. When revenue surged in 2022-2023, **operating income turned positive** and scaled quickly (Circle had a 17% operating margin in 2023). Conversely, in low-revenue scenarios (like 2020-2021), fixed costs rendered the company deeply unprofitable. For example, in 2020 when revenue was only $15M, fixed costs of \~$60M+ led to large losses.

**Trends in costs:** During **economic expansions and high-rate environments**, Circle's revenue rises and its distribution costs also rise (since they are proportional), but fixed costs remain relatively stable - yielding high incremental margins. This was seen in 2022-2023: revenue jumped \~**87% in 2023**, while SG&A rose only \~13%, allowing operating profit to grow significantly. In **downturns or low-rate conditions**, the opposite occurs: revenue plunges but fixed costs cannot adjust as fast, leading to net losses. For instance, in the low-rate era of 2020-2021, Circle's revenue was negligible yet it still carried significant staff and development costs, resulting in heavy losses (over $500M loss in 2020). Management has shown willingness to control costs (they reduced SG&A slightly in 2023 when revenue growth paused, perhaps through efficiency or lower marketing). However, many costs (compliance, engineering) are **strategic and long-term**, so Circle likely won't cut those drastically even if revenue dips - it would instead absorb short-term losses to continue growing the ecosystem. Notably, **labor and compliance costs** could rise in differing regulatory conditions - e.g. if licensed as a national bank, Circle will incur new compliance overhead, a necessary fixed cost increase. **In summary**, Circle's **most significant cost** is the revenue-sharing with partners (variable), and its **fixed cost base** (personnel, technology) is meaningful but far smaller than revenue in boom times. This cost base has grown as Circle scales, but far more slowly than revenue, providing operating leverage. The **current cost structure favors high-volume, high-rate environments**; in low-rate or low-volume scenarios, costs (especially fixed) will outpace revenue, compressing margins or causing losses.

### **Intrinsic Valuation - Warren Buffett-Style Analysis**

To estimate Circle's **intrinsic value** per share, we perform a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** analysis focusing on the company's *owner's earnings* (free cash flow) generation. We use assumptions consistent with a Warren Buffett-style conservative approach: a discount rate between 10-15%, a **margin of safety** of 30-50%, and a low terminal growth rate (&lt;3%). We also consider two scenarios: one using the **lowest growth rate** Circle has seen in the past (as a conservative case), and another using an **average growth rate** from the past (as a more optimistic case). It's important to note that Circle's historical growth rates have been extremely high (revenue grew &gt;800% in 2021 and &gt;87% in 2022), which are not sustainable long-term. Thus, for intrinsic value, we will constrain growth to much lower levels.

**Baseline figures:** In 2024, Circle's free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capex) was about **$300 million**. This is our starting owner's earnings for the model. Circle's shares outstanding after the IPO are \~**227.6 million**.

**Scenario 1 - Conservative (Low Growth):** Assume that going forward, Circle's free cash flow **does not grow at all for several years** - this could happen if interest rates decline (reducing revenue) while USDC volume grows modestly, essentially offsetting each other. For modeling, we take **0% FCF growth for 5 years**, then a **2% terminal growth** (very low, below GDP growth, reflecting a stabilized mature business). Using a **12% discount rate** (midpoint of Buffett's typical 10-15%, to account for crypto-sector risk we lean higher), the DCF yields an intrinsic equity value of roughly **$2.68 billion**, which is about **$11.8 per share**. Applying a 30% margin of safety, the **MOS buy price** would be around **$8.3 per share**. Even without the margin of safety (i.e. intrinsic $11.8), this is far below the current market price ($185). In fact, at $185, the stock is trading at over **15× the conservative intrinsic value** by this estimation. This suggests that if one believes in near-zero growth (or heavy mean reversion of earnings), Circle's stock is significantly overvalued at present.

**Scenario 2 - Optimistic (Moderate/Average Growth):** For a less draconian scenario, consider that Circle manages to grow free cash flows at a decent clip for a while. Historically, ignoring outlier spikes, one might use an "average" growth - however, as discussed, a literal average of past years (285%!) is not meaningful. Instead, let's assume **FCF grows \~30% annually for the next 5 years** (this could correspond to USDC adoption increasing and interest rates staying relatively high in the near term), then growth slows to **5% for the following 5 years**, and then a **terminal growth of 2.5%** thereafter. Using the same 12% discount rate, this DCF scenario produces an intrinsic value of about **$10.0 billion**for equity, or roughly **$43.7 per share**. With a typical Buffett margin of safety (say 33%), a prudent entry price would be **\~$29 per share**. Even *without* any margin of safety, the intrinsic value ($44) is only about 24% of the current $185 stock price. This indicates the market is pricing in far more aggressive growth or success than this "moderate" scenario. In other words, even under quite optimistic growth (30% for 5 years is very rapid growth for a \~$1.7B revenue base), the stock would appear richly priced relative to intrinsic value.

For additional context, we can **solve for the discount rate** that would equate the current price to the DCF value (i.e. the implied expected return). Using the optimistic growth assumptions above, the discount rate needed to justify \~$185 per share is only about **5-8%**. Specifically, we found it to be around **5.1%** under scenario 2 (and \~8.3% under an even more bullish alternate growth pattern) - meaning at the current price, the **market is implying only \~5-8% annual return** if those growth assumptions hold. For a stock with considerable regulatory and crypto-sector risk, a 5-8% expected return is quite low. By Buffett's standards (who often uses 10% as a hurdle), that would not be attractive. Essentially, **Circle's market valuation (&gt;$40B) reflects extremely optimistic expectations** - investors are likely pricing in sustained high interest margins and continuing surges in USDC usage, as well as successful expansion into banking and payments.

From a **Buffett-style intrinsic value perspective**, Circle's stock looks **overvalued at present**. The conservative scenario suggests an intrinsic value in the single or low-double digits per share, whereas the current market price is an order of magnitude higher. Even giving Circle credit for robust growth, our DCF came nowhere close to $185. It's worth acknowledging that Circle is in a nascent industry and could defy low-growth assumptions - if one assumed, say, 50% growth for a decade, the valuations would be higher (and indeed, some crypto bulls might implicitly be modeling something like that). But prudence dictates using modest growth in long-term forecasts. The intrinsic value analysis underscores that **Circle's current valuation embeds tremendous growth and profitability improvements that are far from guaranteed**. A value investor would likely demand a substantial margin of safety given the uncertainties (technology shifts, regulatory outcomes, competition, etc.). Applying, for instance, a 50% margin of safety to even the optimistic case ($44/2 = $22) still yields a buy target of only the low-$20s.

In conclusion, by **Warren Buffett's method of intrinsic value**, Circle does not appear to offer a margin of safety at its current trading price. The stock would need to decline significantly (or fundamentals exceed our high-end growth scenario by a wide margin) to meet typical value-investing criteria. At present, **the expected return, given the lofty price, is well below the \~10-15% range that value investors seek**. In fact, our analysis implies the current price assumes a cost of capital more akin to 5-8%, which is more in line with a stable utility than a risky fintech - a disconnect that suggests caution.

Also, Circle's recent deals (Visa using USDC, partnerships in Brazil, Philippines, etc.) hint at a real use-case expansion beyond crypto trading. This is the bullish narrative: Circle as an *internet-era Western Union + Central Bank* hybrid.

However, one cannot ignore **Tether (USDT)** as a formidable competitor; it's larger, and despite transparency issues, it's deeply embedded in crypto markets. If Tether remains dominant or if governments lean on banks to issue their own coins, Circle's growth could underwhelm. There's also **technological risk**: stablecoins today run on blockchains (Ethereum, etc.), but future innovations (like interoperable CBDCs or improvements in banking rails) could reduce the need for private stablecoins. Circle will have to continuously innovate to stay relevant (its work on cross-chain transfer protocol CCTP, for instance, is aimed at making USDC more ubiquitous across blockchains).

**Management quality** is a strong point - Allaire and team have thus far done the right things: prioritized transparency, navigated crises well (they recovered from the SPAC failure and SVB incident effectively), and captured opportunities (IPO timing was fortuitous, raising capital right when the market was receptive). Their incentives are aligned with shareholders, and they have credibility in both crypto and regulatory circles. I believe this will help Circle manage the upcoming regulatory integration smoothly (which is crucial, as missteps in compliance could be fatal in this business).

**Financially, Circle is sound** in the short to medium term - it has cash to operate and no debt pressure. The key variable is revenue sustainability. If interest rates drop, one might see revenue fall significantly in 2025 vs 2024 (for example, if Fed funds went to 2%, that could cut interest income by more than half assuming similar USDC balances). Circle's profitability could shrink or even vanish in a low-rate scenario unless USDC volume compensates by growing dramatically. That is a real possibility to consider for 2024-2025, given the macro outlook. An investor must be comfortable that either (a) rates will stay higher for longer (which seems contrary to current market predictions of cuts in 2024), or (b) USDC volume will keep growing fast enough to offset rate compression (which is uncertain - USDC faces competition and is already quite large).

**In conclusion,** Circle as a company has an attractive business model with significant long-term potential, but **its stock at current levels appears very overvalued by traditional metrics and DCF analysis**. The market is pricing in perfection - sustained growth, high margins, favorable regulation - leaving no margin of safety. From a value investing perspective, one would likely **wait for a substantial pullback** or a big jump in fundamentals (e.g. USDC volume doubling and interest margins holding) before considering it reasonably valued. For now, the **stock carries a high risk of valuation contraction** if growth slows or if any negative surprise occurs. Circle is a great company in the making, but not necessarily a great stock at $40+ billion market cap. It's a classic case of a **hyped IPO where enthusiasm outran intrinsic value**.

Investors optimistic about Circle's future should keep an eye on a few things: **USDC market share trends**, the **progress of the stablecoin legislation and bank charter**, and **interest rate forecasts**. Any sign that USDC is losing ground (to USDT or a future PayPal stablecoin or CBDC) would hurt the bull thesis. Conversely, if Circle can maintain \~20-30% growth in USDC circulation and manage to only slowly taper the interest yield (via maybe diversifying into services that generate fees), then the high valuation might eventually be earned through rapid earnings growth. But that's a lot of ifs. Given what we know, **Circle the company is solid and growing, but Circle the stock is priced beyond perfection.**
