## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Nike's latest quarterly results (Fiscal 2026 Q2 ended Nov 30, 2025) showed **revenue of $12.4 billion**, up 1% year-over-year (flat on a currency-neutral basis). Growth was driven by strong **wholesale** demand (+8%), while **NIKE Direct** sales fell (-8% reported) as **digital** sales declined 14%. **Gross margin** contracted to 40.6%, down 3 percentage points, mainly due to higher North America tariffs. Selling and administrative expenses were flat (+1%), with a 13% jump in marketing ("demand creation") spend offset by lower operating overhead. Net income was **$0.79 billion**, down 32%, and diluted EPS was **$0.53**, also down 32% year-over-year.

Regionally, **North America** led performance (Q2 North America revenue +9%), while **Greater China** and APLA (Asia Pacific & Latin America) saw declines. Notably, **Converse** revenue dropped 30% as the Chuck Taylor line struggled. Despite the earnings decline, Nike beat analysts' expectations for Q2, aided by better-than-expected revenue and margin; however, concerns over gross margin pressure (from tariffs and higher costs) tempered market reaction.

Nike management highlighted that the company is "making progress" on its strategic priorities and navigating a "dynamic environment" by realigning teams, managing inventory, and focusing on a "consumer-direct offense." Inventory was down 3% from last year (to $7.7 billion) as Nike worked through excess stock. The company returned **$598 million** to shareholders in the quarter via dividends (Nike has **24 consecutive years** of dividend increases). Nike did **not repurchase shares** in Q2, preserving cash given the uncertain environment. Overall, the quarter reflected Nike's resilience - modest top-line growth and inventory improvement - but also ongoing margin and China headwinds.

## **Business Model**

**Nike, Inc.** is the world's leading designer and marketer of athletic footwear, apparel, and equipment. The company's business model revolves around creating innovative sports products, marketing them with powerful branding, and selling through both wholesale partners and direct-to-consumer channels (Nike-owned retail and e-commerce). Nike earns revenue primarily from **footwear** (which accounts for about two-thirds of sales) and **apparel** (around one-third) . In fiscal 2024, Nike Brand footwear revenue was $33.4 billion and apparel revenue was $13.8 billion , together comprising over 96% of Nike's $51.4 billion total revenue . Equipment (sports gear and accessories) is a smaller segment (\~4% of sales). The pie chart below shows Nike's revenue breakdown by product category (footwear vs. apparel vs. equipment):

Nike generates income through product sales across various sports and lifestyle categories. **Footwear** (e.g. running, basketball, Jordan brand sneakers) is the largest revenue source (\~65% of FY2024 sales) . **Apparel** (e.g. athletic clothing, jerseys) contributes around 27%, while **equipment** (balls, bags, etc.) and the Converse subsidiary make up the remainder. Geographically, Nike sells worldwide, with North America its biggest market (41% of FY2023 sales), followed by EMEA (26%), Greater China (17%), and APLA (16%) . Nike's distribution spans **wholesale** (selling to sports retailers, department stores, etc.) and **NIKE Direct** (owned retail stores and **Nike.com**). Notably, Nike has been shifting toward a more direct-to-consumer model - in FY2024, Nike Direct made up \~44% of brand revenue , reflecting the strategy to grow its e-commerce and own stores for higher margins and closer consumer connection.

**Customers:** Nike's end-customers are tens of millions of consumers ranging from professional athletes to everyday fitness enthusiasts and casual "sneakerhead" collectors. Rather than a few large clients, Nike's reach is broad - it sells through \~30,000 retail accounts worldwide in addition to its own direct channels. Nike has built a massive **membership base** of over **300 million Nike Members** in its loyalty program , which drives repeat engagement and sales. These members access exclusive products and content via Nike's apps (e.g. SNKRS, Nike Running Club). Nike's scale is illustrated by its production volume: the company manufactures **800+ million pairs of shoes per year** to meet global demand. No single wholesale customer represents over 10% of sales - Nike's wholesale clients include sporting goods chains (e.g. Dick's Sporting Goods), footwear retailers (e.g. Foot Locker, JD Sports), and boutiques. This diversified customer base and Nike's direct sales help reduce dependence on any one partner.

**Value Proposition & Moat:** Nike's moat is primarily its **brand strength and consumer loyalty**. The Nike "Swoosh" is one of the most recognized logos in the world, associated with top athletic performance and style. This brand equity allows Nike to command premium pricing and shelf space. Nike reinforces its brand through **endorsements** (e.g. Michael Jordan, LeBron James, Serena Williams) and high-profile sponsorships (official uniforms for the NBA, NFL, Olympic teams, etc.), creating an aspirational halo effect. Another moat aspect is Nike's **innovation and R&D** - it invests heavily in product development (e.g. Nike Air cushioning, Flyknit fabric) to deliver performance advantages and constantly refresh its lineup. Nike holds over 5,000 patents and introduces new sneaker models yearly, making its product portfolio difficult to replicate.

Nike's sheer **scale and supply chain** also provide competitive advantage. It leverages a worldwide manufacturing network (primarily in Vietnam, China, and Indonesia) and efficient distribution to produce at high volume and relatively low unit cost. With $51+ billion revenue, Nike can spread fixed costs (design, marketing) over an enormous sales base. Furthermore, Nike's **direct consumer relationships** and data (hundreds of millions of users on its apps) form a virtuous cycle - member data informs product design and demand planning, which in turn improves inventory efficiency and customer experience . In summary, Nike's moat comes from a powerful global brand, continuous innovation, massive scale, and a deep connection with its consumer community.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

Despite its strengths, Nike faces several short-term and long-term headwinds:

-   **Short-term/Macro Risks:** In the near term, Nike is navigating **economic volatility and shifting consumer demand.** High inflation and slower economic growth in key markets are pressuring consumers' discretionary spending on sneakers and apparel. For instance, in 2023 Nike saw softer demand in Greater China due in part to pandemic after-effects and cautious consumer sentiment. **Foreign exchange fluctuations** have also been a headwind - the strong US dollar in 2024 reduced Nike's reported international revenues (flat currency-neutral growth turned into only +1% reported in the latest quarter). Additionally, **supply chain disruptions** and elevated logistics costs are short-term risks. Nike's FY2022 was impacted by factory closures in Vietnam (COVID-related) and shipping bottlenecks, causing delayed product flows . While much of that delayed revenue was later recaptured , the company noted that elevated freight costs and transit times could persist for a few quarters. Another short-term challenge has been **excess inventory** coming out of the pandemic - Nike had to aggressively manage down overstock (which reached +44% year-over-year in mid-2022) through discounting, which hurt margins in recent periods. The latest data show inventory is back under control (-3% YoY), but clearing past glut came at the cost of lower margins.

-   **Long-term/Structural Risks:** A key longer-term risk is **intense competition** in the athletic apparel/footwear industry. Nike competes with Adidas, Puma, Under Armour, Anta, and newcomers like Lululemon (footwear) across global markets. Competitors sometimes out-innovate or capture trends (e.g. Adidas' athleisure surge in mid-2010s) which could erode Nike's market share. Another structural threat is the **shifting retail landscape** - Nike's move toward direct-to-consumer improves margins but means cutting back some wholesale accounts (it has pulled product from undifferentiated retailers) . This strategy risks alienating longtime retail partners and could drive those partners to promote competing brands. Moreover, direct retail makes Nike responsible for store operations and e-commerce logistics, areas where execution missteps or cost overruns can occur.

Nike also faces **geopolitical and regulatory risks**. For example, **tariffs and trade tensions** are a persistent concern given Nike's reliance on Asian manufacturing. The recent drop in gross margin was partly due to higher import tariffs in North America. Future changes in trade policy (e.g. higher US tariffs on Chinese/Vietnamese goods) could raise Nike's costs significantly or force supply chain shifts. In China, Nike must navigate political sensitivities - it faced consumer boycotts in 2021 after statements on Xinjiang cotton, highlighting reputational risk in that key market. **Currency devaluation**, particularly in emerging markets, is another ongoing risk for a company that earns over half its revenue outside the US.

Furthermore, **sustainability and labor** issues pose long-term challenges. Nike's history includes controversies over labor conditions at contract factories. While it has made improvements, any future scandals in its supply chain could damage the brand. Environmental pressures are rising too - from both consumers and regulators - to reduce waste and carbon footprint. Nike has set sustainability goals (e.g. using recycled materials, renewable energy in production), but meeting these without impacting cost or quality is an execution risk. Lastly, **market saturation** in developed economies is a potential headwind. The athletic footwear market is maturing in North America and Western Europe, so Nike's long-term growth depends on emerging markets (China, India, etc.) and expanding categories (women's apparel, casual wear). If Nike fails to win in these areas, its \~10% historical growth could slow.

In summary, while Nike is fundamentally strong, it must contend with near-term macro challenges (inflation, inventory, supply chain) and longer-term strategic risks (competition, geopolitics, sustainability) that could temporarily or permanently impact the business.

## **Cost Structure**

Nike's cost structure comprises both variable costs (tied to production volume) and fixed costs (overhead to design, market, and distribute its products). **Cost of goods sold** - mainly the expenses to manufacture footwear and apparel - is Nike's largest cost, totaling $26.5 billion in FY2025 (approximately 57% of revenue). These production costs are variable, including raw materials (rubber, foam, cotton, polyester), labor paid to contract factories, and inbound freight. On a per unit basis, Nike's **cost per pair of shoes** might be on the order of \~$30 (though it varies widely by model), which it sells at an average wholesale price around \~$60-70 and a retail price of \~$100+. Nike's gross margin of \~44-46% historically indicates a roughly similar cost vs. sell price ratio .

**Labor Expenses:** Nike itself directly employs about **83,000 people** (as of 2023) in corporate, retail, and distribution roles. Most of Nike's factory labor is **outsourced** - the company contracts with hundreds of factories (primarily in Asia) that collectively employ over 500,000 workers to produce Nike goods (these workers are not on Nike's payroll). Nike's own wage costs are concentrated in design, marketing, headquarters staff (in Oregon and international offices), and Nike-owned retail store employees. Selling, General & Administrative (SG&A) expense was $14.1 billion in FY2025, which includes corporate salaries. A rough estimate is that Nike's average SG&A per employee is around $170k (though this also covers advertising spend, office leases, etc., not purely wages). Notably, Nike has to invest in **talent** such as designers, technologists, and marketers to maintain its innovation and brand - these fixed labor costs keep growing moderately each year (SG&A was up 1% in the latest quarter despite cost-cutting, indicating stickiness in wage and overhead costs).

**Marketing ("Demand Creation"):** Advertising and promotion is a major cost for Nike. In FY2025, Nike spent **$3.8 billion** on demand creation (about 8.2% of revenue). This includes endorsement deals (Nike pays athletes like LeBron James an estimated $30+ million per year), advertising campaigns, social media content, and retail brand presentations. Marketing is partly variable (launching a new product or entering a new market will incur extra spend) but also has a large fixed component (long-term endorsement contracts, annual brand campaigns). During economic downturns, Nike can trim some variable marketing (as it did in 2020), but fundamentally the company maintains a significant marketing budget to protect brand strength. For instance, in the latest quarter Q2 FY26, Nike's demand creation expense rose **13%** to $1.3 billion despite lower sales - a deliberate investment in brand and sports marketing ahead of events like the FIFA World Cup and Olympics.

**Manufacturing & Sourcing:** While Nike outsources production, it incurs **cost of sales** for payments to manufacturers, inbound shipping, and customs. These costs are mostly variable per unit. Nike has been pressured by increases here - e.g. higher **freight and logistics costs** cut into margins in 2021-22, and tariffs added several hundred basis points of cost in 2025. Nike mitigates these by optimizing its supply chain (e.g. using ocean freight over air when possible, relocating production to lower-tariff countries). Still, about 55-60 cents of each $1 of sales goes to cost of product and freight.

**Overhead & Fixed Costs:** Key fixed costs for Nike include **innovation/R&D**, corporate facilities, and IT systems. Nike doesn't break out R&D as a separate line, but it's embedded in SG&A (design and innovation teams). The company's **"Project Create"** and other initiatives to improve automation in manufacturing involve upfront investment. Depreciation of its offices, distribution centers, and store build-outs is also a fixed cost (Nike had \~$0.8 billion depreciation in FY2024) . As Nike grows its digital business, spending on technology (e.g. Nike apps, e-commerce platform, data analytics) has become a larger fixed cost - though with benefits of scale as digital sales grow.

Which costs are most significant? In terms of proportion of sales: **cost of goods** (\~55-60% of sales) is biggest, followed by **SG&A** (\~27% of sales in FY2025). Within SG&A, marketing is a large portion (about one-third of SG&A). **Product sourcing costs** (materials, factory labor) dominate COGS, and these have been rising due to input inflation and wage increases in Asia. For example, higher **cotton and oil prices** (affecting polyester, foam) in 2022-2023 raised Nike's input costs. Nike's **gross profit margin** trend reflects these dynamics: it peaked around 46% in FY2015-FY2016 when input costs were favorable, dipped to \~43% in FY2018 (in part due to clearing inventory and a stronger dollar), recovered to 45-46% in FY2021, then fell to \~42.7% in FY2025 amid higher freight and tariffs . In recessionary conditions, Nike often sees margin pressure as it must discount inventory (as happened in 2020 and 2022) - effectively, variable costs don't drop as fast as revenues, and fixed costs like endorsements still must be paid, compressing profitability. Conversely, in boom times or when Nike has hot products, it enjoys full-price sales that boost margins.

Overall, Nike's cost structure has a high proportion of variable cost (which gives it flexibility - e.g. if sales drop, it orders fewer shoes from factories, reducing COGS accordingly). But its significant fixed costs - especially in marketing and corporate overhead - mean that sustained sales declines can hurt profitability until Nike rightsizes those expenses. Nike has historically managed to keep SG&A growth slower than revenue growth (driving operating margin expansion), except in rare weak periods. The company's sheer scale helps: for instance, Nike can leverage its $3+ billion annual marketing spend across all its product lines and regions, a scale rivals struggle to match, giving Nike more bang-for-buck in brand reach.

## **Management & Governance**

Nike's leadership and governance have a strong legacy influence from its founders combined with seasoned industry executives:

-   **Executive Team:** Nike's CEO is **John Donahoe**, who took the helm in January 2020. Donahoe was formerly CEO of eBay and brings digital expertise, aligning with Nike's push into e-commerce and data analytics. Under his leadership, Nike has accelerated its direct-to-consumer strategy and technology investments. The broader executive team includes veterans like **Matthew Friend (CFO)** and **Heidi O'Neill (President of Consumer & Marketplace)**, as well as newer hires from technology backgrounds. Notably, **Phil Knight** - Nike's co-founder - remains involved as Chairman Emeritus, and his son **Travis Knight** sits on the Board of Directors, reflecting the founding family's ongoing influence. Nike's **Board of Directors** is chaired by **Mark Parker** (Nike's former CEO from 2006-2020), indicating continuity in strategic vision. The board also includes prominent independent directors such as Apple's Tim Cook and basketball legend John Thompson Jr. (until 2020), bringing diverse expertise.

-   **Historical Leadership:** Nike was co-founded in 1964 by Phil Knight and Bill Bowerman. Phil Knight served as CEO and chairman for decades, instilling Nike's culture of innovation and aggressive marketing. In the late 1990s and 2000s, leadership transitioned to professional managers like William Perez and then Mark Parker. Parker, a lifelong Nike employee and product expert, led Nike through a period of tremendous growth and was known for fostering Nike's design and innovation talent. This history of long-tenured insider leadership has meant consistency in Nike's strategy. The current CEO Donahoe is only the 4th chief executive in Nike's 50+ year history - a testament to stability.

-   **Management Performance & Integrity:** Nike's management has a track record of setting ambitious growth targets and largely meeting them. Former CEO Mark Parker famously drove Nike to hit $50 billion in revenue by 2020 (Nike got close, reaching $51B in FY2022). Management tends to be **transparent about challenges** - for example, in 2022 Nike leadership openly discussed inventory issues and their plan to normalize stock levels over a couple of quarters. Analysts generally consider Nike's guidance credible; the company does not often surprise with hidden problems. One incident testing transparency was the **2021 inventory reselling scandal**: a VP (Ann Hebert) resigned after it was revealed her son was reselling large volumes of Nike shoes using her employee discount. Nike acted swiftly to address this conflict, which reflects a culture that does not tolerate breaches of trust that could harm the brand's reputation.

During earnings calls, Nike's management is known for emphasizing long-term strategy (innovation, brand, digital) and often **downplaying short-term hiccups** (like quarterly margin dips) in favor of the big picture. This candor has generally kept investor confidence high. Questions about integrity in Nike's history mainly relate to its supply chain labor practices (in the 1990s) - which was more of a structural issue than management hiding problems. Since then, Nike has published annual sustainability reports and factory audits to maintain accountability.

The current management team's tenure varies: Donahoe is relatively new (3 years), while others like COO Andy Campion have been with Nike 15+ years. This mix ensures fresh perspective with deep company knowledge. Management bench depth is strong - Nike often promotes from within for key roles (e.g. design chief John Hoke started at Nike in 1992 and is now Chief Design Officer). There have been no serious questions of management integrity or accounting red flags in recent years.

-   **Governance & Shareholder Alignment:** Nike's governance structure features a dual-class share system - Class A shares (held largely by the Knight family) have 10x voting power of Class B (public) shares. This gives the Knight family effective control (\~77% of voting power) despite owning about only \~17% of total equity . While this means ordinary shareholders have limited say, it has allowed Nike's leadership to pursue long-term initiatives without fear of activist interference. On the positive side, the founding Knight family and management are **highly invested in Nike's success** - Phil Knight (through his holding company Swoosh LLC) is the largest shareholder, and many executives receive substantial stock-based compensation. As of 2025, insiders (management and directors) collectively own roughly 1.5% of Class B shares plus all Class A shares, aligning their wealth with company performance.

Nike has consistently returned cash to shareholders via **dividends and buybacks**, indicating management's confidence and alignment with investors. For example, Nike has increased its dividend **for 24 consecutive years**, and the current CEO continued the prior $18B share repurchase authorization. Management usually buys back stock opportunistically during dips - in FY2023, when Nike's share price fell \~30%, the company repurchased $4.3B of stock . This suggests they view Nike's stock as a good long-term investment and aim to "buy low" to benefit remaining shareholders.

In terms of accountability, Nike's board has a majority of independent directors and standard committees (audit, compensation, etc.). There have been initiatives by shareholders on issues like sustainability and diversity, to which Nike has responded with commitments (e.g. tying some executive compensation to diversity goals). Given the Knight family control, outside investors have limited ability to enforce changes via proxy votes, but Nike's performance track record has generally kept serious governance criticism at bay. Overall, Nike's management is seen as **experienced, capable, and shareholder-friendly**, with the caveat that the dual-class structure places ultimate control with the founding family.

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns**

Nike's shareholder base is a mix of the founding Knight family, institutional investors, and retail holders:

-   **Top Shareholders:** The single largest owner is co-founder **Phil Knight**, who (along with his son Travis Knight) controls the entire Class A share block (approximately **\~16.5% of total shares**, but with \~77% voting power) . This effectively gives the Knights control over major decisions - for instance, they can elect a majority of the board. Among public shareholders, the largest are passive institutional investors. As of 2025, **Vanguard Group** and **BlackRock** each hold around 7-8% of Nike's Class B common stock (together owning roughly 130 million shares) . Other top holders include State Street (\~4%) and various mutual funds. These institutions generally vote with management on routine proposals. Notably, because of the dual-class structure, even if all Class B shareholders united, they cannot outvote the Class A bloc on contested issues. Nike's long-term investors have largely accepted this structure due to Nike's strong performance.

-   **Voting & Governance Topics:** In recent shareholder meetings, significant proposals have included advisory votes on executive compensation and requests for reports on environmental or social issues. For example, some shareholders have pushed for greater transparency on supply chain labor practices and diversity. The Knight-controlled votes have consistently ensured management's recommendations prevail - for instance, executive pay packages (which include substantial stock grants) have passed with over 95% approval in 2023. The Knight family tends to **support management** and the status quo; there has been no instance of them voting against a management-supported proposal publicly. Top institutional shareholders like Vanguard and BlackRock, which prioritize governance principles, have given Nike relatively high corporate governance scores - Nike has an independent board majority and eliminates supermajority voting requirements (except the dual-class share, which these institutions typically oppose in principle but tolerate in Nike's case).

One notable topic is the **shareholder proposal on political contributions disclosure** which has come up - large investors have increasingly backed calls for companies to report their lobbying and political spending. In Nike's 2022 meeting, such a proposal received around one-third support (BlackRock signaled support) but failed due to Knight's votes against. Top shareholders overall have not mounted any activist campaigns - Nike's performance and capital returns have kept them satisfied. Essentially, Nike's primary shareholders (the Knights and passive funds) are long-term oriented, and there hasn't been a contentious proxy battle or hostile action in Nike's history.

-   **Capital Return Policies:** Nike has a **consistent track record of returning cash to shareholders**. The company pays a quarterly dividend that has grown at \~11% annual rate over the past 5 years . In FY2024, Nike paid out $2.17 billion in dividends , and in November 2025 it announced a further +3% increase to the dividend (now $0.41 per quarter) . This marks the 24th straight year of dividend increases - a sign of management's commitment to sharing profits. Even during the 2020 pandemic, Nike maintained and raised its dividend, underscoring confidence in its cash flow.

Nike also engages in substantial **share buybacks**. In June 2022, the board approved a new **$18 billion, four-year share repurchase program** . Under this plan, Nike bought back $4.3B in FY2024 and $3.0B in FY2025 , retiring roughly 79 million shares (about 5% of shares outstanding). As of May 2025, approximately **$10.7 billion** remained authorized in this buyback program . Nike's buybacks over the past decade have meaningfully reduced the share count - for example, in 2015 Nike had \~1.36 billion shares, and by 2025 it has \~1.19 billion shares outstanding (after accounting for a 2-for-1 split in 2015). This \~13% net reduction in share count boosts EPS growth for continuing shareholders.

Nike typically repurchases more shares when the stock price is under pressure - for instance, in the second half of calendar 2022, Nike accelerated buybacks as the stock dipped to multi-year lows around $85. According to filings, **$8 billion** of the $18B authorization was used through late 2025 , leaving plenty of capacity. Management has indicated they view buybacks as an efficient way to return excess cash after funding growth investments and dividends .

Overall, Nike's capital return approach balances **dividends (which appeal to long-term investors)** and **opportunistic buybacks (which signal confidence in the stock's value)**. Top institutional shareholders are supportive of this strategy - for example, when Nike's earnings calls highlight share repurchases, analysts often positively note the offset to dilution from employee stock plans. One could say Nike's shareholder relations are strong: The company communicates clearly, meets or exceeds most financial targets, and rewards shareholders with both income and capital gains. The only governance drawback from some investors' perspective is the entrenched control of the Knight family via dual-class stock - but given Nike's success, this has not been a hindrance to investment for the vast majority of shareholders.

## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

Warren Buffett-style "great company" criteria can be used to evaluate Nike's fundamentals. Below is a checklist of key criteria and how Nike measures up:

1.  **Valuation Discipline:**

    -   **P/E ≤ 15:** *Fail.* Nike's price-to-earnings ratio has seldom been at or below 15 in the past decade. It's typically traded at a premium - for example \~25-30x earnings in recent years (31.0 in 2023, 24.5 in 2024) as investors price in growth. Even during the 2020 market crash, Nike's P/E only briefly touched \~20. Thus, Nike's current P/E (\~30) is double the classic value threshold of 15.

    -   **PEG &lt; 1:** *Fail.* Nike's PEG ratio (P/E divided by earnings growth rate) is well above 1. Using a \~10% earnings growth estimate, Nike's PEG is about 2.5-3.0 . Historically, slow growth years pushed the PEG even higher. This indicates the stock is not a bargain relative to growth.

    -   **P/B ≤ 1.5:** *Fail.* Nike's price-to-book is far above 1.5. As of FY2024, P/B is \~7.6 (market cap \~$145B vs book equity $19B). Nike has always traded at a rich multiple of book value due to its brand intangible - it has never been near 1.5x book in the last 20+ years.

    -   **Margin of Safety (Buy ≥ 25% below intrinsic):** *Fail (at current price).* Our DCF analysis (next section) suggests Nike's intrinsic value in a conservative case might be around $63 per share, versus the current \~$60 - only a 5% margin. A 25% discount ($47) has rarely been seen except during crises (e.g. 2020 lows). At present, Nike's stock does not offer a 25% MOS relative to reasonable intrinsic valuations; it's priced for perfection.

2.  **Cash Generation & Allocation:**

    -   **Positive Free Cash Flow & FCF/Revenue &gt; 5%:** *Pass.* Nike has generated positive free cash flow each of the past 10 years. Even in downturns like FY2020, FCF was +$1.76B . Over the decade, FCF as a percent of revenue averaged \~8-10%. In FY2024, FCF margin was 11.5% (nearly $6B on $51.4B sales), well above the 5% benchmark. Nike also allocates cash prudently - balancing reinvestment ($0.8B capex p.a.) with hefty buybacks and a growing dividend.

    -   **Return on Retained Earnings ≥ 12%:** *Fail.* We calculate Nike's return on retained earnings by comparing incremental earnings to cumulative earnings retained. From 2015-2024, Nike retained about $30B of profits (after dividends) and grew annual net income by \~$2.43B . That's roughly an 8% return on retained earnings (2.43/30), below the 12% threshold. This indicates that not all retained capital translated into high incremental returns - some was used for buybacks (which boost EPS but not net income) and growth initiatives that take time to pay off.

3.  **Growth & Predictability:**

    -   **EPS Record - Positive & rising every year for 10 yrs:** *Fail.* Nike's EPS has been positive each year (no losses), but not strictly rising every year. EPS dipped in FY2018 (due to the one-time tax hit) and again in FY2020 (pandemic). While the trend is upward over the decade (EPS doubled from $1.85 to $3.73), it doesn't meet the "every year" increase criterion .

    -   **Book Value per Share 10yr CAGR ≥ 10%, no year of decline:** *Fail.* Nike's book value per share grew at \~4% CAGR (from $7.00 in 2014 to about $12.03 in 2024) and actually declined in some years. Notably, FY2018 saw equity drop due to tax law changes, and heavy buybacks in FY2019-FY2020 reduced book value as well. So, Nike falls short on this metric.

    -   **Owner's Earnings positive every year & OE/Market Cap ≥ 10%:** *Partially Fail.* Nike's owner's earnings (approximately free cash flow) were positive each year - it has never had negative OE in the past decade. However, Nike's OE yield is far below 10%. At the current market cap \~$85B, the FCF yield is \~3-4%. Even at trough prices, it hasn't hit 10%. Thus, while Nike generates solid owner earnings, the stock's high valuation keeps the yield low for value investors.

4.  **Balance-Sheet Safety:**

    -   **Debt-to-Equity &lt; 0.5:** *Fail.* Nike's D/E ratio is 0.76 as of 2024 (and was above 1.0 in 2020). Nike took on debt in recent years, pushing D/E above the conservative 0.5 mark. That said, Nike's net debt is low given its cash, and the absolute debt load ($8.9B) is modest relative to EBITDA. But strictly on book D/E, Nike fails.

    -   **Interest Coverage ≥ 3x:** *Pass.* Nike's EBIT covers interest expense many times over. In fact, Nike had net interest *income* in 2023-2025 (more interest earned than paid) . Even when it had \~$100M net interest expense, EBIT was $6-7B - a coverage of \~60x. Nike easily satisfies this criterion.

    -   **Interest Exp ≤ 15% of Operating Income:** *Pass.* Nike's interest expense has been negligible - e.g. $205M in 2022 vs $6.86B op income (\~3%) . In recent years, interest expense is near zero (or negative net). So Nike passes with flying colors.

    -   **Current Ratio &gt; 1.5:** *Pass.* Nike's current ratio has ranged from \~2.1 to 3.4 over the decade . At \~2.7 in 2024, it's well above 1.5. Nike carries a healthy buffer of current assets (cash, inventory, receivables) relative to liabilities, reflecting strong liquidity.

5.  **Profitability & Efficiency:**

    -   **Gross Margin ≳ 40%:** *Pass.* Nike consistently posts gross margins in the mid-40s% . Even its low point (FY2025 at \~42.7%) was above 40%. This high margin signifies strong pricing power and brand premium.

    -   **Net Profit Margin ≳ 20%:** *Fail.* Nike's net margin is around half the 20% target. It averages \~11-12% in good years . A 20% net margin is more typical of a software or niche luxury business; Nike, with cost of sales and marketing, doesn't reach that. Its best net margin was \~13% in 2022.

    -   **ROE ≥ 15% (10-year avg):** *Pass.* Nike's ROE 10-year average is well above 15%. Even excluding the unusual 55% in 2021, Nike routinely delivered ROE in the 25-35% range. This reflects efficient use of equity (partly due to share buybacks shrinking equity). By Buffett's standard, Nike's high ROE is a positive indicator.

    -   **ROIC ≥ 12% (10-year avg):** *Pass.* Nike's return on invested capital (using NOPAT) has averaged \~20%+. Even in weaker years it stayed near or above 12%. For instance, 2024 ROIC \~21%, 2025 dipped to \~12-13% by our estimate - still at the threshold . Over 10 years, Nike comfortably clears 12% ROIC average, evidencing a durable competitive advantage.

    -   **ROA ≥ 6%:** *Pass.* Nike's return on assets has been \~9-17% most years , easily above 6%. Only in FY2018 and FY2025 did ROA fall \~8-9%, which is still above the benchmark. This indicates Nike's total asset base (including cash) generates healthy profit.

    -   **Depreciation / Gross Profit ≤ 10%:** *Pass.* Nike's depreciation is relatively small compared to gross profit. In 2024, depreciation $0.796B vs gross profit $22.887B = \~3.5% . Historically it's been under 5%. Low depreciation as a fraction of GP suggests a less capital-intensive business model - a positive for investors because it means more of gross profit converts to cash.

**Summary:** Nike meets many of the quality metrics (high margins, ROE/ROIC, solid FCF, interest coverage) that define a "great company." It falls short on classic value metrics like low P/E, PEG &lt;1, or net margin, indicating it's a great company perhaps, but usually at a great (expensive) price. Nike's strong returns on capital and brand moat are evident, but an investor following Buffett's deep value approach might wait for a more attractive entry price. At current \~$60/share levels, Nike does not provide a margin of safety by those strict standards - it's more of a long-term compounder one might pay a premium for. In essence, Nike is a **wonderful business** that rarely trades at a wonderful price for value buyers.

## **DCF Analysis**

To estimate Nike's intrinsic value, we performed a Discounted Cash Flow analysis under three scenarios: **No-Growth**, **Conservative Growth**, and **Nominal Growth**. We use Nike's **free cash flow** as the basis (trailing twelve-month FCF is about $3.27 billion, though FY2024 was higher at \~$5.9B). For discount rate, we assume Nike's cost of equity \~**10%**(reflecting a large-cap with low debt). The terminal value is calculated using the Gordon Growth Model at the same perpetual growth as the scenario.

**1. No-Growth Case:**

Assume Nike's FCF stays flat going forward (0% growth). This is a "steady state" scenario - Nike neither grows nor declines, simply maintaining current cash flows. Using FY2024 FCF of \~$5.9B as a normalized base (Nike's FY2025 FCF was lower due to one-time inventory actions, so we use a slightly higher base reflecting a mid-cycle level), the present value can be estimated as a perpetuity: Value = FCF / discount rate = $5.9B / 0.10 = **$59.0 billion**. On a per-share basis (using \~1.19B shares), that's about **$49.60 per share** intrinsic value. With a 30% margin of safety, a buy price would be **$34.70** or lower. This no-growth valuation suggests that if Nike never grows again, the stock is worth roughly $50 - notably below the current market price (around $60), implying the market is pricing in growth.

**2. Conservative Growth Case:**

For a conservative scenario, we use Nike's lowest plausible long-term growth rate. Over the past 10 years, revenue grew \~6% CAGR, but net income had down years. We'll assume FCF grows at a **2%** annual rate (roughly in line with inflation - essentially very modest real growth). This might reflect ongoing challenges or a maturing business. Starting again with \~$5.9B FCF, and growing at 2% in perpetuity, the DCF formula value = FCF \* (1+g) / (r - g) = $5.9B \* 1.02 / (0.10 - 0.02) = $6.018B / 0.08 = **$75.2 billion**. Per share intrinsic value = **$63.20**. A 30% margin of safety would call for a price **≤ $44.25**. Under this low-growth case, Nike stock (at \~$60) is slightly undervalued relative to the $63 intrinsic value - but not by much. It suggests mid-single-digit growth is almost priced in.

**3. Nominal Growth Case:**

For a base "nominal" scenario, we use Nike's historical average FCF growth. Free cash flow has roughly tracked earnings; excluding anomalies, Nike's EPS/FCF grew 8-10% annually in the last decade. However, to be a bit cautious we'll use a **5%** growth rate (Nike's FY2024 revenue was flat, but long-term perhaps 5% growth in FCF is reasonable). Using g = 5%, value = $5.9B \* 1.05 / (0.10 - 0.05) = $6.195B / 0.05 = **$123.9 billion**. Per share this is about **$104.10**intrinsic value. With a 30% MOS, the buy target would be \*\*$72.90\*\* or below. This scenario indicates Nike's upside if it can resume mid-single-digit or higher growth: intrinsically around $100+, significantly above today's price. Essentially, if you believe Nike will continue its long-term growth trajectory (\~5-8% annually) for years to come, the stock appears undervalued in this DCF framework.

**Key DCF Assumptions:** We used a 10% discount rate (which may be slightly high given Nike's 8% WACC, but we chose it for conservatism). We treated FY2024 as a representative cash flow year; using the depressed FY2025 FCF ($2.5B) would dramatically lower the valuation, but that assumes the worst conditions persist (which is unlikely). The perpetual growth rates of 0%, 2%, 5% bracket a reasonable range of Nike outcomes - essentially zero real growth vs. modest vs. moderate. Notably, Nike's brand and competitive advantages suggest it could very well achieve higher than 5% growth (analysts forecast \~8-10% EPS growth over next 5 years). If we used 8%, the DCF value would skyrocket (over $200B). Conversely, if growth stagnated or turned slightly negative (a risk if the brand falters or margins compress), the value would tilt toward the no-growth \~$59B or lower.

**DCF Results Summary:**

-   **No Growth (0%):** \~$59.0B intrinsic value = **$49.6/share**. *Buy Below \~$34*.

-   **Conservative (2%):** \~$75.2B intrinsic = **$63.2/share**. *Buy Below \~$44*.

-   **Nominal (5%):** \~$123.9B intrinsic = **$104.1/share**. *Buy Below \~$72*.

Nike's current market cap (\~$85-90B at $60/share) implies roughly a 3-4% growth expectation baked in. The no-growth case shows downside (intrinsic $50 vs market $60), whereas the nominal case shows significant upside. Given Nike's strong brand and past performance, the conservative 2% growth scenario is arguably pessimistic unless the world economy stagnates. Thus, one could argue Nike is somewhat undervalued relative to its likely growth (making it reasonable for a long-term investor), but it does not offer a wide margin of safety for a strict value investor.
