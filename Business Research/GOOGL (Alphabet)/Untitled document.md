## **Background Research**

### **1. Business Model and Revenue Streams**

**Alphabet's Core Business:** Alphabet Inc. is the parent holding company of Google and various other ventures ("Other Bets"). Google is by far the largest component, contributing essentially all of Alphabet's revenue and profit. Alphabet generates **about three-quarters of its revenue from advertising** across Google's platforms . The company's mission-critical products - **Google Search**, **YouTube**, **Google Maps**, **Google Play (Android)**, **Chrome**, **Gmail**, and more - funnel billions of users into its ecosystem, which Alphabet then monetizes primarily via **paid advertising** placements and, to a lesser extent, **cloud services** and other fees. Alphabet reports results in three segments: **Google Services**, **Google Cloud**, and **Other Bets** .

**Main Sources of Income:** Within Google Services, the largest revenue drivers are: **Search advertising**, **YouTube advertising**, and the Google Network (ads on partner sites via AdSense) - collectively these make up the vast majority of revenue. Smaller but growing sources include **Google Play app sales, YouTube subscriptions (Premium, YouTube TV), hardware (Pixel devices, Nest), and other paid services**. Google Cloud generates revenue from cloud infrastructure and platform services (GCP) and Google Workspace (productivity SaaS), and has recently turned profitable . Other Bets (e.g. Waymo autonomous driving, Verily life sciences) generate minimal revenue (&lt;1%) and are **operating-loss-making experimental units**. As shown, **Google's advertising businesses (Search ads, YouTube ads, Google Network ads)** comprised roughly **75-80%** of total revenue . In the first nine months of 2024, Google Services (which includes all ad revenue plus Android/Play, hardware, etc.) contributed **\~87% of revenue** while Google Cloud was about **12%**, and Other Bets just \~0.5% . This mix illustrates Alphabet's heavy reliance on advertising. Notably, Google Search (including ads on Google search and other Google-owned properties) alone accounted for **56-57% of revenue** . YouTube advertising is around 10%, and the Google Network (ads on third-party sites via AdSense/AdMob) \~9%. Non-ad "Google other" revenues - like app sales, YouTube subscriptions, and hardware - contributed \~11-12%. Google Cloud's 12% share is growing rapidly .

**Customer & User Base:** Alphabet serves **multiple customer groups**. Its primary "customers" for revenue are **advertisers** - **over 1 million businesses** use Google Ads to market products/services . This includes everyone from small businesses bidding on local search terms to large enterprises running global YouTube campaigns. On the user side, Google's platforms have unparalleled scale: **Google Search handles \~90% of global search queries** , with billions of users. **YouTube** has over **2 billion logged-in monthly users** and generates &gt;**1 billion hours** of video views daily. **Android**, Google's mobile OS, is active on **\~3 billion devices worldwide** (with Google Play services). **Gmail** has \~1.8 billion active users, and **Chrome** browser has billions of users. **Google Maps** and **Drive** each have over 1 billion users. These massive free user bases attract advertisers. In Cloud, Google serves \~**several hundred thousand customers** (from startups to large enterprises) for cloud computing and productivity tools, including notable clients in retail, healthcare, finance, etc. (e.g. *Snap*, *Ford*, *Unilever* as public references). Google Cloud's enterprise customer count isn't disclosed, but Google's broad reach and partnerships suggest a growing client roster in the thousands. In summary, Alphabet's economic moat derives from billions of consumer users (who use its products for free) and millions of advertisers and developers (who pay for ads or cloud services) .

### **2. Short-term and Long-term Headwinds & Risks**

Alphabet faces a variety of **headwinds and risk factors** - some transient (short-term) and some structural (long-term):

-   **Short-Term/Cyclical Factors:** Alphabet's revenue is sensitive to the economic cycle and advertising demand. **Macroeconomic slowdowns** or recessions can **temporarily reduce advertising budgets**, directly impacting Google's core ad revenue . For instance, in late 2022 and early 2023, fears of economic slowdown led to softer ad spending growth, which weighed on Alphabet's results . Currency exchange rate fluctuations are another short-term headwind - a stronger U.S. dollar can reduce reported international revenues (over 53% of Google revenues are from outside the US) . Additionally, **seasonal advertising patterns** (Q4 is typically strong; Q1 weaker) create short-term volatility. **Inflation and interest rate increases** can tighten marketing budgets as companies become cautious, as seen in 2022-2023 when ad growth slowed to single digits . On the cost side, Google has faced **higher expenses** in certain quarters due to legal settlements and one-time charges (e.g. a legal settlement charge impacted operating expenses in Q2 2025) . These are largely short-term in nature.

-   **Long-Term/Structural Factors:** Alphabet's longer-term risks center on **regulatory and competitive threats** and potential shifts in technology. The most prominent is **regulatory and antitrust risk**. Alphabet is under scrutiny by governments globally for its dominance in search and advertising. The U.S. DOJ and several state attorneys general have active antitrust lawsuits accusing Google of anti-competitive behavior in search (e.g. paying to be default on browsers) and ad tech . In the EU, Google has faced large fines and may be forced to **change business practices or even consider structural remedies** (extreme case: spinning off parts of the ad business or Chrome) . New regulations (e.g. the EU's Digital Markets Act) could limit how Google leverages data and preferencing of its own services. These actions pose a **permanent risk** of fines or business model adjustments that could reduce Alphabet's profitability. Another long-term risk is **technological disruption and competition**: e.g. the rise of AI-powered search alternatives. The emergence of OpenAI's ChatGPT and Microsoft's new AI-integrated **Bing** search is a potential threat to Google's core search franchise . If users shift to AI chatbots for information instead of traditional search, Google's search ad model could be impacted. Alphabet is responding by infusing AI (e.g. Bard, AI snapshots in search), but the competitive landscape is intensifying. Similarly, **competition in digital advertising**from Meta (Facebook/Instagram's Reels competing with YouTube, etc.), Amazon (growing search ad business on Amazon.com), TikTok (stealing YouTube's user attention), and others is a persistent long-term threat . In Cloud, Google is a distant \#3 behind AWS (Amazon) and Azure (Microsoft); these rivals are larger and aggressively investing, pressuring Google Cloud's margins and market share. **Privacy changes** are another structural challenge: moves like Apple's iOS privacy restrictions (ATT framework) have made targeted advertising harder, which could permanently raise hurdles for Google's ad effectiveness on mobile devices. Over the long haul, **shifts in consumer behavior** (e.g. younger users favoring TikTok or other platforms for search-like queries) could erode Google's dominance if not addressed.

In summary, **short-term risks** include macroeconomic dips in ad spend, FX swings, and one-off costs, whereas **long-term risks** revolve around regulatory actions that could **alter Alphabet's business model** or impose fines, as well as **competitive/technological disruptions** in search, video, and cloud that could **permanently impact growth**. Alphabet's management openly acknowledges these challenges in filings, noting that increased regulation or an inability to adapt to new tech (AI, voice search, etc.) could materially affect the business . So far, the company's wide moat (global scale, strong AI/data capabilities) has helped it weather competition, but investors remain watchful of these enduring headwinds.

### **3. Cost Structure: Fixed vs Variable Costs**

Alphabet's cost structure includes both substantial fixed costs and significant variable expenses. Key cost components:

-   **Cost of Revenue:** This includes traffic acquisition costs (TAC) - the **payments Google makes to partner websites and device makers (like Apple) to obtain traffic**. TAC is a **variable cost** that rises with search query volume and revenues. In 2024, Google's TAC was over $45 billion (roughly \~22% of ad revenues) . Other cost of revenue elements are content acquisition costs (e.g. payouts to YouTube content creators via revenue sharing) and **infrastructure costs** to operate Google's services (data center electricity, depreciation of servers). Many infrastructure costs (depreciation, data center operations) are more **fixed (or stepped)** - Google must invest in massive data centers and undersea cables upfront. In 2025, Google is ramping CapEx to \~$85B for AI capacity , which will add to fixed cost base.

-   **Operating Expenses:** Alphabet's OpEx is dominated by **R&D and headcount**. **Research & Development (R&D)**was about $39.5B in 2022 - Google is consistently one of the world's top R&D spenders . R&D (which includes salaries for engineers working on search algorithms, self-driving cars, etc.) is largely a **fixed cost** (driven by strategic investment decisions rather than short-term revenue changes). **Sales & Marketing** expenses (around $22B in 2022) include advertising Google's products and personnel costs for sales teams - these have some variable aspect, but also significant fixed elements (brand marketing campaigns, etc.). **General & Administrative** (G&A) includes corporate overhead, legal, finance, etc. - largely fixed. In total, Google's operating costs have grown but management has achieved some efficiency: for example, G&A actually *decreased* in 2024 vs 2023, indicating cost control .

-   **Labor** is a major component: Alphabet had **\~183,000 employees** at end of 2024 . **Compensation** (especially in R&D) is high and includes stock-based compensation. These are relatively fixed commitments in the short run. In early 2023 Alphabet announced it would **cut \~12,000 jobs** (\~6% of workforce) to rein in costs , showing it can adjust labor in downturns. However, core talent (AI researchers, etc.) is retained, so labor costs don't drop proportionally with revenue dips.

-   **Significant Cost Areas:** The most significant costs for Alphabet are *infrastructure depreciation* and TAC within cost of revenue, and *personnel/R&D* within operating expenses. In 2024, Cost of Revenue was $146.3B (which includes \~$51B TAC and data center costs), while total operating expenses (R&D, S&M, G&A) were about $93B . Thus, **traffic acquisition (partner payouts)** and **engineer salaries** are two largest cost buckets.

-   **Cost Trends:** Over time, Google's costs have grown but with improving efficiency. **Gross margin** has improved as revenue growth outpaced TAC growth in recent years . **R&D as % of revenue** was \~15% in early 2025, slightly down from \~16-18% a few years ago . During economic booms (e.g. 2021), Google expanded headcount rapidly, but in leaner times (2022-23) it slowed hiring and even implemented layoffs. In economic downturns, S&M and G&A can be trimmed (as seen by G&A drop in 2024 ), and management can moderate CapEx growth. For instance, capital spending was tempered in 2020's pandemic onset, then ramped up again in 2021 when growth returned. **Overall, fixed costs (like data centers and R&D) give Google high operating leverage**, which boosts margins in good times but can hurt margins if revenue stalls. Fortunately, Google's revenue has a strong long-term uptrend, allowing it to absorb rising costs. Below is a trend of Google's operating margin over the past decade, reflecting these dynamics:

*Figure 2: Alphabet's Operating Margin Trend (2015-2024). After dipping in 2017-2018 due to higher costs and one-time charges (tax change in 2017), operating margins rebounded to \~30%+ in recent years as revenue growth outpaced expense growth .*

In summary, Alphabet's **variable costs** include TAC, content payouts, and certain sales/marketing expenses; its **fixed costs** include massive R&D investments, infrastructure depreciation, and overhead. The **most significant costs** are TAC (payments to partners like Apple, which was \~$19B in 2022) and personnel. These costs have generally **trended upward**, but Google has maintained healthy margins due to scale. In downturns, it can pull back hiring, negotiate TAC (though TAC is relatively inelastic due to contracts), and delay CapEx, demonstrating some cost flexibility to protect profitability in different economic conditions .

### **4. Management Team and Governance**

**Current Executive Team:** Alphabet is led by **Sundar Pichai**, CEO of both Alphabet Inc. and Google LLC. Pichai, a longtime Google executive, became Google's CEO in 2015 and Alphabet's CEO in 2019 (when founders stepped back) . **Ruth Porat** has been Chief Financial Officer (CFO) since 2015, overseeing disciplined financial strategy. Other key executives include **Prabhakar Raghavan** (SVP, Google - in charge of Search, Assistant, Ads), **Philipp Schindler** (Chief Business Officer, overseeing ad sales), and **Thomas Kurian** (CEO of Google Cloud). The **Board of Directors** is chaired by **John Hennessy** (former Stanford University president). Notably, co-founders **Larry Page** and **Sergey Brin** remain on the board and are controlling shareholders (through super-voting Class B shares), but they no longer hold day-to-day roles . Other board members include venture capitalist **Ram Shriram**, former Google CEO/Chairman **Eric Schmidt** (who served 2001-2017, now an advisor), and other industry leaders.

**Historical Leadership:** Google was founded in 1998 by Larry Page and Sergey Brin. They led the company through its early growth, with Eric Schmidt as CEO for a decade (2001-2011) to provide "adult supervision." In 2015, the founders created the Alphabet holding structure and appointed Sundar Pichai as Google CEO (later Alphabet CEO) . Page and Brin stepped back from executive duties in 2019, though they occasionally advise on projects (e.g. reportedly on AI in 2023). Google's management culture emphasizes technology and long-term bets (the "moonshots"). Many early execs (Omid Kordestani, Marissa Mayer, etc.) have departed over time, but Google has maintained stability at the top with Pichai and Porat now in their roles \~8-10 years.

**Management Performance & Integrity:** Generally, Alphabet's management is regarded as **competent and conservative** in financial reporting. Under Pichai, the company has **met or exceeded many of its strategic goals**: e.g. expanding Google's core advertising, achieving profitability in Cloud (Q4 2023 was first profit for Cloud segment), and instilling more cost discipline (e.g. 2023 layoffs to improve efficiency). Pichai is known for a steady, low-key style; he tends to underpromise and focus on product improvements rather than hype. The management has not been known to **hide problems from investors**; indeed, they routinely disclose key metrics and challenges (for example, they broke out YouTube and Cloud revenues in 2020 to increase transparency ). There have been **no major financial scandals or restatements** under current management. However, some have criticized management for being slow in certain areas (e.g. initially lagging in AI chatbot release, which Pichai acknowledged and then quickly pivoted the company to focus on AI in late 2022). On earnings calls, Pichai and Porat answer analysts' questions in a straightforward if sometimes guarded manner - Porat in particular is known for her careful, no-nonsense commentary, often reiterating that the company will invest for long-term while minding costs. Analysts sometimes press about revenue outlook or capex, and management typically provides reasonable detail (though Google historically avoided giving official guidance).

**Tenure and Reputation:** Pichai has been with Google since 2004 (initially leading Chrome) and CEO for 8 years; Porat joined in 2015 from Morgan Stanley. This long tenure suggests stability. No significant **integrity issues** have been raised about Alphabet's financial management - though the company has faced **criticism on other fronts** (e.g. handling of user privacy, internal workplace issues). One notable incident was a 2018 controversy where Google initially did not disclose a Google+ data breach; while troubling, it was not related to financial honesty but rather PR handling. Overall, Alphabet's management is considered **shareholder-friendly in governance but also maintains founder control** (via dual-class stock).

**Value Alignment with Shareholders:** The co-founders Page and Brin have the most skin in the game - they together control over **51% of voting power** through Class B shares (though owning \~11% of economic interest) . This alignment means they benefit from long-term stock appreciation, but it also insulates management from external shareholder pressure. Sundar Pichai's compensation has a large stock component (he was awarded significant stock grants, e.g. $200M+ in 2019); while his annual pay is high, it vests based on Alphabet's stock performance relative to peers . Thus, Pichai will "earn more from growing the business than from salary" if the stock does well. Many top execs have regularly scheduled stock sales (to diversify their wealth), but there have been **no alarming insider dumps** signaling lack of confidence. In fact, insider ownership (founders and early execs) remains substantial. During short-term stock declines, we haven't seen executives making conspicuous open-market purchases - likely because they already receive stock grants and have preset trading plans. The Board did approve a huge share buyback, effectively returning cash to shareholders (more on that in section 6), which indicates management believes in the stock's value.

In terms of following through on promises, management has generally delivered: for example, they promised to improve Cloud profitability - Google Cloud's operating loss narrowed and turned to a profit by 2023 . They said they'd moderate expense growth in 2023, and indeed reduced headcount growth and excess costs. However, management can be cautious in commitments - they often highlight "long term" opportunities in AI, etc., without overpromising near-term results. There's no evidence that **management hides problems**; they disclose challenges like ad market softness or increased capex frankly in calls . Integrity-wise, the main question mark some raise is the dual-class stock structure: Page and Brin's control means outside shareholders have little say in governance matters (the founders can overrule any vote). There was a **shareholder proposal** to give each share equal voting rights, which management (with founders' votes) rejected . Some investors also questioned large stock-based pay packages (Pichai's) and whether management is too slow to address workforce size - but these are more strategic differences than integrity issues. Broadly, Alphabet's management is **focused on long-term value creation** (investing in AI, quantum, etc.) and appears **ethically driven** (the famous "Don't be evil" motto may be retired, but the ethos of not overtly abusing power persists). They do face internal criticisms (e.g. employee protests over defense contracts in 2018 led to canceling a Pentagon AI project), indicating management does respond to concerns of stakeholders beyond just shareholders.

**Board and Governance:** The board includes independent directors with significant industry experience (e.g. finance, academia, tech). However, with the founders' voting control, external shareholders have limited ability to effect changes like splitting the CEO/Chairman role or forcing dividend initiation. Notably, in 2023 Alphabet finally initiated a dividend (after long avoiding one), perhaps reflecting management's confidence in cash flows and willingness to return capital (discussed next). Management's overall alignment with shareholders is good in terms of economic incentives (large equity stakes), but governance is somewhat insular due to the controlled company structure.

### **5. Top Shareholders and Voting Power**

Alphabet's stock structure consists of Class A shares (GOOGL, one vote each), Class C shares (GOOG, no votes), and Class B shares (held by insiders, 10 votes each). **Larry Page and Sergey Brin (co-founders)** are the top shareholders - together they hold **\~84% of Class B shares**, giving them **51%+ of total voting power** . In economic ownership, they each own around 5-6% of Alphabet's total equity (having sold some over the years). Their voting control means they effectively **exercise ultimate decision-making at shareholder meetings**. For example, any shareholder proposals opposed by Page/Brin are virtually guaranteed to fail regardless of outside investor support.

Aside from the founders, the largest shareholders (by economic stake) are typically **institutional investors**: **Vanguard Group** (\~7.4% of Class A+C combined), **BlackRock** (\~6%), **State Street** (\~3%), and other index fund providers . **Mutual fund and ETF investors** own a substantial portion of the freely traded shares. Another notable holder has been **Berkshire Hathaway**, which disclosed a small stake in 2022, but it's not among top holders (likely &lt;1%). There are also active managers like T. Rowe Price, Fidelity, and Capital Group with significant positions, but none approach the voting influence of the founders.

**Voting and Shareholder Meetings:** With Page and Brin's control, **shareholder votes are mostly formalities**. For instance, at the 2023 annual shareholder meeting, multiple shareholder proposals (on topics like equal voting rights, political advertising transparency, and audit of AI ethics) were brought forth. All were defeated, typically receiving 5%-25% support from Class A shareholders - but with the founders voting **against**, none passed . The **board's recommendations** almost always align with the founders' stance, and thus all board-nominated directors are elected easily, compensation packages are approved, etc. Notably, top institutional shareholders often vote in favor of some governance reforms (e.g. one-share-one-vote), but their votes are overwhelmed by the Class B super-votes. In 2022, a proposal to recapitalize into single class common stock got over 30% of the Class A votes but still failed due to insiders . **Major topics in recent years** included calls for better disclosure of lobbying, linking exec pay to diversity goals, and reports on misinformation on YouTube - all of which management opposed and the founders blocked, despite some outside support.

**Stances of Top Shareholders:** Index fund giants (Vanguard, BlackRock, etc.) generally vote in line with governance best practices - for example, **BlackRock supported some environmental and diversity proposals**. However, they cannot override the founders. Some large active funds have been quietly supportive of management's strategy (enjoying the stock's performance). The lack of activist investor influence (because activists have no leverage against majority voting control) means the founders and board set the agenda. **In essence, Larry Page and Sergey Brin have executed their voting rights to maintain the status quo**: keeping the dual-class structure and voting down outside proposals. They have shown a preference for management and board continuity and have not pushed for any radical changes via vote - their stance is usually aligned with management's recommendations on proxy items. For example, in 2024 the board (with Page/Brin's support) approved the first dividend; any shareholders who might have been against dividends had no practical way to stop it (though most welcomed it).

It's worth noting that despite their voting power, the founders have been fairly hands-off operationally in recent years - they exercise control mainly to **ensure strategic freedom for management** and to fend off unwanted interventions. The top institutional holders, while unable to change outcomes, do engage with Alphabet on governance. In 2022, Vanguard and others urged Alphabet to improve disclosures on sustainability and AI ethics. Alphabet responded somewhat by publishing **AI principles and reports**. But on core governance (dual-class stock), the company remains unchanged.

**Summary:** The **founders (Page & Brin)** are by far the most influential shareholders, executing votes to maintain control. **Institutional investors** hold large stakes but limited influence; they often support shareholder-friendly proposals, which get overruled. The shareholder meeting topics often revolve around social responsibility and governance, with the board/founders typically **opposing all shareholder proposals** - and winning. For instance, in 2023 at least 18 shareholder proposals were defeated, including one to report on data privacy and one to assess alignment of YouTube policies with human rights - both defeated by over 90% of total vote (due to Class B votes) . Thus, the **top outside shareholders' stances** (generally favoring more transparency and equal voting rights) are noted in proxy vote tallies, but ultimately **Page and Brin decide the outcomes** in line with management's preferences.

### **6. Share Buyback Plan (Capital Return)**

Alphabet has historically been frugal about returning capital, but in the past few years it instituted large **share repurchase programs**. **Currently, Alphabet has a substantial buyback authorization** in place. In April 2024, the board approved a new **$70 billion stock repurchase program** - this was announced alongside the Q1 2024 earnings. It marked one of Alphabet's largest buybacks ever, roughly 5.5% of its market cap at the time . The buyback covers both Class A and Class C shares and has no set expiration (management executes purchases at its discretion).

**Comparison to Last 10 Years:** Alphabet's embrace of buybacks is relatively recent. For most of its history since the 2004 IPO, Google did **not repurchase stock** and paid **no dividend** (preferring to reinvest cash). The first significant repurchase program came in 2019, when Alphabet announced a $25 billion buyback. It followed with even larger authorizations: $28 billion in 2020, $50 billion in 2021, and $70 billion in 2022 . The pace accelerated - in 2022, Alphabet bought back \~$60 billion worth of shares (reducing share count by \~1.5% net of issuance). Over 2017-2021, total shares outstanding actually **decreased** modestly (from \~15.0 billion adjusted shares in 2017 to \~14.0 billion in 2022) , indicating buybacks outpaced stock-based compensation issuance. The current plan (2022-2024 authorizations combined) sums to $120B+, a dramatic ramp-up. By contrast, **10 years ago (2013-2014)** Google had no buybacks and share count was diluting \~1-2% annually due to employee stock grants.

As of Q1 2025, **share count stood at \~13.15 billion** (down \~2.6% from a year prior) , reflecting aggressive repurchases. The new $70B authorization in 2024 followed a $70B plan from 2022 (mostly completed by 2023). In April 2023, Alphabet had also authorized $70B. So effectively, **Alphabet is buying back \~$60-70B of stock per year recently**, a huge step up from \~$15B/year in 2018-2019. Over the past 10 years, cumulative buybacks now exceed $200B, with the majority occurring in the last 5 years.

**Management's Rationale:** Alphabet generates enormous free cash flow (e.g. $69.5B in 2023) and had a cash pile of $115B+ at one point . With limited M&A and now moderate capex relative to cash generation, management began returning excess cash via buybacks. CFO Ruth Porat has said buybacks are intended to offset dilution and return excess capital to shareholders "in a prudent manner." The timing tends to be opportunistic - for example, in 2022-23 when the stock price dipped due to macro concerns, Alphabet continued repurchasing shares heavily (its share count fell \~3% in 2023 despite stock-based comp) .

Notably, a 20-for-1 stock split in July 2022 increased share count by factor 20 (reflected in the adjusted numbers above), but excluding that, the underlying share count has been shrinking \~1-2% annually in recent years, accelerating to \~3% in 2023 .

**Summary:** Alphabet's current buyback program is massive and ongoing - **$70B authorized in 2024** on top of similar amounts in prior years . Compared to the prior decade, this is a significant change: up until \~2017, Alphabet had *zero*buybacks; from 2018 onward it gradually ramped repurchases, now returning roughly **40-60% of annual free cash flow**via buybacks. The share count reduction enhances EPS growth and signals confidence that management views the stock as undervalued. Importantly, these buybacks are executed without jeopardizing Alphabet's huge cash reserves (still \~$95B cash on hand at end of 2024) . Investors generally applaud the buybacks, though some have noted that given the dual-class structure, buybacks also slightly **increase the founders' voting power** (since Class C shares are often the ones repurchased, the proportional ownership of Class B rises). So far this effect is minor. Overall, Alphabet's buyback track record in the last 5 years demonstrates a shift to a more **shareholder-return-friendly capital allocation**, while in the prior 10 years it prioritized reinvestment.

### **7. Value Investors' Buy/Sell Activity in Alphabet Stock**

Given Alphabet's dominance and cash flows, many prominent investors - including those with a "value" orientation - have taken positions over the years. However, traditional value investors have sometimes been wary of big tech due to valuations. That said, **Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway** made headlines when it revealed a (small) stake in Alphabet in 2022. Buffett had long **regretted not buying Google earlier** (since Google's ad business helped Berkshire's Geico). By Q4 2022, one of Berkshire's investment managers initiated a modest position (\~0.3% of Alphabet's shares) around an average price in the $90-$100 range (post-split) . Berkshire's average cost is estimated around $95. As of the latest filings, Berkshire *still holds* its Alphabet stake (roughly 2 million shares), which is a tiny allocation for Berkshire (and about 0.02% of Alphabet). This indicates Berkshire sees long-term value; they have not sold, implying they are content holding indefinitely.

Other noted value/long-term investors: **Seth Klarman's Baupost Group** bought Alphabet in past years - Baupost had a significant GOOG position in 2017-2019, buying around the $1000 (pre-split) level ($50 post-split). Klarman trimmed that stake by 2020 (possibly taking profit as it ran up). **Terry Smith's Fundsmith** (a UK-based quality-focused fund) has held Alphabet as a top position for many years, buying more on dips - Fundsmith's cost basis is likely low (pre-2015 buys) and they have *not* sold, as Alphabet fits their "high quality, high ROIC" profile. **Chuck Akre** (Akre Focus Fund) also bought Google years ago in the $500-$600 pre-split range (=$25-$30 now) and held it long-term, benefiting from its compounding.

On the other hand, some famed value investors trimmed or sold in recent years. **Princeton's endowment (David Swensen)** was an early investor in Google pre-IPO and held for a long time; by mid-2010s some had distributed those shares. **Philippe Laffont's Coatue** (growth/value crossover fund) had large Alphabet stakes but reduced them in 2022 amid tech volatility.

An interesting case: **Christopher Hohn's TCI Fund** - a value-oriented activist fund - has been a significant Alphabet shareholder. ### **8. Intrinsic Valuation (Warren Buffett's Method)**

To estimate Alphabet's *intrinsic value*, we perform a **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** analysis inspired by Warren Buffett's approach, using **conservative assumptions** and a **margin of safety**. Alphabet's "owner earnings" (free cash flow) in 2024 was about **$72.8 billion** . We will project this cash flow over 10 years under two scenarios:

-   **Scenario A (Conservative/Low Growth):** Assume **8% annual growth** in free cash flow for the next 5 years, then growth decelerates to a **terminal growth rate of 2%** by year 10. This roughly reflects Alphabet's lowest revenue growth rates in the past decade (\~8-10% at the trough in 2022-2023) . Terminal growth is capped at a modest 2% (below expected GDP growth, satisfying Buffett's preference for conservative terminal assumptions).

-   **Scenario B (Average Growth):** Assume **15% annual growth** in FCF for 5 years then tapering to &lt;3% by year 10. Alphabet's revenue grew \~18% CAGR over the last 10 years (boosted by a 41% jump in 2021), so 15% represents a reasonable average growth scenario if AI and Cloud keep driving strong gains.

For discount rates, Buffett often uses a **10% discount rate** (as a hurdle for large stable companies) - we'll start with 10%. We will also examine a higher discount (15%) to reflect more risk or to compute expected return. We incorporate a **30%-50% margin of safety** to identify attractive buy prices.

**Inputs Summary:**

-   **Initial FCF (2024)**: $72.8B

-   **Growth Scenario A:** \~8% for years 1-5, tapering to 2% by year 10; **Scenario B:** \~15% for 5 years, tapering to 2.5% by year 10.

-   **Discount Rate:** 10% (and 15% for sensitivity/expected return).

-   **Terminal Growth:** 2% (below 3% upper bound).

-   **Shares Outstanding:** \~13.31 billion (2024 year-end) .

Using these inputs, the DCF calculation yields:

-   **Intrinsic Value per Share (Scenario A, 10% discount):** ≈ **$96/share**. This is the present value of projected cash flows (years 1-10) plus terminal value, discounted at 10%. At a 30% margin of safety, this implies a **buy target around $67**. At a 50% MOS, about $48. (Notably, $96 is much lower than the current market price, indicating the conservative nature of this scenario and/or a high hurdle rate.)

-   **Intrinsic Value per Share (Scenario B, 10% discount):** ≈ **$150-$160/share** (higher growth drives a larger PV). With 30% MOS, target ≈ $105; with 50% MOS, ≈ $75. This suggests that if Alphabet can sustain \~15% growth for several years, the stock's intrinsic value is closer to the current market price - but a prudent investor might still wait for a pullback to \~$100 to have a margin of safety.

For completeness, here are implied valuations at 10% vs 15% discount in Scenario A:

-   At **10%** discount, intrinsic \~$96 (as above).

-   At **15%** discount (much harsher), intrinsic ≈ **$69/share**. The higher discount dramatically reduces present value. The current stock price ( \~$190 ) is well above this, indicating the market is not pricing Alphabet as if it will only return 15% - rather, the market expects a lower required return or higher growth.

**Expected Return (IRR):** We can invert the process to find the discount rate that equates the DCF value to the current stock price (around $185-$190). Using Scenario A cash flows, the **implied expected annual return** is extremely low, roughly **1-3%**. This suggests that if Alphabet only grows FCF \~8% then slows to 2%, buying at $190 would yield a very subpar return (because the stock is pricing in more optimism). Under Scenario B (15% growth), the IRR equating \~$190 is higher, on the order of **8-9%**. This means the market might be expecting something closer to low-teens growth to justify current pricing with an \~8-10% investor return.

Buffett's method also involves qualitative judgment - Alphabet's tremendous competitive moat and cash generation may justify using the lower discount (10%) for a high-quality business. If one believes Alphabet can grow its owner earnings at double-digits for a decade, then the stock's intrinsic value likely exceeds $200, and the current price could be reasonable. However, Buffett would insist on a *margin of safety*.

**Conclusion of Intrinsic Value Analysis:** Under conservative assumptions, Alphabet's **intrinsic value** appears lower than the current market price, reinforcing the need for a margin of safety. A value investor might prefer to buy significantly below current levels - for example, **below \~$130 (which was \~30% off the highs)** - to ensure an adequate return. Indeed, when Alphabet's stock dipped to \~$85-$100 in late 2022, that was a zone arguably below intrinsic value with margin of safety (and we saw investors like Berkshire buying then). At the current \~$190, the expected return assuming conservative growth is relatively modest. That said, if Alphabet's **intrinsic quality (high ROIC, dominant franchise)**enables it to outperform these low growth forecasts, then the intrinsic value could be higher. Buffett often looks for exceptional companies he can hold through market fluctuations. Alphabet may well be one - but even Buffett's team waited until a significant dip to buy. Thus, using Buffett's style: one would calculate a **buy price that yields a 10-15% expected return with no margin** (which, in our DCF, for Scenario B was around $150), then **demand 30-50% margin** - yielding a **desired entry perhaps around $75-$105**. Those levels align with periods of market pessimism (which occurred in 2020 and 2022). In summary, **Alphabet's intrinsic value range** might be roughly **$150-$200** by a reasonable DCF, but a value investor would aim to pay significantly less to ensure upside.

*(All figures in USD. These intrinsic value estimates are subject to big assumptions; slight changes in growth or discount rate produce very different results. Investors should treat them as rough guides rather than precise values.)*
