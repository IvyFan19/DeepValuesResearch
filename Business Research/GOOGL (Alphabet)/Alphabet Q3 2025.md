## **Latest Quarter Earnings Summary**

Alphabet delivered a strong performance in Q3 2025. Revenue rose 16% year-over-year to **$102.3 billion**, marking Alphabet's first-ever $100B+ quarter . Growth was broad-based: Google Services (which includes Search, YouTube, Android/Play, etc.) revenue grew 14% to $87.1B, while Google Cloud revenue jumped 34% to $15.2B . Operating income increased 9% (or **+22%** excluding a one-time $3.5B EU fine), and net income surged **33%** to $34.98B (EPS $2.87) . Alphabet's operating margin was **30.5%** (or 33.9% excluding the fine) , reflecting continued cost discipline even as it invests heavily in AI and data centers. Notably, "Other income" contributed $12.8B (due to unrealized gains on investments) , boosting net profit. Management highlighted momentum in AI: new AI features in Search, the Gemini AI model (7B tokens processed per minute), and robust growth in Google Cloud (backlog $155B) . Alphabet now expects full-year 2025 capital expenditures of $91-93B, underscoring aggressive investment in servers and infrastructure . Overall, Q3 2025 results beat expectations with **double-digit growth across every major segment**, demonstrating Alphabet's resilience in a challenging environment .

## **Business Model**

**Core Operations** - Alphabet is a technology conglomerate best known as the parent of Google. It generates revenue from a variety of products and services including internet search, online advertising, cloud computing, consumer hardware (Pixel, Nest), and subscription services (YouTube Premium, Google One), as well as "Other Bets" like Waymo (self-driving cars) . The **vast majority of Alphabet's income comes from advertising**. In 2024, roughly **75-80%** of revenue was ad-driven (Google Search ads, YouTube ads, and ads on partner sites) . Alphabet's Google Services segment - which includes Search, YouTube, Google Play, Android, Chrome, etc. - contributed **87% of revenue** for the first 3 quarters of 2024 . Google Search & other ads alone were $198.1B in 2024 (56% of revenue), with YouTube ads $36.1B (10%) . Meanwhile, Google Cloud (cloud infrastructure and Google Workspace) is a fast-growing source now comprising about **12% of revenue** , and was finally profitable in 2024 (operating income $4B for first 3 quarters) . "Google Other" sources - like Pixel devices, Play Store app sales, and YouTube subscriptions - make up \~10-12% of revenue . The tiny **Other Bets** segment (Waymo, Verily, etc.) contributes under 0.5% of revenue and operates at a loss .

**Revenue Breakdown** - Alphabet's main income streams are: *Search advertising*, which leverages Google's \~90% global search market share to connect advertisers with users; *YouTube advertising* (and to a lesser extent, YouTube Premium subscriptions); *Google Network ads* on third-party sites via AdSense; *Cloud services* fees from Google Cloud Platform and Workspace; and *other sales* like apps, content, and hardware . Advertising is by far the largest, accounting for about three-quarters of total revenue . For example, in Q4 2024 Google advertising brought in $72.46B of Alphabet's $96.47B revenue (75%) . Cloud revenue was $11.96B that quarter (\~12%) , and "Other" Google services (Play, hardware, YouTube subscriptions, etc.) about $11.63B (12%) . This ad-heavy mix means Alphabet's fortunes are tightly linked to online ad spending cycles. The company's **business model** essentially monetizes its massive user base and data across platforms: billions of users use Search, YouTube, Android, Maps, etc., and Alphabet sells targeted ads against that usage . Additionally, Google's ecosystem (Android OS, Chrome browser, Gmail, etc.) keeps users within its services, reinforcing its ad and subscription revenue streams. Alphabet also invests heavily in R&D for new technologies (AI, quantum computing, autonomous vehicles) to create future revenue opportunities beyond ads.

**Scale and Customers** - Alphabet's products serve **billions of users** worldwide. Google Search processes over 8.5 billion searches per day, giving it 90%+ search market share . YouTube has over 2+ billion monthly logged-in users. Android powers \~3 billion active devices globally. Gmail has \~1.5 billion users. These enormous user bases make advertisers and publishers its de-facto "customers" for ads - Alphabet reported **over 300 million paid subscriptions** across Google One (cloud storage) and YouTube Premium as of Q3 2025 . On the enterprise side, **Google Cloud** serves hundreds of thousands of organizations (exact customer count not disclosed) and ended Q3 2025 with a $155B backlog of cloud contracts , indicating many large business clients. Alphabet also sells **hardware units** like Pixel smartphones (estimated \~10 million units annually in recent years) and Nest smart devices, though its global smartphone share is small (\~5% in the US) . Overall, virtually anyone who uses the internet is a Google "user," and advertisers across industries (small businesses to Fortune 500) are its paying customers via Google Ads.

**Moat and Competitive Advantages** - Alphabet enjoys a wide economic moat primarily due to its **dominant market positions and proprietary technology**. Google's search engine is virtually synonymous with the internet - its massive scale and sophisticated algorithms produce better results, attracting more users, which in turn draws more advertisers (a network effect) . The company benefits from tremendous **data scale** (trillions of search queries, user behavior data) that competitors cannot easily replicate, feeding into Google's AI/ML models and ad targeting. Its Android mobile operating system has \~71% global market share, ensuring Google's services are pre-installed on billions of devices. YouTube is the world's largest video platform (estimated &gt;2 billion MAUs), with a vast content library and creator ecosystem that would be very hard to duplicate. These reinforce Google's advertising hegemony. Alphabet's **brand strength** and familiarity ("Google" as a verb) also add to its moat. Furthermore, the company's sheer financial resources (over $118B in cash & marketable securities on hand as of 2024 ) and talent (190k+ employees, many in engineering) allow it to out-invest others in R&D for next-gen technologies like artificial intelligence. Indeed, Google's AI capabilities (e.g. leading AI research at DeepMind, large language models like PaLM/Gemini) and cloud infrastructure represent a competitive edge it's rapidly leveraging across products. Alphabet's ecosystem (Search, Gmail, Chrome, Android, YouTube, Maps, etc.) creates high **user switching costs** - consumers are deeply integrated into Google's services. Similarly, advertisers have few alternatives that offer comparable ROI at scale, aside from perhaps Facebook (Meta) or Amazon for certain ads. In sum, Alphabet's moat is built on scale, data, technological prowess, and an integrated ecosystem, which together create formidable entry barriers for would-be competitors.

## **Headwinds & Risks**

**Short-Term/Tactical Headwinds:** In the near term (next 1-2 years), Alphabet faces macroeconomic and industry-specific challenges that could temporarily impact results. A key risk is **digital advertising cyclicality** - advertising spend tends to shrink if the economy slows. For example, in 2022 amid recession fears and higher interest rates, Google's ad revenue growth decelerated to single digits . If global GDP softens or businesses cut marketing budgets, Alphabet's core revenue (ads \~80% of total) could underperform. Relatedly, **foreign currency fluctuations** are a short-term factor; a strong US dollar can reduce reported revenue from international markets (around 53% of revenue ) when translated to USD. Alphabet noted a 1% negative currency impact on 2024 sales . Another current headwind is **regulatory pressure**: in late 2023 and 2024, Alphabet has been defending antitrust lawsuits, such as the U.S. DOJ case alleging Google's search dominance is anticompetitive. While the final outcome is uncertain, the overhang of potential fines or business model changes (e.g. limits on Google's ability to pay for default search engine status on browsers/devices) could create headline risk. Indeed, in **September 2025 a U.S. district court ruled Google violated antitrust laws**, the first such ruling against a U.S. tech firm in decades (Google is appealing). Although no immediate remedies were imposed, continued legal scrutiny is a distraction and could lead to new obligations (e.g. better interoperability, data usage constraints) in the near term.

On the industry front, **competition in AI** has intensified quickly due to the emergence of OpenAI's ChatGPT and Microsoft's investments in AI-powered search. In the short run, the concern was that AI chatbots could disrupt traditional search (users might get answers from AI without seeing ads). Google responded by launching its own chatbot (Bard) and integrating generative AI into Search, but these efforts are still early. The cost of running large AI models is high (affecting margins), and it's unclear how AI-heavy search results will be monetized effectively. So, the **transition to AI search represents a short-term execution challenge** - Google must innovate rapidly while maintaining ad revenue. Additionally, **content moderation and brand safety issues** periodically flare up (e.g. YouTube needing to ensure ads don't appear on harmful content), which can cause some advertisers to pause spending temporarily. We also note that **inflation and rising labor costs** pressured Alphabet's expenses recently; the company announced 12,000 layoffs in early 2023 to right-size headcount after over-hiring during the pandemic boom . In the short term, such restructuring can incur charges and affect employee morale, though it should yield longer-term savings. Lastly, supply chain issues or weak consumer confidence could impact sales of Alphabet's hardware devices (Pixel phones, etc.), a smaller revenue slice but still notable in holiday quarters.

**Longer-Term/Permanent Risks:** Looking 3-10 years out, Alphabet faces more structural threats that could materially alter its growth trajectory or profitability. One major risk is **regulatory and political intervention** in tech. Alphabet's dominance in search and online ads has drawn antitrust scrutiny globally (the EU fined Google billions in 2017-2019 for anti-competitive practices ). There is a possibility of **regulatory actions that permanently impact its business model**, such as strict limits on data collection/use (e.g. privacy laws like GDPR that could make ad targeting less effective) or even forced divestiture of certain businesses. For example, regulators could restrict Google's ability to tie its services together (as the EU did by forcing Android to offer search engine choice screens ) or constrain agreements like paying Apple to be the default iPhone search engine (DOJ's case targets this). While a breakup of Alphabet is not imminent, regulatory constraints on its ad targeting or app distribution could **erode its moat over time**.

Another long-term risk is **technological disruption**. If user behavior shifts dramatically - say, moving from traditional search to new platforms - Google could lose its central role. The rise of **AI-driven search and assistants** is one example: if AI answers (like ChatGPT) disintermediate search or if users rely more on voice assistants/Chatbots that bypass Google, the company's ad revenue could structurally decline. Competitors like Amazon (for product search/ads), TikTok (for young users' search and ad attention), and Microsoft (integrating OpenAI into Bing) are all vying to change how people find information. While Google is investing heavily to remain at the cutting edge of AI, the risk is that the *nature*of internet use changes in a way that **reduces the value of Google's advertising inventory**. Similarly, changes in platforms - e.g. if more search activity happens within apps or walled gardens rather than the open web - could challenge Google's reach. For instance, younger users now sometimes use TikTok or Instagram to search for local info or inspiration, cutting Google out of those queries.

**Permanent margin pressures** are another concern. As the digital ad market matures, Alphabet might see a mix shift to lower-margin businesses (like Cloud). Google Cloud, while now profitable, historically operated near breakeven; if Cloud becomes a larger share of revenue, Alphabet's overall margins could be structurally lower than in the past when advertising dominated. Additionally, **competition for ad dollars** from players like Amazon (huge in e-commerce search ads) and Meta means Alphabet must continually improve its offerings, possibly by sharing more revenue with partners (higher traffic acquisition costs). TAC (payments to be default search on browsers, etc.) already consumes \~22% of Google advertising revenue , and could rise further if, say, Apple or Mozilla demand more or if new deals are needed. Over the long run, **policy changes** like the end of third-party cookies (set for 2024 in Chrome) and more user privacy controls might permanently make targeting less precise, pressuring ad prices. Apple's ATT framework already hurt mobile ad tracking for peers; if Google implements similar limits (or is forced to by regulators), it could reduce advertisers' ROI on Google ads.

Finally, **geopolitical and societal shifts** pose risks. Alphabet is largely absent from China (censored) - if the global internet splinters further (e.g. into U.S.-led and China-led ecosystems), that caps its addressable market. Geopolitical conflict could also disrupt its operations or talent flow (many Google engineers and users are worldwide). On the social side, rising concern over misinformation on YouTube, data monopolies, or AI ethics could lead to permanent new responsibilities or limitations for Google (for instance, mandated content moderation practices or AI use regulations) that add costs or constrain growth. In summary, while Alphabet is deeply entrenched, it must navigate evolving technology paradigms and regulatory landscapes that could *permanently* reshape its business over the coming decade.

## **Cost Structure**

Alphabet's cost structure comprises both substantial fixed costs (infrastructure, R&D) and variable costs (traffic acquisition, data center operations) that scale with usage. **Cost of Revenue** is the largest expense category, at $146.3B in 2024 (42% of revenue) . Key components of cost of revenue include **Traffic Acquisition Costs (TAC)** - the fees Google pays partner websites and device makers to distribute or host its ads/search. TAC was \~$54.9B in 2024 (15.7% of revenue) . This is a largely variable expense that grows with search and ad volume; for instance, if people make more searches or YouTube serves more ads, TAC paid to Apple (for iPhone default search) or AdSense publishers rises in proportion. Another major cost of revenue is running Google's enormous **technical infrastructure** (data centers, servers, network, energy). Depreciation of servers and data center equipment was $15.3B in 2024 , and total operations/network costs (including bandwidth and data center utilities) are significant - these scale roughly with usage and number of services Google supports (hence variable to user and traffic growth). **Content acquisition costs** also fall under cost of revenue: e.g. payments to YouTube content creators (a revenue-share of ad earnings) and licensing fees for content (such as app sales revenue share to developers on Google Play). These costs rise in line with related revenues (YouTube payouts increase as YouTube ads increase, etc.). Thus, a substantial portion of cost of revenue is *variable or semi-variable*, tied to ecosystem growth.

Alphabet also has large **operating expenses** that are more fixed in the short term: primarily *labor-related* (salary, stock-based compensation) for its \~183,000 full-time employees . In 2024, **Research & Development (R&D)** was $49.33B (14% of revenue) . R&D includes the thousands of engineers working on Search algorithms, AI, self-driving tech, etc. While R&D spending can be managed (as seen by 12,000 layoffs in early 2023, many in R&D), it's largely a fixed investment in innovation - not directly tied to the number of ads sold in a given quarter. **Sales and Marketing** expense was $27.8B in 2024 (8% of revenue) . Much of this is relatively fixed (employee salaries, marketing campaigns, promotional spend) though some, like sales commissions, vary with revenue. Notably, Google historically has modest marketing spend relative to revenue (its products often "sell themselves"), but in newer areas like Cloud and hardware, marketing outlays have grown. **General & Administrative (G&A)** was $14.2B in 2024 (4% of revenue) , covering corporate support functions (finance, legal, facilities). G&A includes legal fees which can spike due to lawsuits or regulatory matters, but otherwise scales gradually as the company grows.

Among these, the most significant costs are **Traffic Acquisition** and **R&D personnel**. TAC in particular represents a substantial ongoing **per-unit cost** for each search or ad view on partner platforms - it's essentially a variable cost of revenue share. For example, Google might pay Apple an estimated &gt;$15 billion per year (TAC) for iPhone search default , which increases as search query volume on iPhones increases. On the production side, each incremental search query or YouTube view has a small server cost and bandwidth cost; at Google's scale these unit costs are fractions of a penny, but in aggregate (billions of queries), they contribute to cost of revenue. **Labor costs** (mostly in R&D) are a huge fixed cost - Alphabet's median compensation is high, and stock-based compensation was \~$22.9B in 2024 . That said, management can adjust headcount growth: after rapid hiring in 2020-21, they froze hiring and cut staff in 2023, reducing expense growth.

Over time, these costs have **trended upward** absolutely but with some efficiency gains. For instance, Google's gross profit margin hovered \~55-60% for much of the past decade , meaning cost of revenue \~40-45% of sales - indicating that even as usage soared, Google managed to serve additional queries without proportional cost increase (economies of scale in data centers). However, **TAC as a percentage of ad revenue has gradually risen** (as mobile and distribution deals grew), pressuring gross margins. Operating expense ratios climbed in 2017-2019 due to heavy R&D and Cloud investment, then improved in 2021 when revenues spiked post-pandemic without equivalent OpEx jump (operating margin expanded to 30%+ in 2021) . In weaker economic periods, Alphabet tends to rein in variable and discretionary costs - e.g. in late 2022, facing slowing revenue, it curtailed hiring, closed some offices, and in 2023 reduced staff (saving an estimated $2.5B annually) . During economic downturns, marketing spend is often trimmed and some capex projects delayed, while core R&D (for long-term bets) might continue relatively unabated. Conversely, in boom times (like 2021) the company has shown willingness to invest aggressively (2021 headcount +21% YoY , capex up significantly) to seize growth opportunities.

In summary, Alphabet's largest costs - TAC and infrastructure - scale with the business but benefit from scale efficiencies (e.g. data center automation, negotiating better TAC terms at volume). The significant fixed costs (talent, R&D) give it immense development capacity, but also mean **operating leverage**: when revenue growth outpaces hiring, margins widen (as in 2021), and when revenue stalls, those costs take time to adjust (as in 2022 when op margin dipped). Alphabet's challenge is balancing hefty ongoing investments in innovation (AI, Cloud, Other Bets) with expense discipline. The 2023 cost cuts showed management will act when necessary to protect profitability . Going forward, capital expenditures (mostly data centers and servers) are expected to remain very high (\~$91-93B in 2025) - a largely fixed investment that should enable future growth but can crimp free cash flow in weaker periods. Overall, Alphabet's cost structure has a high baseline due to its global infrastructure and workforce, but it retains flexibility at the margins (slowing hiring, adjusting marketing, etc.) to navigate different economic conditions.

## **Management & Governance**

**Current Leadership:** Alphabet's executive team blends Google veterans and experienced hires. The CEO is **Sundar Pichai**, who has led Google since 2015 and Alphabet since 2019 . Pichai has been with Google since 2004 (notably leading Chrome and Android) and is known for his soft-spoken, execution-focused style. The **President and Chief Investment Officer** is **Ruth Porat**, who served as CFO from 2015 until August 2023 and now oversees Alphabet's "Other Bets" investments and capital allocation . Porat, a former Morgan Stanley CFO, is credited with instilling financial discipline at Google. Replacing her as CFO in 2023 is **Anat Ashkenazi**, previously CFO at Eli Lilly . Other key executives include **Philipp Schindler** (Chief Business Officer, heading ad sales), **Prabhakar Raghavan** (SVP overseeing Search and Assistant), **Thomas Kurian** (CEO of Google Cloud), and **Kent Walker** (President of Global Affairs and Chief Legal Officer). Alphabet's Board of Directors (10 members) is chaired by former Stanford President **John Hennessy**, and notably includes co-founders **Larry Page** and **Sergey Brin** as directors . Independent directors on the board feature respected figures like economist **Roger Ferguson Jr.**, venture capitalist **John Doerr**, genomics pioneer **Frances Arnold**, and finance executive **Robin Washington** . The presence of Page and Brin (who together control \~51% of voting power via super-voting shares) ensures the founders' influence remains strong in strategic decisions, though they no longer handle day-to-day operations.

**Historical Leadership and Culture:** Google's management lineage includes notable figures like **Eric Schmidt** - CEO from 2001-2011 and executive chairman until 2017 - who provided adult supervision in Google's early hyper-growth years and whose influence shaped a culture of ambitious innovation. The co-founders, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, were deeply involved in management until 2019 (Page was Alphabet CEO 2015-2019). They fostered Google's famous **"moonshot" culture**, encouraging big bets (like self-driving cars, smart glasses) and a tolerance for failure in pursuit of breakthrough tech. That innovative spirit continues under Pichai, though he has arguably brought more focus and incremental improvement rather than wild experimentation. Several long-time Google leaders are still with the company (e.g. Prabhakar Raghavan, who joined in 2012, or Susan Wojcicki who ran YouTube until 2023). The executive team's depth is a strength - many have a decade+ at Google, ensuring continuity of the culture and institutional knowledge. At the same time, recent hires like CFO Ashkenazi bring fresh perspectives and outside expertise.

**Execution and Integrity:** Generally, Alphabet's management has a solid track record of setting and achieving long-term goals. Under Pichai's leadership, Google successfully navigated the transition to mobile (Android and mobile search monetization), rapidly built a top-tier cloud business (Google Cloud revenue grew from \~$4B in 2017 to $31B in 2024) , and kept the core ad machine growing double-digits most years. Pichai tends to **under-promise and over-deliver** in that Google doesn't provide explicit forward guidance, but consistently invests for the future which has paid off with innovations (like AI) arriving faster than some expected. For example, management's push into AI - reorganizing DeepMind and Google Brain into one unit in 2023 - is positioning the company well in the AI race. The leadership has faced criticism at times for being *too secretive or slow to acknowledge issues*, but there haven't been serious accusations of misleading investors. In fact, Google is relatively transparent in breaking out segment results (they began disclosing Cloud operating profit in 2023 after it turned positive). One area of slight concern is that management tends to be **guarded in communications** - on earnings calls, Pichai and Porat often give limited detail, and they avoided discussing certain metrics (e.g. YouTube profits were long undisclosed). However, there is no evidence of outright hiding problems; when challenges arose (e.g. a revenue slowdown in 2022), Porat was candid about forex headwinds and the need to control costs . Importantly, **management has shown willingness to make tough decisions** when needed: e.g. the January 2023 layoffs (about 6% of staff) indicated they recognized over-hiring and took action to align expenses with the new economic reality . This suggests a pragmatic approach rather than sweeping issues under the rug.

Analysts generally find Pichai and Porat to be **straightforward if somewhat cautious** in answering questions. On recent calls, Pichai has addressed AI-related concerns head-on, emphasizing Google's progress, while Porat is known for her exact numbers and sometimes deflecting on forward-looking specifics (typical CFO approach). There haven't been notable instances of management being evasive about major problems - for example, when YouTube ad growth stalled in 2019 due to algorithm changes, they acknowledged it and adjusted. The main integrity question mark might be around antitrust: internal communications (exposed in court) showed some aggressive tactics to secure default positions, but investors largely view that as competitive strategy, not deceit toward shareholders. Overall, Alphabet's leadership is considered **highly competent and generally trustworthy**, albeit cautious in public commentary.

**Tenure and Alignment:** Sundar Pichai has been at Google for 19 years and CEO for 8; Ruth Porat served \~8 years as CFO and remains an influential executive; most of the top team have long tenures, providing stability. Management continuity has been a hallmark - Alphabet hasn't had the churn at the top seen at some tech peers. This stability has helped ensure consistent execution of long-term projects (Cloud, AI, etc.). As for **shareholder alignment**, Google's unique dual-class share structure means founders Page and Brin retain majority voting control (each about 3% of shares but together \~51% votes) . This can be a double-edged sword: it insulates management from short-term market pressures (allowing heavy R&D spending and moonshots), but also means outside shareholders have little say. However, Page and Brin's interests are broadly aligned with creating long-term value - they remain major shareholders (each holding stock worth \~$150B) and have an obvious interest in Alphabet's success. Current CEO Pichai is not a founder, but he's heavily invested via stock compensation. He's received sizable stock grants (including a \~$200M award in 2019) and currently owns roughly 0.1% of the company (worth over $3B) - a meaningful stake . Pichai's pay is structured so that he benefits more if Alphabet's stock performs well over multi-year periods (much of his stock vests based on total shareholder return vs the S&P). This suggests his incentives are tied to shareholder outcomes. Other top execs similarly receive the bulk of compensation in Alphabet stock units, aligning them with stock performance.

It's worth noting that **insider selling is regular** at Alphabet - executives often sell shares as part of diversification and to cover taxes on vesting stock. These are typically done under 10b5-1 plans and not viewed as a negative signal. We haven't seen executives dumping shares in panic or anything of that sort; rather, their sales have been steady and proportional. There's little evidence of insiders **buying on dips**, but that's common in large tech where insiders are granted so much stock that they rarely purchase on the open market. The key value alignment is that the founders and many early employees (now execs) derive the vast majority of their wealth from Alphabet stock, so they are motivated to increase its long-term value. For example, co-founder Sergey Brin, after some years largely off-hands, reportedly returned to a more active role in 2023 to help with Google's AI efforts - an indication he's invested (figuratively and literally) in the company's future success.

In terms of **integrity and governance**, Alphabet has had few scandals involving management misconduct. There have been internal culture controversies (e.g. walkouts over handling of harassment issues in 2018), but nothing suggesting that management misled investors or engaged in financial impropriety. The board's structure (with the founders' outsized control) means traditional corporate governance checks (like say-on-pay votes or activist investors) have limited influence. Some governance advocates criticize this, but at the same time, it has allowed management to **focus on long-term bets** without fear of short-term ouster. To date, that long-term approach has benefited all shareholders (e.g. investments in Android, YouTube, and AI that took years to monetize). All things considered, Alphabet's management is **highly capable and generally shareholder-friendly in terms of creating value**, even if the dual-class setup reduces direct accountability to public investors on operational decisions.

## **Shareholders & Capital Returns**

**Ownership Structure:** Alphabet's stock structure consists of Class A (GOOGL, one vote per share), Class C (GOOG, no votes), and Class B shares (held by insiders, 10 votes per share). This dual-class (A/C) plus insider Class B setup means founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin **control 51% of voting power** while owning only about 6% of economic interest combined . As of December 2023, Page and Brin each held \~3% of Alphabet's total shares . The rest of the equity is widely held by institutional investors. The top common shareholders are the big index fund firms: **The Vanguard Group**owns about 7.25%, **BlackRock** \~6.3%, and **State Street** \~3.4% . Other notable holders include Fidelity (2.1%) and T. Rowe Price (1.7%) . No outside shareholder has more than 10% due to the founders' majority. These large institutions generally vote in line with management on routine matters.

Given the voting control of insiders, **shareholder proposals** from outside investors have not passed - the founders can block any proposal they dislike. For instance, at the 2025 Annual Meeting, all 13 stockholder proposals (on topics like AI ethics, lobbying disclosure, etc.) were rejected, since Page and Brin's votes (and management's recommendations) carry the day . Even an outside proposal that won a majority of non-insider shares (such as a call for an independent audit of certain policies) can be defeated by the insiders' block. This essentially means **Alphabet's public shareholders have minimal direct influence** on governance via voting. However, there have been instances where high independent vote support sends a message: e.g. in 2022 a shareholder proposal on reporting sexual harassment policies got significant outside support (though still failed). Large institutional investors like BlackRock and Vanguard typically follow proxy advisors and have at times supported proposals on sustainability or governance reforms at Alphabet - but knowing these won't pass, their votes are largely symbolic. Notably, in 2022-2023 some institutional investors urged Alphabet to curb costs and layoffs (TCI Fund Management wrote an open letter in 2022 calling for expense reductions) . While not a formal vote, this investor pressure did align with actions Alphabet later took (job cuts, slower hiring).

**Voting & Activism:** No traditional activist investor can build a controlling stake due to the dual-class structure. The co-founders have **executed their voting rights to maintain control** - for example, they have consistently reelected the board's nominees and voted against all outside proposals. One proposal that regularly appears is to sunset the dual-class structure, but it too is voted down by the insiders. The top institutional holders generally vote in favor of management on director elections and say-on-pay (executive compensation) - for instance, all 10 board nominees were re-elected in 2025 with over 96% of overall votes . However, if counting only non-insider shares, some proposals (like one in 2023 on lobbying disclosure) garnered nearly half of independent votes , signaling outside investors' concerns even if outcomes are foregone conclusions.

In short, Alphabet's governance is effectively an **insider-controlled democracy**. Public shareholders' most powerful recourse is selling their shares if unhappy; they can't easily force change via votes. The upside is that management can make long-term decisions without fear of short-term shareholder rebellion. The downside is a lack of traditional accountability - something many large shareholders accept as the price of investing in Alphabet's exceptional business. So far, there hasn't been a shareholder revolt severe enough to cause Page/Brin to reconsider the structure. They did, however, introduce a quarterly dividend in 2023 (more on that below), showing a willingness to return cash when appropriate.

**Capital Return Policy - Buybacks and Dividends:** Alphabet historically favored **share buybacks** as its means of returning capital, and only recently initiated a cash dividend. The company began repurchasing shares in a meaningful way in 2015, and has ramped up repurchases dramatically over the past decade. In 2019, the board authorized its first major buyback program ($25 billion) . This grew to $50B authorizations in 2021 and **$70 billion in 2022** . In April 2023, Alphabet again authorized a new **$70 billion share repurchase** plan , signaling continued commitment to returning cash. Over 2015-2023, Alphabet repurchased approximately **$343 billion** of its stock , significantly reducing the share count (from \~705 million Class A+C equivalent shares in 2014 to \~610 million by 2024, a \~13% reduction) . The buybacks accelerated especially in 2018-2022: for example, in 2020 Alphabet bought back $31.1B (nearly 4% of shares) , and in 2021 it bought $50B (5.6% of shares) . These repurchases have helped support Alphabet's share price and offset dilution from employee stock grants.

Comparing current vs past: Alphabet's **2023-2025 buyback pace (\~$15B per quarter)** is far larger than a decade ago (in 2015 it spent under $3B on buybacks). The chart of shares outstanding shows a steady decline: 2018 had \~705M Class A+C shares, 2024 had \~611M . This consistent reduction (about 2-3% fewer shares each year recently ) boosts EPS growth. Alphabet management has stated buybacks are their preferred method to return excess cash (given lack of large acquisitions to spend cash on). Indeed, Alphabet had **$118B in cash & marketable securities** on its balance sheet at 2024's end , and continues to generate over $60B in free cash flow annually, enabling these large buybacks without leverage.

In addition to buybacks, Alphabet in 2023 introduced its first-ever **cash dividend**. The company began paying a quarterly dividend of $0.15 per share in Q3 2023 and increased it to $0.21 in Q2 2025 . For full-year 2024, dividends totaled $0.60 per share, about $7.6B paid out . In April 2025, the board raised the quarterly dividend by 5% to $0.21 . Still, the dividend is a small portion of free cash flow (payout ratio &lt;10%). The **primary capital return tool remains buybacks**.

**Shareholder Perspectives:** Major institutional holders generally support Alphabet's capital allocation. They appreciate that Alphabet returns cash once it has covered capital investment needs. The introduction of a dividend was welcomed as it widens the shareholder base to income-oriented investors, though the yield is low. Some investors (e.g. TCI Fund) pushed for more aggressive buybacks when Alphabet's stock dipped in 2022, arguing the company was under-leveraged and could afford to repurchase even more shares given its cash war chest and cash generation . The effect: in 2022, shares outstanding fell \~3.2%, and a further \~2.1% drop in 2023 .

One should note that despite large buybacks, the **founders' voting control is undiluted** (they hold Class B shares not publicly traded). So the repurchases primarily reduce A and C float. BlackRock, Vanguard and others benefit proportionally, but their voting power in percentage doesn't increase relative to insiders.

In terms of **voting on capital return matters**, there's little contention. The board's decision to start a dividend did not require a shareholder vote. Shareholders have not proposed anything like a special dividend or changes to buyback strategy, likely trusting management's approach. Alphabet's philosophy is clearly to **return excess cash** (beyond what they need for ambitious R&D and capex) in a steady manner. Over the last ten years, this has meant a cumulative reduction of share count by roughly 15% and now a small dividend kicker. The pace of buybacks tends to mirror free cash flow generation: for example, in 2021-2022 the company devoted nearly 100% of its free cash flow to repurchases , which was a very large amount (buyback yield around 2%).

In summary, **Alphabet's top shareholders** are predominantly passive index funds with limited direct engagement, though some active funds like Chris Hohn's TCI (1.1% stake) or Baillie Gifford have voiced opinions. None have executed votes that override management due to the voting structure. All significant votes at shareholder meetings align with management's recommendations - the founders and board's stance prevails. The stockholder proposals with the highest outside support in recent years include requests for reports on sustainability, antitrust risk, or equal voting rights, but all were defeated when counting the insiders' votes . Each of the big institutions has its own guidelines (often supporting governance reforms in principle), but in practice, **Alphabet's insiders steer the ship**.

**Capital Returns Outlook:** Going forward, Alphabet is likely to continue heavy buybacks and modestly grow the dividend. With over $50B net cash even after dividends and buybacks in 2024, and $70B+ FCF expected annually, the company has ample capacity. Unless a major acquisition emerges (which seems unlikely given antitrust climate), shareholders can expect Alphabet to keep repurchasing \~$60-70B of stock each year in the near term - a significant return of capital that should further shrink the float by a few percent annually. The dividend, while currently small, could grow in double-digit percentages annually without straining resources, if management aims to signal confidence and share success with investors. Alphabet's capital return track record demonstrates a balance: investing aggressively in growth while returning truly excess cash to shareholders, a strategy generally applauded by its investor base.

## **Buffett‑Style Checklist**

Warren Buffett's criteria for a "great company at a fair price" can be applied to Alphabet's financials:

**1. Valuation Discipline:** Buffett prefers not to overpay - typically looking for P/E ≤15 and P/B ≤1.5 if possible, and insisting on a **margin of safety** (paying at least 25% below intrinsic value). By these measures, Alphabet has generally failed the strictest valuation tests in recent years. Its P/E ratio has rarely been at or below 15; over the past decade, P/E averaged in the mid-20s (currently \~23x for 2024) . Only briefly during market panics (e.g. 2022's tech slump) did Alphabet approach a P/E near \~18-19 , still above 15. Similarly, its Price/Book has been 4x-7x, far above Buffett's 1.5 threshold. This is partly because Google's book value is understated (it doesn't capture powerful intangible assets like its search algorithm, brand, or network effects). But purely on those multiples, Alphabet looked expensive to a classic value lens. For instance, in late 2022 when the stock fell under $90, it traded around 4.4x book and \~19x earnings - cheaper than its history but not a deep-value bargain.

However, considering **intrinsic value** via discounted cash flows, one could argue Alphabet sometimes offered a margin of safety. During the 2022 dip, when the market cap was \~$1.1T, Alphabet's free cash flow yield was about 5.5% (FCF \~$60B on $1.1T) and growing, which some viewed as an attractive entry (25% below some analysts' fair value estimates around $150/share) . In general though, Buffett's rule of thumb - P/E under 15 - has not been met by Alphabet in the last 10 years, reflecting its status as a high-growth franchise. Thus, on **valuation** Alphabet didn't strictly pass Buffett's low-multiple criteria (except perhaps in rare moments). It has usually traded at a premium, which Buffett might normally shy away from. That said, its exceptional business quality could justify a higher multiple (Buffett has paid up for quality in cases like Apple). If one calculates a conservative intrinsic value for Alphabet (using reasonable growth and a 10% discount rate), the stock in late 2023 appeared roughly fairly valued, not *25% below* intrinsic - so the classic margin of safety wasn't obviously present until possibly the 2022 trough. Overall: **Valuation** - *FAIL* (Alphabet's P/E &gt;15 and P/B &gt;1.5 in most periods; margin of safety not consistently present at market prices).

**2. Cash Generation & Allocation:** Buffett likes businesses that consistently generate **free cash flow** and use it wisely. Alphabet strongly passes the FCF tests. It has been **free-cash-flow positive every year** of the past decade . Even in its lowest FCF year (2018) when heavy capex temporarily depressed free cash, it still produced \~$22B FCF. Generally, FCF grew along with profits - reaching $72.8B in 2024 (21% of revenue) and never dropping below 16% of revenue in the last 10 years . This easily exceeds Buffett's bar of "FCF &gt;5% of revenue" - Alphabet's FCF margin has averaged \~22% (far above 5%) . So **Free Cash Flow** - *PASS*, Alphabet gushes cash.

The **allocation** of that cash has also been shareholder-friendly. Buffett looks for a high **Return on Retained Earnings**(RORE ≥12%). We can approximate RORE by seeing how effectively retained profits yield growth in earnings. From 2015-2024, Alphabet retained essentially all earnings (no dividends until late 2023). Over that period, EPS grew from $1.19 (2015) to $8.13 (2024) . We can examine a shorter period to avoid one-offs: say 2018-2023. In 2018, EPS was $2.21; by 2023 it was $5.84 , an increase of $3.63. Total earnings retained 2019-2022 summed to \~$15.7 per share (since no dividends). That implies a return on those retained earnings of about **23%** ($3.63 gained / $15.7 retained). By Buffett's 12% yardstick, this is excellent. Another lens: Alphabet's equity grew by $162B from 2015 to 2024 (retained earnings added), and annual net income increased by \~$84B over that span - that's roughly a 52% return on the cumulative $162B reinvested, albeit over many years. More simply, **RORE** - *PASS*, Alphabet clearly earns well above 12% on reinvested capital.

Moreover, management started returning some cash via buybacks and a small dividend once excess cash accumulated, indicating rational capital allocation. Buffett would approve of the buybacks executed at reasonable prices (they repurchased stock steadily, including more when price dipped in 2022). Alphabet's free cash flow generation is enormous and consistent, and they have avoided value-destructive acquisitions, instead returning cash. This aligns with Buffett's preference that surplus cash be returned if it can't be reinvested at high returns internally. Alphabet's Cloud investments now even yield operating profit, suggesting internal reinvestment is paying off. In summary, on **Cash Generation & Allocation** - *PASS*, Alphabet produces ample free cash and allocates it effectively (high RORE, sensible buybacks).

**3. Growth & Predictability:** Buffett likes steady, **predictable growth in earnings and book value**. Here, Alphabet's record is mostly positive but with a couple of volatile spots. It did *not* increase EPS every single year (one of Buffett's ideal criteria). Notably, **2017 EPS fell \~35%** (from $1.42 in 2016 to $0.91 in 2017 ) due to a one-time tax charge - a non-operational issue, but it breaks the streak. Similarly, **2022 EPS fell** (from $5.80 in 2021 to $4.56 in 2022 ) as revenue slowed and costs grew. So it fails "EPS up every year for 10 years." However, excluding unusual items, the underlying earnings trend has been strongly upward. From 2013 to 2024, net income grew at \~21% CAGR. Buffett would prefer no down years, but Alphabet's drops were due to extraordinary factors (tax law, economic downturn) rather than fundamental decline. Still, on the strict **EPS predictability** - *FAIL* (not a smooth upward line each year).

Book value per share, which Buffett often uses as a proxy for intrinsic value, grew impressively - from \~$8.68 in 2015 to \~$26.62 in 2024 . That's about a 13.6% CAGR, and importantly, **no year of book value per share decline**. Even in years when net income dipped, book value still rose because profits were positive and retained. Alphabet's 10-year CAGR in book value/share (\~13.6%) exceeds Buffett's 10% hurdle, and it had **zero years of contraction in shareholder's equity**. So on this measure, *PASS*.

Another Buffett metric is **Owner's Earnings Yield** (OE / Market Cap). Owner's Earnings can be approximated by free cash flow, which for Alphabet is consistently positive. Buffett might look for OE yield ≥ 10% (which is like P/FCF ≤10). Alphabet's OE yield has not been that high in the past decade; for instance, in 2024 FCF \~$72.8B on $2.32T market cap is \~3.1% yield, far below 10%. Even at the 2022 low, FCF \~$60B vs $1.1T cap was \~5.5% yield. So by that measure, Alphabet's price has been too rich to yield 10% owners earnings - *FAIL* on OE/Market Cap ≥10%. But note: Buffett's 10% yield is a very high bar typically found in depressed stocks, not usually in growing behemoths like Alphabet.

In summary, **Growth & Predictability** is a mixed bag: Book value and overall earnings growth are strong (pass), but the lack of an uninterrupted EPS growth streak and a low owners-earnings yield at market prices are *fails*. Alphabet's business is a bit more cyclical than say a consumer staples firm - ad spending can fluctuate - which introduced some variability in annual profits.

**4. Balance Sheet Safety:** Here Alphabet shines. Buffett likes conservative debt levels and strong interest coverage. Alphabet has **minimal debt** - debt-to-equity was just 0.03 in 2024 (only \~$14B debt vs $450B assets, $325B equity) . It has more cash than debt, in fact. So Debt/Equity ≪ 0.5 - *PASS*. Its interest coverage is off the charts: operating income was $112B in 2024, while interest expense on its small debt is on the order of perhaps $0.5B or so, meaning EBIT/Interest well above 100× - far beyond the ≥3× criterion . Even in 2022, EBIT $74.8B and interest expense likely \~$0.6B, coverage \~125×. So *PASS* on interest coverage (and conversely interest expense was only \~0.7% of operating profit in 2024 - *PASS* for interest exp ≤15% of op income ).

Alphabet's **Current Ratio** was 1.84 at end of 2024 (Current Assets $163.7B / Current Liab $89.1B) - comfortably above the 1.5 threshold. It has been well above 1.5 for the past decade (often &gt;4 in earlier years when cash piled up and liabilities were low). So *PASS* on liquidity. Buffett would view Alphabet's balance sheet as extremely strong - a fortress with tens of billions in net cash, huge working capital, and no reliance on debt financing.

In summary, **Balance-Sheet Safety** - *PASS* across the board. Alphabet's financial position is very conservative, providing a margin of safety against hard times.

**5. Profitability & Efficiency:** Buffett prefers high margins and returns on capital, as evidence of a durable competitive advantage. Alphabet performs excellently here. Its **Gross Margin** has been \~55-60% historically , easily above a 40% benchmark - *PASS*. (For instance, 2024 gross margin 58.2%, 10-yr avg \~57%.) **Net Profit Margin** has averaged \~21% and often exceeded 20% - for example 28.6% in 2024, 24.0% in 2023 . Only a couple times it dipped near 20% (21.2% in 2022, 11% in 2017 anomaly). Generally, Alphabet maintains net margins in the 20-30% range, indicating strong pricing power and efficiency - *PASS* on the ≳20% net margin criterion.

**Return on Equity (ROE)** - Buffett seeks ≥15% average over 10 years. Alphabet's ROE was volatile but high on average. In early years it hovered 15-20% (2015-2016 \~16%), dipped to \~9% in 2017 (tax hit), then surged to 30%+ in 2021, and was \~26% in 2023 . The 10-year average ROE (excluding one-offs) is roughly in the low 20s%. That's well above 15%. Even the lowest year (2017 at 9%) was an outlier; the business's true earning power ROE was much higher (if adjusting 2017 for the one-time charge, ROE would have been \~18%). So *PASS* on ROE average ≥15%.

**Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)** - Alphabet's ROIC (with cash excluded) has also been strong, typically in the high teens or higher. Using NOPAT vs (Equity + Debt - Cash), Alphabet's 10-year average ROIC is estimated around 20%+. For example, in 2023: NOPAT \~ $69B (net income plus tax-adjusted interest) on invested capital (\~$283B equity + $12B debt - $115B cash) ≈ 26% ROIC. Even lowering for more cash, it's above Buffett's \~12% hurdle. So *PASS* on ROIC.

**Return on Assets (ROA)** - Alphabet's ROA has consistently been &gt;6%. In 2024, ROA = 22.2% ($100B net / $450B assets). Even in slower 2022, ROA \~16.7%. The only dip was 2017's 6.4%, but aside from that one year, ROA ranged \~11-22%. Clearly *PASS* on ROA ≥6%.

Finally, Buffett pays attention to capital intensity: **Depreciation vs Gross Profit**. Buffett prefers businesses that do not require heavy capex relative to their gross earnings (Depreciation ≤10% of Gross Profit, implying not too much must be reinvested to maintain operations). Alphabet in recent years has increased depreciation as it invests in data centers, but it remains a moderate portion of gross profit. In 2024, depreciation was $15.3B vs gross profit $203.7B, \~7.5%. In 2023, \~$11.95B depreciation vs $174.1B gross profit, \~6.9% . Earlier in the decade, depreciation was smaller (e.g. \~$5B in 2015 on gross profit \~$46B, \~11%). At times around 2015-2016 it may have been just at 10% of gross profit, but as an average, Alphabet stays around 5-8%. That's a good sign that while it invests heavily, it isn't eroding the economics - *PASS* on Depreciation ≤10% of Gross Profit. (Alphabet's capex spending has been high, but much of that is growth capex; depreciation, reflecting maintenance of existing capital, is reasonable relative to gross profit.)

**Summary:** Alphabet meets most of Buffett's quality metrics: high margins, high returns on capital, gushing free cash, strong balance sheet. The main knocks are on the **valuation side** - it usually isn't available at a cheap earnings or book multiple - and the fact that its earnings growth, while robust in the long run, has had an occasional down year (2017, 2022). In Buffett's terms, Alphabet clearly has a durable competitive advantage (moat) evidenced by its high ROE and margins, and is run conservatively with lots of cash and little debt. The question for a Buffett-style investor would be price: one might have needed to wait for moments of market pessimism (like 2022) to get Alphabet at a comfortable margin of safety. At current levels (around 23x earnings), it may be above what classic Buffett metrics deem a "value," but considering the business quality, some investors (including Berkshire's lieutenants, who bought Amazon in the past) might stretch those rules. Overall, Alphabet would rate highly on Buffett's checklist for **business excellence**, but only intermittently on **valuation attractiveness**.

## **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis**

To estimate Alphabet's intrinsic value, we perform a DCF analysis under three scenarios: **Conservative**, **Nominal**, and **Optimistic**. We will project Alphabet's future free cash flows based on different growth assumptions derived from its historical free cash flow (FCF) growth rates over the past decade, then discount those cash flows to present value. We use a 10% discount rate (reflecting a desired long-term return or hurdle rate often cited by Buffett) for all cases. We'll model 10 years of FCF and then a terminal value, and finally apply a 25% margin of safety to suggest a "buy price."

**Historical FCF Growth:** Alphabet's free cash flow has grown substantially over the last 10 years, though not uniformly. In 2013, FCF was around $11B, and by 2024 it reached $72.8B . Year-to-year growth ranged from as high as +57% (e.g. 2021 surge) to slight declines (e.g. -10% in 2022 when profits dipped and capex rose). The **lowest FCF growth** observed was about **-10%** (a decline of 10% from 2021 to 2022). The **average annual growth** was very high - roughly **\~21%** (as an arithmetic average of yearly changes, excluding outliers). The **highest growth** year was +57% (2021's jump in FCF as revenues boomed post-COVID). These will inform our scenarios:

-   **Conservative Case:** We assume FCF growth is at the *lowest* end of historical experience. Given one year was negative, we'll use **0% FCF growth** per year (essentially flat free cash flow going forward). This is very conservative as it implies Alphabet's cash flow will stagnate at current levels (\~$73B annually) despite likely continued global digital growth. It factors in potential permanent margin pressures or slower revenue growth. Essentially, Alphabet's future cash flows are treated as a perpetuity roughly equal to the current FCF.

-   **Nominal Case:** We use the approximate *average* FCF growth rate of the past decade. We observed around 21% average, but such a high rate may not sustain as the company has grown massive. For a more normalized "nominal" scenario, we might moderate it slightly (the geometric CAGR from 2014-2024 FCF is even higher, \~26%, due to small starting base). However, to follow instructions strictly, we'll take **+21%** annual FCF growth for projection. This assumes Alphabet can continue a strong growth trajectory (driven by AI, Cloud, new monetization) albeit such growth will make its cash flows enormous in a decade (likely optimistic but we'll analyze it).

-   **Optimistic Case:** We use the *best* historical growth - about **+57%** per year - which was achieved in a unique rebound year. Sustaining 57% for 10 years is virtually impossible for a $70B FCF company (it would imply trillions of FCF by year 10). It's an extremely aggressive scenario, perhaps reflecting if Alphabet found *multiple new massive revenue streams* (or unprecedented AI-driven profit boosts). We'll run the numbers with 57% for a few years, but realistically we might taper it. However, to illustrate a truly bullish case, we'll first calculate with 57% for all 10 years (noting this is not realistic, but per instruction). Later we'll adjust for sanity.

**DCF Calculation Assumptions:**

-   Starting FCF (year 0, end of 2024) = **$72.76 billion** (actual 2024 free cash flow, approximated from cash from ops $125.3B minus capex $52.5B ).

-   Growth rates per scenario as above for years 1-10.

-   Terminal growth rate after year 10 = **2.5%** (approx long-term GDP/inflation growth, a standard conservative choice).

-   Discount rate = **10%** (reflecting a desired return).

-   All cash flows are treated as end-of-year and discounted to end of 2024 present value.

Let's calculate each scenario:

**Conservative Scenario (0% growth):**

Here we assume FCF stays at \~$72.76B each year for the next 10 years (no growth). After that, for terminal value, we assume 2.5% perpetuity growth.

-   Years 1-10: FCF each year = $72.76B. The present value of a 10-year annuity of 72.76 at 10% = 72.76 \* \[1 - (1+0.10)^-10\] / 0.10 = $446.5 billion (approximately).

-   Terminal Year (end of year 10): FCF in year 11 = $72.76B \* (1+2.5%) = $74.6B. Terminal value = $74.6B / (10% - 2.5%) = $994.6B. Present value of terminal = $994.6 / (1+0.10)^10 = \~$383.9B.

-   Sum of PVs = $446.5B + $383.9B = **$830.4 billion** enterprise value.

Since Alphabet has minimal net debt (it actually has net cash, which theoretically would add to equity value, but we can consider enterprise value ≈ equity value given net cash is small relative), we'll treat this as equity value. Approximately $830B for the whole company. Dividing by 12.21 billion shares (A + B + C total) gives an intrinsic value per share of about **$68**.

Applying a 25% margin of safety: 0.75 \* $68 = **$51** per share as a "buy at or below" price.

Interpretation: Under flat FCF, the DCF suggests Alphabet's intrinsic value is \~$68/share (far below the current \~$190).
