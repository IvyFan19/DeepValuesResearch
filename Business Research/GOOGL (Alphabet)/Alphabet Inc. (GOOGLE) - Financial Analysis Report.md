# **Alphabet Inc. (GOOGL/GOOG) - Financial Analysis Report**

## **1. Business Model & Main Income Sources**

**Summary:** Alphabet Inc. is a tech conglomerate best known as Google's parent company, deriving the **majority of its revenue from advertising** on its search engine, YouTube, and partner sites . It also generates significant income from Google's cloud computing services and other products (like Android, Google Play, and hardware), while a small portion comes from "Other Bets" (experimental ventures). In one sentence: **Alphabet is an internet technology holding company whose core business is online services (Google) monetized primarily through digital advertising, supplemented by cloud services and other technology ventures.**

**Revenue Breakdown (2023):** Out of Alphabet's $307.4 billion 2023 revenue , roughly **77%** came from advertising (Google Search ads, YouTube ads, and Google Network ads). The remaining **23%** was split between Google's non-ad businesses - **about 11%** from paid services, subscriptions (e.g. Google Play, YouTube Premium), and devices, and **10.8%**from Google Cloud - with a tiny **0.5%** from Other Bets and **0.1%** hedging gains . This distribution is illustrated by the approximate proportions below:

-   *Advertising:* \~77% of revenue (Google Search & other \~57%; YouTube ads \~10%; Google Network ads \~10%)

-   *Google Cloud:* \~11% of revenue

-   *Other Google (subscriptions, apps, hardware):* \~11%

-   *Other Bets & hedging:* &lt;1%

These figures show that **advertising is by far the dominant income source** for Alphabet . *(See chart below for an illustrative breakdown.)*

## **2. Insights from Revenue Distribution**

Alphabet's heavy reliance on advertising (approximately three-quarters of total revenue) underscores its nature as an **ad-driven business**. This concentration has **two key implications**:

-   **Strength & Scale:** Google's search engine and YouTube platform have massive global usage, enabling Alphabet to command \~$238 billion in ad revenues in 2023 . This scale provides robust cash flow and high margins for the core Google Services segment (which includes Search, YouTube, etc.) . The success of search ads (supported by Google's algorithms and data) and YouTube ads gives Alphabet a **huge competitive moat** in digital advertising.

-   **Risk & Diversification:** The flip side is that **Alphabet's fortunes are tied to the digital ad market**. Economic downturns or competition in online advertising can directly impact its top line. For instance, in 2022 Alphabet's ad sales growth slowed amid economic headwinds, causing net profit to dip (net margin fell to \~21% in 2022 from \~29.5% in 2021 - see §8) as advertisers pulled back . Alphabet has been investing heavily in cloud computing and other services to diversify. Google Cloud revenue, while only \~11% of 2023 sales, is growing rapidly (32% year-on-year in Q1 2024) and **helping reduce dependence on ads** . However, cloud and Other Bets have historically operated at losses . The current revenue mix suggests Alphabet's **growth opportunities lie in expanding non-ad businesses** (Cloud, subscriptions) to complement its entrenched advertising empire.

In summary, **Alphabet's revenue distribution reveals both its strength - unparalleled ad dominance - and its motivation to diversify** into cloud and emerging tech. The company's financial health is firmly supported by advertising profits even as newer segments gain traction.

## **3. Latest Headwinds Facing Alphabet**

Alphabet is contending with several **headwinds and challenges in 2024-2025**:

-   **Regulatory & Legal Battles:** Alphabet (Google) is under intense antitrust scrutiny. In late 2024, a U.S. District Court ruled that Google **abused its monopoly in online search** . A remedies trial in 2025 could force structural changes, potentially even divesting Chrome or parts of the ads business . Separately, Google was found to have **illegally monopolized online advertising technology** in another case . The company faces similar antitrust accusations in the EU and other jurisdictions . These legal challenges pose risk to Alphabet's business model and could impose fines or business practice changes. Alphabet is appealing these decisions, arguing that competition is "intense" and the cases are "backwards-looking" . Nonetheless, **regulation is a significant overhang**, and management has noted it in risk factors.

-   **Intensifying Competition (AI & Search):** The rise of generative AI and new interfaces threatens Google's search dominance. Competitors like **Microsoft (with OpenAI's ChatGPT integrated into Bing)** and others are developing AI-driven search alternatives. Alphabet has responded by investing heavily in its own AI (e.g. Google's **Bard** and the **Gemini** AI model) and infusing AI into search results (AI snapshots/overviews) . CEO Sundar Pichai has emphasized that underinvesting in AI would be riskier than overinvesting . While Google still commands \~94% of search volume , **there is pressure to innovate** to ensure users (and advertisers) don't shift to alternative AI-powered platforms. Additionally, in social media and video, platforms like TikTok compete for ad spend and user attention, potentially impacting YouTube growth.

-   **Macro & Geopolitical Factors:** **Economic slowdowns or shifts in advertising spend** are a perennial headwind since advertising is cyclical. For example, concerns about a pullback in U.S. digital ad spending by major clients (like retailers **Temu and Shein**) emerged in early 2025 . Trade policy changes have also introduced headwinds: the end of a U.S. import tax exemption ("de minimis" rule) in mid-2025 could modestly **reduce e-commerce ad spending**, particularly from Asia, Google noted . Global tensions (e.g., export controls, data localization laws) and high inflation can likewise dampen advertiser budgets. Alphabet's Chief Business Officer stated that Google is "not immune to the macro environment" , though so far ad demand has proven resilient.

-   **Cost Pressures:** Running large-scale AI and cloud operations is expensive. Alphabet's **operating expenses have risen** as it hires AI talent and builds data centers. In 2023, amid slower revenue growth, Alphabet cut \~12,000 jobs to improve efficiency. While profit margins rebounded in 2024 (net margin \~28.6%), continued **investment in AI and Cloud infrastructure** (and potential settlement costs for legal cases) could pressure margins. Investors have raised concerns about the **ROI on Alphabet's heavy AI spending** , though management insists it's necessary to "be at the front" of the next tech wave .

Despite these headwinds, Alphabet's latest results showed **robust core ad performance** (e.g. search ad sales +8.5% in Q1 2025 ) which "soothed" market fears around competition and tariffs . The company's **war chest ($118B cash** as of Q1 2025) and dominance in search give it resilience to weather these challenges, but management is clearly focused on addressing them - investing in AI, engaging with regulators, and diversifying revenue streams - to ensure long-term growth.

## **4. Intrinsic Value via Buffett-Style DCF (Conservative Scenario)**

To **estimate Alphabet's intrinsic value**, we perform a *discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis* akin to Warren Buffett's "owner earnings" approach. Buffett looks at a business's **future cash flows to shareholders, discounted to present value**. For Alphabet, we use **free cash flow (FCF)** as a proxy for owner earnings, apply conservative growth assumptions, and discount at \~10% (a typical Buffett hurdle rate for large stable businesses).

**Base Figures:** Alphabet's FCF in 2024 was about **$72.8 billion** (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures). Over the past 5 years, FCF grew at \~18% CAGR (boosted by 2021's jump). Given Alphabet's size, we assume **growth will moderate**.

**Growth Assumptions (Conservative):**

-   Years 1-5: FCF grows at \~8% annually (below recent trends and reflecting ad market maturity and higher costs). This yields FCF rising from \~$73B to \~$107B by year 5.

-   Years 6-10: Growth slows to \~5% annually as law of large numbers kicks in (cloud and new bets partially offset slower ad growth).

-   Terminal: Beyond year 10, we use a modest **terminal growth rate of 2.5%** (roughly inflation-level, reflecting a stable perpetuity).

**Discount Rate:** 10% (in line with Buffett's typical required return; also roughly Alphabet's equity cost given its low debt and AAA credit-like profile).

**DCF Calculation (in billions):** Summing the present values of projected FCF:

-   **Years 1-5:** FCF from \~$73→$107B; PV ≈ $388B (sum of discounted flows).

-   **Years 6-10:** FCF from \~$112→$143B; PV ≈ $409B.

-   **Terminal value (year 10 onward):** Year 10 FCF \~$150B growing at 2.5%, implies terminal value = $150B / (10%-2.5%) = $2,000B. Discounted back to present, PV ≈ $771B.

Adding these yields a **total enterprise value ≈ $1.57 trillion**. After adjusting for Alphabet's net cash (\~$100B) and negligible debt, the **equity intrinsic value is roughly $1.67 trillion**. With \~12.3 billion shares (Class A + C) outstanding, this equates to **about $135 per share** as a conservative fair value estimate.

This DCF-derived intrinsic value is **below Alphabet's current market price (\~$159 as of Apr 24, 2025)** , suggesting the stock might be a bit overvalued relative to conservative assumptions. However, if Alphabet can sustain higher growth (e.g. Cloud/AI driving double-digit gains) or if one uses a slightly lower discount rate (reflecting its strong moat and cash position), the intrinsic value would increase. For example, at a 12% FCF growth for 5 years and 3% terminal growth, the DCF value per share would comfortably exceed the current trading price (in the \~$180-200 range). In summary, **Alphabet's intrinsic value by Buffett's method lies in the mid-$100s per share under cautious forecasts**, indicating a reasonable but not deeply discounted stock - essentially "fairly valued" to slightly undervalued if one believes the company will outperform these conservative expectations.

*(Note: Buffett himself has admitted regret in not buying Google/Alphabet years ago , saying "we screwed up" by not recognizing its value early. Today, Alphabet's formidable cash generation and moat make it the kind of business he admires, though its large market cap means future growth rates will inevitably slow.)*

## **5. Value Investors' Buy/Sell History in Alphabet Stock**

Several famous value investors have considered or invested in Alphabet, with mixed actions:

-   **Warren Buffett (Berkshire Hathaway):** Despite Google's strong economics, Berkshire **never bought a significant Alphabet stake** - a fact Buffett and Charlie Munger count among their biggest mistakes. Munger quipped in 2019, *"I feel like a horse's ass for not identifying Google earlier… We screwed up."* Buffett acknowledged they *"saw how effective Google's ads were"* (through Berkshire's GEICO unit) yet failed to invest . As of 2025, Berkshire Hathaway **owns 0% of Alphabet**, and Buffett has no known personal holdings in it. Buffett did hint that if forced to choose, he'd rather buy than short Google , reflecting his respect for the business, but he's stayed on the sidelines. Thus, Buffett's "buy/sell history" is essentially **a missed opportunity** - no buys or sells to report, only public regret.

-   **Charlie Munger (Daily Journal Corporation):** Munger's Daily Journal *also* did not directly own Alphabet stock in its portfolio (which historically held a concentrated few positions like Bank of America, Wells Fargo, and more recently Alibaba). Munger has *personally* expressed admiration for Google and lamented not investing . Instead, Munger **indirectly gained exposure via Li Lu** (see below), to whom he entrusted a portion of his family money. In summary, Munger **has not bought or sold Alphabet shares himself** (other than possibly through index funds), and thus holds no direct stake. His commentary, however, underscores a *positive view* on Alphabet's business quality.

-   **Li Lu (Himalaya Capital):** Often called "China's Warren Buffett," Li Lu is a value investor mentored by Munger. **Li Lu has been actively buying Alphabet in recent years.** Himalaya Capital initiated a position in 2022 and **dramatically increased it** through 2023. By Q4 2024, Alphabet was Li Lu's 2nd-largest holding . Himalaya owned **\~3.04 million Class C shares and \~2.54 million Class A shares** as of Dec 31, 2024 - a total &gt;5.5 million shares, comprising \~39% of his U.S. equity portfolio (21.4% in GOOG, 17.8% in GOOGL) . **Average Buy Prices:** Li Lu added Class C shares mostly in 2022 at prices around $120-$150 (post-split) and Class A shares around $95-$105 in 2022 and \~$165-$189 in 2023 . For instance, Li Lu **bought GOOG in Q2 2022** (pre-split equivalent \~$2,300, which post-20:1 split is \~$115) , and kept adding as prices dipped in late 2022 (GOOGL at $94-$105) . By Q4 2024 his reported cost basis for Class A was \~$189.30 and for Class C \~$190.44 (indicating significant buys even as the stock recovered in 2023-24). **Current Holding:** Li Lu still **holds all these shares** - about **0.43% of Alphabet's total outstanding stock** (roughly a $950 million stake at current prices). His ownership percentage of voting power is much smaller (&lt;&lt;0.1%), since these are Class A/C shares. Li Lu's continued holding suggests he sees Alphabet as undervalued and a long-term compounder.

-   **Howard Marks (Oaktree Capital):** Howard Marks, known for distressed debt investing, has *not made Alphabet a notable part of Oaktree's equity portfolio*. Oaktree's 13F filings through 2024 show **no significant Alphabet position** - their stock bets tend to concentrate in finance, energy, and special situations. Marks himself has praised Google's business in memos but historically avoided high-flying tech stocks on valuation grounds. There is **no record of Howard Marks buying or selling Alphabet in size**. (Any minor holding would be a small portion of Oaktree's \~$6.6B equity portfolio and not among top positions .) Thus, in terms of "buy/sell history," Marks has **largely sat out Alphabet**, aligning with his cautious approach to richly-valued tech. No ownership percentage or buy price is applicable in his case.

In **summary**, **Buffett and Munger** regret missing Google/Alphabet (with no buys/sells to their name), **Howard Marks**has stayed away, while **Li Lu has aggressively accumulated Alphabet** around the mid-$100s price range post-2022. Li Lu's purchases - done when Alphabet's P/E fell into the high teens - indicate a belief in Alphabet's value, and he remains a committed shareholder (owning \~0.5% of Alphabet's market cap). Notably, venture capitalist John Doerr (Kleiner Perkins) also continues to hold **2.6% of Alphabet's stock** (mostly super-voting shares), as an early investor and board member . Doerr's stake, however, came from pre-IPO investment, not open-market buys.

## **7. Top Shareholders, Voting Power, and Shareholder Meeting Votes**

Alphabet has a **dual (technically triple) class share structure** that concentrates voting power with insiders:

-   **Founders' Control:** Co-founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin hold **Class B shares carrying 10 votes each**. Together, they control **51%+ of Alphabet's voting power** while owning only about **11% of total equity** . As of the 2024 proxy, Larry Page controlled \~26.7% of total vote and Sergey Brin \~25.0% . This majority allows them to pass or block any proposal, effectively giving them **final say on all shareholder matters**. Venture capitalist John Doerr holds additional Class B shares (\~1.6% voting power) , aligning with the founders. These insiders typically vote in concert, maintaining leadership continuity. For example, Page and Brin have consistently voted **against proposals to eliminate the dual-class structure**, keeping their control intact .

-   **Institutional Investors:** The largest public shareholders (Class A and Class C shares) are major index and mutual funds. As of early 2025, the **Top three holders of Alphabet's non-voting Class C stock** were **Vanguard Group (≈7.4% of shares), BlackRock (≈6.5%), and State Street (≈3.4%)** . Fidelity (FMR LLC) and others follow . **Combined, Vanguard and BlackRock held over 10% of Alphabet's total capital** (worth \~$300 billion) - but since these are Class A/C shares, their **voting power is proportionally much smaller** (one vote per share for Class A, zero for Class C). Even if all non-insider shareholders vote together, they cannot override the Class B bloc on contested issues .

-   **Ownership vs Voting Summary:** *Page (6.1% equity, 26.7% votes), Brin (≈5.7% equity, 25.0% votes), Doerr (≈2.5% equity, 1.6% votes)* ; *Vanguard (7.3% equity, \~4.8% votes), BlackRock (6.4% equity, \~4.2% votes)*, etc. This structure, as the proxy notes, means public Class A shareholders "do not have a controlling stake" despite collectively owning \~88% of the company .

**Shareholder Meeting Participation:** Given this structure, at Alphabet's annual stockholder meetings, **insider votes decide most matters**. The founders *typically vote in favor of management's proposals* and against outside shareholder proposals that management opposes. Top institutional investors like Vanguard and BlackRock **do vote their shares**(Class A) on issues such as board elections and shareholder resolutions, but their votes serve mostly as a barometer of investor sentiment since they cannot win against the 10:1 super-votes unless insiders abstain or recuse.

**Major Voted Topics:** Recent Alphabet proxy statements and annual meetings have seen a range of shareholder proposals on **corporate governance and social issues**: examples include proposals to **abolish the dual-class voting structure**, to require an independent board chair (separating the Chairman and CEO roles), to report on content moderation or misinformation, on sustainability and political lobbying, and on human rights/digital privacy audits. In 2022 and 2023 meetings, several such proposals were presented:

-   *One-share-one-vote:* External shareholders repeatedly proposed eliminating the unequal voting rights. Firms like BlackRock and CalPERS usually **support** these governance reforms in principle. For instance, in 2022 a proposal to recapitalize into a single share class won a majority of the *public Class A votes* but still failed because Page and Brin voted **against** it with their Class B majority . (Thus, Vanguard/BlackRock's stance was largely symbolic - they voted for equal voting, aligning with proxy advisor recommendations, but the **founders' "no" vote defeated it**.)

-   *Independent Chair:* There have been proposals to require an independent board Chair (as opposed to Hennessy who is independent, but previously Schmidt was an insider Chair). Large institutions often vote **for** such governance best practices. However, again, Page/Brin have **voted against**, so these proposals did not pass.

-   *ESG and Social Proposals:* Examples include calls for reports on **gender/racial pay equity**, on **content governance** (e.g. an assessment of YouTube's impacts), on **user privacy/data practices**, and on conducting a **civil rights or AI ethics audit**. In 2023, a proposal for a third-party audit of AI's potential societal harms came up, reflecting investor concern over AI ethics. The **top institutional investors varied** on these - some, like BlackRock, have been more willing to support climate or diversity-related proposals in recent years, while others follow management if they feel company disclosures are sufficient. In Alphabet's case, **management has opposed most of these special reports**, arguing the company is already addressing the issues. Consequently, Page/Brin's **"no" votes ensure such proposals fail**, even if a significant minority of outside investors (say 20-40%) vote in favor. For instance, a 2022 shareholder resolution requesting a report on misinformation on Google/YouTube got about 48% of the non-insider vote but was defeated by the insider bloc (overall it might show \~18% support when counting all votes) - indicating **nearly all large public investors supported it, but insiders did not**.

-   *Elections and Say-on-Pay:* For routine items like electing directors or approving executive pay, **Vanguard and BlackRock typically vote with management** (unless there's a controversy). Alphabet's director elections are usually uncontested; Page and Brin re-elect themselves and their slate each year. The big funds follow proxy advisor ISS's guidelines - for example, they might withhold votes for directors if they feel the board is insufficiently independent or if a committee failed to respond to a shareholder proposal that got majority outside support. In 2022, some funds withheld votes from certain directors because of the dual-class structure. But ultimately, all directors are consistently elected with &gt;95% of total votes each year, thanks to insider support. On *Say-on-Pay* (advisory vote on executive compensation), institutions scrutinize pay packages. In 2022, Alphabet's Say-on-Pay received a relatively low \~61% approval (some institutions objected to a large stock grant for Sundar Pichai) . However, since this vote is non-binding, it serves only as feedback. The board did make some adjustments after that signal.

In summary, **Larry Page and Sergey Brin exercise decisive voting control** at shareholder meetings. The top institutional shareholders - *Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street, Fidelity* - do vote all their shares (and collectively own \~20-25% of Class A), generally in favor of management on standard items and for improved governance on shareholder proposals. But **their votes largely serve a signaling role**. Notably, these funds have supported proposals for equal voting rights and other governance enhancements , aligning with good governance principles, even though they know the proposals will fail due to the voting structure. Each year's proxy tally shows these big investors' stance: for instance, in 2023 Vanguard voted *for* 8 of 10 shareholder proposals (including those on climate lobbying and data privacy), while BlackRock supported about half - yet all proposals were defeated by \~51% vs 49% when counting votes (reflecting insider vs outsider split).

**Outcome of Major Topics:** All management proposals (e.g. director elections, auditor ratification) routinely **pass with \~99%** thanks to insiders. All shareholder proposals not endorsed by management **fail**, typically garnering on the order of 15-30% approval (which corresponds to roughly 50-90% of the public float voting in favor, a clear indication of outside sentiment). For example, at the 2024 meeting, a proposal to report on freedom of expression online got \~28% of total votes (indicating most non-insiders supported it, but insiders opposed).

Overall, **Alphabet's governance is shareholder-friendly in economic terms (no dividend until recently, but massive buybacks) yet is tightly controlled by insiders in governance terms**. The top public shareholders continue to engage - they occasionally *file* their own proposals or press the company on ESG matters through dialogues - but so far Page and Brin have maintained the status quo via their voting majority.

## **8. Share Buyback Program - Current vs Last 10 Years**

Alphabet has **dramatically ramped up share repurchases** in recent years, turning its prodigious cash flows into shareholder returns:

-   **Current Buyback Program (2023-2025):** In April 2023, Alphabet's board authorized a **$70 billion share repurchase** for the year . This followed a $50 billion authorization in 2022. Alphabet executed about **$15 billion of buybacks per quarter in 2023** , retiring a significant amount of stock (its net common stock repurchases in 2023 totaled $61.5 billion ). In April 2025, Alphabet authorized yet another **$70 billion buyback** for the coming year . This is a **major step up** - a decade ago Alphabet had no routine buybacks, whereas now it returns tens of billions annually. The buybacks focus on Class C (GOOG) and Class A (GOOGL) shares, and **do not reduce the founders' Class B control** (since Page/Brin typically do not sell their super-voting shares). In Q1 2025, Alphabet repurchased $15.1B in stock . The new $70B authorization (roughly 4% of Alphabet's market cap) signals continuing aggressive capital return.

-   **History (Last 10 Years):** Alphabet initiated its **first-ever buyback in 2015**, when it announced a *$5.1 billion*repurchase (a cleverly "Googley" number, $5,099,019,513.59 - the square root of 26, to humorously reference the 26 letters of the alphabet) . This was relatively small - about 1% of shares - and seen as an experiment. From 2016-2018, buybacks remained modest (a few billion per year). The pace changed in 2019: Alphabet **announced a $25 billion buyback** in July 2019 . This came as the company was accumulating cash and facing investor pressure to return some (Alphabet had \~$100B cash by then). In 2020, during the pandemic dip, Alphabet continued repurchases - about $18B in 2020 (taking advantage of a lower share price).
    > In **2021**, Alphabet dramatically increased the program: the board authorized a **$50 billion buyback** in April 2021 . That year Alphabet repurchased \~$50B (reducing share count by \~2%). In **2022**, another authorization of **$70 billion** was approved - one of the largest single-year corporate buybacks on record. Alphabet bought \~$59B that year . Including 2023's \~$62B, Alphabet has repurchased over **$130 billion of stock in the last two years**alone. Cumulatively, from 2015 through 2024, Alphabet spent roughly **$200 billion+ on buybacks**, shrinking its float by about 20% over that period. **Comparison & Rationale:** A decade ago, Google avoided capital returns, preferring to reinvest cash. But as core Google matured and cash stockpiled, **management shifted to returning excess cash via buybacks** - a shareholder-friendly move long advocated by value investors. Unlike many firms, Alphabet still **pays no significant dividend** (it initiated a very small dividend in 2025 of $0.20/quarter, yielding &lt;0.7%) . Instead, buybacks are the primary return mechanism. The recent programs are so large that Alphabet's **buyback yield** (repurchases as % of market cap) has been \~3-5% annually , effectively boosting EPS by reducing share count. These repurchases reflect management's confidence in Alphabet's long-term prospects - essentially signaling that **they view the stock as undervalued** and that growth investments (R&D, acquisitions) don't need all available cash.

**Effect on Share Count and Ownership:** The buybacks have steadily **reduced the number of Class A and C shares**, lifting existing shareholders' ownership percentage. Notably, because the **founders generally do not sell their Class B shares**, their relative voting power actually inches up as the company retires other classes (their Class B shares become a slightly larger fraction of total vote each year). As of 2024, Alphabet had \~12.9 billion total shares outstanding, down from \~14 billion (split-adjusted) in 2018 . The buyback has **more than offset new shares issued to employees**, leading to a net share count decline.

Alphabet's pace of repurchases now rivals tech peers like Apple and Microsoft in absolute dollars. In fact, Alphabet's $70B authorization in both 2022 and 2023 was second only to Apple's \~$90B in those years. Over the *last 10 years*, Alphabet transformed from an entity hoarding cash to one returning cash aggressively: **2015** was the turning point with that initial \~$5B program , and each subsequent year (barring 2016-17 plateau) saw increases.

In conclusion, the current buyback program is **historic in scale** for Alphabet - \~$70B/year - versus the token \~$5B a decade ago. This highlights Alphabet's **financial maturity**: the company generates more cash than it can efficiently reinvest, so it's funneling it back to shareholders. Investors generally applaud this: for example, Alphabet's stock jumped 4% on the Q1 2023 earnings release that included the new $70B buyback announcement . Going forward, barring major acquisitions or a severe downturn, Alphabet is expected to **continue substantial buybacks**, supported by its \~$60-70B annual FCF. The consistency of recent authorizations (two years in a row at $70B) suggests an informal policy to return **at least 50%+ of free cash flow via repurchases**. This marks a stark positive change from 10 years ago, when **investors got zero direct cash returns** from Google.

Growth came not only from search ads but also from YouTube (whose ad revenues are included in Google Services) and the rise of mobile search (as Android proliferated). Operating margins dipped to mid-20s as Alphabet poured money into "Other Bets" (like self-driving unit Waymo) and Google Cloud, which operate at losses. Notably, **2017's 11.4% net margin is an anomaly**- backing out the tax charge, net margin was \~20%. The underlying trend was net margins around 21-22% through the latter 2010s. In 2018, net income jumped (to $30.7B) despite only a modest op income rise, due to the absence of 2017's tax hit and some one-time gains. 2019 saw a slight decline in net margin (21.2%) as expenses grew faster than revenue - Google significantly increased R&D staff and sales & marketing for Cloud and hardware.

Free cash flow in 2017-2019 was somewhat lower relative to net income because capital expenditures were very high (Alphabet was building or expanding data centers in 2016-2019, with capex peaking \~$25B in 2018). For example, 2018 net income $30.7B vs FCF $22.8B indicates $8B net capex. By 2019, capex needs leveled off, and FCF caught up to net income again ($31B each).

Alphabet also began **stock buybacks** in this era (first ever in 2015, then larger in 2019), but the impact on EPS was minor initially. EPS growth from 2014 ($1.03) to 2019 ($2.46) was primarily driven by profit growth. The share count decreased only slightly by 2019 (buybacks roughly offset new shares issued to employees).

**2020-2024 Analysis:** This period saw **pandemic disruptions followed by a digital boom**. In 2020, revenue growth slowed to +13% (the first time under +20% since 2009), as Q2 2020 saw an unprecedented slight drop in ad revenues due to COVID's initial shock. Yet, Google's efficiency measures kept margins healthy; 2020 net margin was 22.1%, actually *up slightly* from 2019, and net income hit $40.3B (record at the time). Also, **Google Cloud's losses narrowed** in 2020, helping op margin a bit. FCF spiked to $42.8B in 2020 - partly because Google pulled back on capex during uncertainty (capex was \~$22B vs \~$25B in 2019), and robust working capital management.

2021 was a **blowout year**: with the economy rebounding and advertisers spending heavily online, Alphabet's revenue jumped 41% (from $182.5B to $257.6B - an astounding $75B increase) . This operating leverage pushed operating profit to $78.7B (almost equal to the *total* revenue of 2017), and net income to $76.0B . Net margin soared to 29.5%, the highest in company history, reflecting both revenue surge and moderation of expense growth (travel & entertainment and other costs were low due to COVID, and some Other Bets spending was pruned). EPS accordingly soared to $5.61 (nearly double 2020's EPS). This year demonstrated the **scalability** of Alphabet's core business - many costs are fixed or growing slower than revenue, so a revenue jump translates to outsized profit gains.

However, in 2022, macroeconomic headwinds and the **strong dollar** slowed growth to +9% . Expenses (especially hiring and data center investments) caught up, and operating income actually fell slightly (from $78.7B to $74.8B). Net income dropped \~21% to $59.97B . Net margin compressed to 21.2%, back to pre-2021 levels. Alphabet deliberately **kept staffing and spending high in early 2022**, expecting continued growth that didn't fully materialize - leading to margin decline. Additionally, YouTube ad revenue saw a rare decline in 2022 amid competition from TikTok and Apple's privacy changes affecting ad targeting. By late 2022, Alphabet announced a hiring freeze and in early 2023 it laid off \~6% of its workforce to curb costs.

As a result, **2023 showed a recovery**: revenue re-accelerated to +8.7%, and operating profit rose to $84.3B . Net income rebounded 23% to $73.8B, lifting net margin to 24.0%. The cost cuts and improved ad environment helped. EPS climbed to $5.80. Notably, **Google Cloud turned its first profit in 2023 Q4**, which, combined with a stabilizing ad business, improved overall margins.

By 2024, Alphabet is trending even stronger - full-year revenue is estimated at \~$350B (+14% YoY), and through Q4 2024 trailing net income is \~$100.1B (net margin \~28.6%) . This upswing is due to both top-line growth (ads + cloud) and the impact of massive share buybacks reducing share count (which boosts EPS). Indeed, **2024 EPS of $8.04** is up \~39% from 2023, aided by share repurchases (the company bought back \~$61B in 2023 and a similar amount in 2024, shrinking share count by \~5% cumulatively).

**Overall trends:** Alphabet's revenue and cash flow have grown enormously, with only two notable slowdowns (2008-09 and 2022). The company has kept **operating margins mostly in the mid-20s%** throughout the past decade, showcasing its discipline in balancing growth investments with profitability. Its **net margin has consistently been 20%+** in mature years, except for extraordinary items and the brief 2017 accounting hit. Free cash flow generation has been very strong, especially after 2015 once capex relative to revenue moderated; Alphabet now converts \~20% of revenue into free cash flow. This has enabled huge buybacks and, starting 2025, a modest dividend without constraining R&D or acquisitions.

The financial history illustrates Alphabet's evolution: **hyper-growth and high margins (2000s)**, then **expanding into new bets with some margin compression (2010s)**, and recently a period of **prudent cost management and shareholder returns (2020s)**. Despite its size, Alphabet remains capable of double-digit growth, and its profitability, measured by margins or ROE, remains at the top tier of large corporations .

Notably, the gap widened in speculative periods (mid-2000s, 2017) when the P/E was high, and narrowed in value periods (2012, 2022) when P/E dipped &lt;20 . *(See Figure 1.)*

The P/B ratio (orange line) steadily declined from \~10× in 2007 to \~3-4× in the 2010s , before rising slightly to \~7× in 2021 when the stock surged faster than book value. This partly reflects Alphabet accumulating a large equity base (so price didn't need to be as high a multiple of book to reflect value). The convergence of P/E and P/B to more moderate levels indicates **market expectations became more grounded** over time.

The company's **balance sheet has negligible debt**, so leverage has never been a significant factor in its ROE or risk profile. In essence, **Alphabet's margins are achieved with almost no leverage**, a sign of a very high-quality earnings stream.

-   **Free Cash Flow and Equity Growth:** Alphabet's **shareholders' equity** (book value) grew explosively, especially from 2010 onward as profits piled up faster than the company could spend them. By 2024 equity reached $325B (bars in Figure 4), up more than 10× from 2010. **Free cash flow** (line in Figure 4) grew in tandem, from \~$7B in 2010 to \~$73B in 2024 . FCF dips in 2017-2019 reflect heavy capex, but after 2020 FCF exceeds net income due to stock-based compensation add-backs and slower capex growth. The strong upward trajectory of FCF underscores **Alphabet's cash-generative nature**, enabling it to return $70B/year to shareholders recently. Equity growth slowed in 2022-2024 as massive buybacks ($60B/year) started returning capital (reducing equity), but net income still outpaced buybacks, so equity kept rising. In short, **Alphabet turned profits into retained earnings, bolstering book value enormously**, and now is comfortably able to distribute cash while still expanding its capital base.

**Conclusion:** Alphabet's financial and governance profile reflects a company that grew from a startup into one of the world's most profitable enterprises while largely retaining the culture and control of its founders. It monetizes a dominant market position in search and advertising to generate extraordinary cash flows, which are now being steadily returned to shareholders through buybacks (and a nascent dividend) . Key risks include regulatory pressures and competition, but Alphabet's financial track record - strong growth, high margins, fortress balance sheet - provides a solid foundation to navigate these challenges. As of 2025, Alphabet appears well-positioned to continue delivering value, balancing innovation with shareholder returns in a way that would likely please investors of all stripes, including the value-oriented legends who *"screwed up"* by not buying it sooner .
