## Analysis: Li Lu's Keynote Address at Peking University (Circa April 2025)

In late April 2025, reports surfaced detailing a wide-ranging and philosophical keynote address delivered by Li Lu at Peking University.<sup>1</sup> This speech served as a roadmap for value investors confronting a challenging global environment, marked by significant economic and geopolitical uncertainties. It represents a major recent articulation of his investment framework and market outlook. Several key themes emerged from this address and the subsequent Q&A session.<sup>10</sup>

### Theme 1: Navigating Turbulent Times - Macro Acceptance, Micro Focus

A central tenet of Li Lu's message was the imperative for investors to distinguish between uncontrollable macro factors and controllable micro analysis: "The macro is what we must accept; the micro is where we can and should make a difference".<sup>1</sup> He acknowledged the significant challenges facing the global economy, referring to "predicaments of our era," including issues like rising youth unemployment, deflationary pressures, and a strained global order.<sup>1</sup> These concerns echo previous observations regarding the potential consequences of unprecedented monetary policies, rising debt levels relative to GDP, historically high asset prices, and escalating geopolitical tensions, such as trade conflicts.<sup>12</sup>

However, rather than advocating for attempts to predict or time these macro shifts-an endeavor he implicitly views as fraught with difficulty given the inherent unpredictability of the future <sup>13</sup>-Li Lu urged investors to accept the macro environment as a given. The practical implication is to concentrate intellectual energy and analytical effort on understanding individual companies and their intrinsic value. This approach represents a strategic compartmentalization of risk. By acknowledging macro headwinds but focusing analytical resources on the "micro"-company fundamentals, competitive advantages, management quality-investors can maintain discipline and act rationally based on knowable factors, rather than becoming paralyzed or making reactive decisions based on unpredictable macro events. It fosters a strategic calm, directing effort towards the sphere where diligent work can yield an edge.

### Theme 2: Redefining Wealth and the Imperative of Equity Ownership

Li Lu presented a specific definition of wealth crucial to his investment philosophy: "The essence of wealth is the proportion of purchasing power in the economy".<sup>1</sup> This definition moves beyond nominal currency amounts to focus on real economic command. In an environment characterized by inflation, currency oversupply <sup>12</sup>, and macroeconomic volatility, he argued that "static wealth, carried in cash, cannot achieve sustainable, compound growth".<sup>1</sup> Holding cash, while seemingly safe, risks a steady erosion of purchasing power over time. He explicitly advised non-professional investors that holding cash is preferable to speculating, but for long-term wealth growth, it is insufficient.<sup>14</sup>

His solution is direct: own "shares of the most dynamic companies in the most vibrant economies".<sup>1</sup> This perspective reframes wealth not as a static pool of money but as a *dynamic participation* in economic value creation. Owning equities, particularly in high-quality enterprises, allows an investor's wealth to grow organically with the economy and potentially faster if the chosen companies outperform the average.<sup>11</sup> This view elevates equity ownership from a mere investment option to a fundamental strategy for preserving and enhancing real wealth over the long term, acting as a necessary defense against the potential debasement of static currency holdings.

### Theme 3: Core Principles of Value Investing

Li Lu distilled his investment approach into core principles, largely rooted in the tradition of Benjamin Graham and influenced by mentors like Charlie Munger.<sup>1</sup> These principles form the operational bedrock of his strategy:

1.  **Stock as Part Ownership:** Viewing a stock not as a fluctuating ticker symbol but as a fractional ownership interest in an underlying business is fundamental.<sup>1</sup> This owner's mindset encourages analysis based on business fundamentals rather than market sentiment. He suggested analysts imagine inheriting the business to cultivate this perspective.<sup>14</sup>

2.  **Mr. Market:** The market should be regarded as a temperamental partner offering opportunities to buy low and sell high, not as a source of wisdom ("Mr. Market is here to serve value investors, not guide").<sup>11</sup> This allows rational investors to exploit volatility rather than be driven by it.

3.  **Margin of Safety:** Investments must possess a significant buffer between the purchase price and the estimated intrinsic value.<sup>11</sup> This is a crucial risk management strategy <sup>15</sup>, protecting against analytical errors, unforeseen events, and the inherent uncertainty of the future. Li Lu views permanent capital loss, not price volatility, as the true investment risk, and the margin of safety is the primary defense against it.<sup>13</sup> Price drops, in fact, can create opportunities.<sup>13</sup>

4.  **Circle of Competence:** Investors must honestly assess the boundaries of their knowledge, clearly understanding what they know and, importantly, what they do not know.<sup>1</sup> A competence without defined borders is not a true competence.<sup>13</sup> Building this circle should be guided by personal interest <sup>10</sup>, and its validity tested rigorously, for instance, by analyzing worst-case scenarios over a decade or defending one's thesis against knowledgeable individuals with opposing views.<sup>10</sup> This principle is deeply tied to intellectual honesty.<sup>13</sup>

5.  **Fish Where the Fish Are:** Investors should seek opportunities in areas where mispricings are more likely to occur or where competition is less intense.<sup>1</sup> This might involve looking at specific industries, geographies, or types of securities that are overlooked or misunderstood by the broader market. The anecdote of Charlie Munger fishing with a local guide illustrates this point: find your specific 'lake' where you have an advantage.<sup>1</sup>

6.  **Wealth as Purchasing Power Share (Li Lu's addition):** He explicitly added his definition of wealth and the goal of value investing as holding shares in dynamic companies within vibrant economies to preserve and grow this purchasing power share.<sup>11</sup> This principle provides the overarching objective for applying the other five.

These principles function as an interconnected system. An investor's "circle of competence" determines where they can reliably estimate intrinsic value and thus ascertain a "margin of safety." Understanding a stock as "part ownership" enables the rational detachment needed to treat "Mr. Market" as a servant. "Fishing where the fish are" guides the search for opportunities where competence and safety margin can be effectively deployed. The ultimate goal, preserving and growing one's share of purchasing power, provides the motivation for rigorously applying this entire framework.

### Theme 4: Lessons from History and Mentors

Li Lu drew inspiration from the history of value investing, noting its origins in periods of significant turmoil.<sup>1</sup> He cited Benjamin Graham developing the methodology during the Great Depression and World War II, and John Templeton's legendary success buying depressed stocks at the outset of WWII in 1939.<sup>1</sup> This historical context serves to validate value investing not merely as a strategy for stable times, but as one particularly well-suited for navigating and potentially capitalizing on uncertainty and distress. By highlighting these examples, he suggests that challenging environments, like the "predicaments of our era" <sup>1</sup>, often create the very mispricings and emotional extremes that disciplined value investors are equipped to exploit. Turmoil, therefore, can be fertile ground for those adhering to the principles.

He also paid explicit tribute to his mentors, particularly Charlie Munger.<sup>1</sup> He shared Munger's fishing anecdote to illustrate the "fish where the fish are" principle.<sup>1</sup> Munger's influence was pivotal in Li Lu's shift from earlier trading strategies to long-term value investing <sup>17</sup>, and Munger demonstrated significant confidence by investing a substantial portion of his family wealth with Li Lu early on.<sup>18</sup> Li Lu also referenced Munger's approach to building mental models through broad curiosity as foundational to his investment framework.<sup>10</sup> Acknowledging this intellectual lineage places his philosophy firmly within the respected tradition of value investing.

### Theme 5: Distinguishing Investing from Speculation

Li Lu emphasized the fundamental difference between investing and speculation.<sup>14</sup> Investing, in his view, involves participating in the long-term value creation of companies, making it a potentially positive-sum game where wealth is generated. Speculation, conversely, focuses on forecasting the behavior of other market participants, rendering it essentially a zero-sum (or negative-sum after costs) game where one participant's gain is another's loss.<sup>14</sup> He observed that long-term track records overwhelmingly favor investors over speculators.<sup>14</sup>

This distinction underscores the philosophical basis of his approach. By defining speculation as focusing on market psychology and predicting others' actions, it inherently connects to the behavioral weaknesses and emotional volatility that Li Lu warns can be exploited by the market <sup>2</sup> and which successful investors must overcome through rationality and patience.<sup>14</sup> Investing, focused on underlying business reality, seeks to insulate the practitioner from these potentially destructive short-term fluctuations. Choosing speculation is thus framed as choosing a path more susceptible to the behavioral errors that undermine long-term compounding.

### Theme 6: Practical Guidance for Investors

The speech and subsequent Q&A offered guidance tailored to different types of investors. For aspiring professionals, particularly those transitioning from other areas like the sell-side, he stressed the difficulty but necessity of a mindset shift towards long-term ownership and intrinsic value analysis, while acknowledging that accumulated industry knowledge remains valuable.<sup>10</sup> He highlighted passion and genuine interest as critical differentiators for success, suggesting failure often stems from a lack thereof.<sup>10</sup> Rigorous self-assessment-such as the "worst-case scenario" test and seeking counterarguments-is essential.<sup>10</sup> He also advised focusing analytical efforts, suggesting investors should eventually subtract companies from their research list, not just continually add them due to fear of missing out.<sup>14</sup>

For non-professional investors, Li Lu offered several concrete suggestions <sup>14</sup>:

-   **Index Investing:** He endorsed index funds as a sensible approach, cautioning investors to choose indices that accurately represent the underlying economy (e.g., favoring the S&P 500 over potentially sector-skewed indices like China's CSI 300).

-   **Outsourcing:** Alternatively, non-professionals can delegate management to carefully selected professional managers who demonstrate a genuine long-term orientation and operate within their stated circle of competence.

-   **Cash vs. Speculation:** Holding cash is explicitly preferred over engaging in speculation, which he views as a losing proposition long-term.

-   **Realistic Expectations:** Investors should avoid chasing high returns in areas they don't fully understand, instead accepting potentially lower but more sustainable returns (e.g., 6-7% compounded over many years) from investments within their comprehension.

The demanding nature of his advice for professionals-requiring deep understanding, passion, intellectual honesty, rigorous self-critique, and acknowledging the scarcity of true value investing mentors <sup>10</sup>-implicitly reinforces the logic behind recommending simpler, passive strategies or careful delegation for the majority of investors. The bar for successful active value management is set exceptionally high.

### Theme 7: Key Insights from the Q&A Session

The question-and-answer session provided further nuance <sup>10</sup>:

-   **Mindset and Passion:** The difficulty of adjusting one's investment mindset was reiterated, alongside the critical role of genuine interest driving the necessary deep work. Competency must be combined with passion.

-   **Testing Understanding:** The "worst-case scenario" and "seek counterarguments" tests were presented as practical tools for validating one's understanding and combating psychological biases.

-   **Learning Process:** Value investing is best learned organically by following personal interests, allowing knowledge to accumulate over time.

-   **Moat Durability:** The primary source of a company's competitive advantage (moat) depends on the time horizon; industry characteristics tend to be more decisive over the very long term, while management quality might be more impactful in the shorter term.

-   **Analyzing Entrepreneurs:** Assessing leadership requires context. The focus should be on understanding *why* a specific entrepreneur was successful in building a *specific* business, rather than applying generic checklists.

-   **Career Path:** Li Lu acknowledged the practical difficulty for aspiring value investors in finding suitable mentors or positions at authentic value firms, suggesting self-learning and networking as viable alternatives.

-   **Munger's Framework:** Charlie Munger's mental model framework was described as stemming from a lifelong, broad curiosity about how the world works, developed even before his investment career.

## Connecting the Speech to Li Lu's Established Investment Doctrine

The themes articulated in the Peking University speech demonstrate remarkable consistency with Li Lu's long-established investment philosophy. His emphasis on value orientation, a long-term investment horizon <sup>15</sup>, the paramount importance of intellectual honesty <sup>13</sup>, and the focus on identifying high-quality companies possessing sustainable competitive advantages (moats) and run by capable, motivated management teams <sup>15</sup> are all hallmarks of his approach. The principles of circle of competence and margin of safety, central to the speech <sup>1</sup>, have always been cornerstones of his methodology.<sup>13</sup>

Therefore, the speech should be interpreted not as a significant shift in doctrine, but rather as a reaffirmation and application of his enduring philosophy to the specific challenges and opportunities presented by the current global environment-the "predicaments of our era".<sup>1</sup> The core message remains unchanged, but the emphasis is adapted to provide guidance relevant to contemporary concerns, such as navigating volatility and justifying equity ownership amidst macro uncertainty. The clear intellectual lineage tracing back through Munger and Buffett to Graham is reinforced <sup>1</sup>, positioning his framework within the classical value investing tradition.

## Conclusion

While no public statements or activities by Li Lu were identified specifically for April 6, 2025, his keynote address at Peking University, reported later that month, provides significant insight into his investment philosophy and market perspective during that period. The speech delivered a message of disciplined optimism amidst global turbulence.

Key takeaways from his address include:

-   **Acknowledge Macro, Focus on Micro:** Investors should accept broad economic and geopolitical conditions as largely uncontrollable and concentrate their analytical efforts on understanding individual businesses within their circle of competence.

-   **Wealth as Purchasing Power:** True wealth is defined by its command over real goods and services, necessitating ownership of dynamic productive assets (equities) for long-term growth and preservation, especially in inflationary or volatile environments.

-   **Adherence to Value Principles:** Rigorous application of core value investing tenets-viewing stocks as business ownership, using Mr. Market opportunistically, demanding a margin of safety, knowing one's limits (circle of competence), and seeking less competitive arenas-is crucial.

-   **Learn from History and Mentors:** Value investing has proven resilient, particularly during challenging times, and the wisdom of predecessors like Graham, Templeton, and Munger provides valuable guidance.

-   **Invest, Don't Speculate:** Focus on long-term value creation through business ownership rather than attempting to predict short-term market movements or others' behavior.

Li Lu's message emphasizes rationality, patience, intellectual honesty, and a deep commitment to understanding the underlying businesses. His highly concentrated portfolio, focused on established financial institutions and dominant technology companies, serves as a concrete example of his high-conviction approach, directly reflecting the principles he espouses. For investors navigating complex modern markets, his framework advocates prioritizing depth of understanding over breadth of coverage, maintaining emotional discipline, focusing resolutely on the long-term goal of growing real purchasing power, and being rigorously honest about the boundaries of one's own knowledge.
