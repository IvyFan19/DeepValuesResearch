# Harvard University's 2025 Bond Issuance: Navigating Unprecedented Financial Headwinds

## 1. Executive Summary

Harvard University, a globally recognized institution with the highest credit ratings, executed a significant debt issuance in April 2025, notably the Series 2025B taxable bonds totaling USD 750 million. This issuance comprises two tranches: USD 450 million maturing in 2030 with a 4.887% coupon (US740816AS29) and USD 300 million maturing in 2036 with a 5.259% coupon (US740816AT02).<sup>1</sup> Officially, the proceeds are designated for "general corporate purposes" and issuance costs.<sup>2</sup> However, the timing and nature of this taxable bond sale strongly indicate that its primary driver is "ongoing contingency planning" in direct response to an escalating and unprecedented financial dispute with the U.S. federal government.<sup>3</sup>

The federal administration has frozen over USD 2.2 billion in research grants and contracts vital to Harvard's operations and has further threatened to halt all new federal research funding.<sup>5</sup> Concurrently, Harvard faces demands for sweeping structural reforms and a challenge to its long-held tax-exempt status.<sup>7</sup>

For investors, Harvard's bonds offer the advantage of association with a premier, AAA/Aaa-rated institution, providing a degree of perceived safety and potentially attractive yields in the taxable market.<sup>1</sup> However, these are counterbalanced by substantial and unique risks. The ongoing political interference poses a direct threat to Harvard's financial stability and operational autonomy. The potential loss of its tax-exempt status carries profound implications for its financial model and the tax treatment of its bonds. Furthermore, the situation has drawn scrutiny under securities laws regarding disclosure adequacy.<sup>7</sup>

The decision by an institution of Harvard's financial caliber to issue substantial taxable debt primarily for contingency planning, rather than for specific capital expansion, signals a significant shift. It underscores a new level of financial risk assessment within the higher education sector, where even the most robust institutions must now actively prepare for politically driven financial shocks. This development has broader implications for how the entire sector may need to approach financial strategy and risk management in an increasingly polarized environment.

## 2. Harvard University's Recent Debt Issuances: Focus on the Series 2025B Taxable Bonds

In early 2025, Harvard University engaged in significant capital raising activities, issuing both tax-exempt and taxable bonds. The most prominent of these, particularly in the context of the university's evolving financial landscape, is the Series 2025B taxable bond issuance.

### 2.1. Detailed Terms of the Series 2025B Bonds (US740816AS29 & US740816AT02)

The President and Fellows of Harvard College announced the sale of USD 750 million of Series 2025B taxable bonds around April 7, 2025, with the bonds being issued on April 9, 2025.<sup>1</sup> This offering was divided into two distinct tranches:

-   **Tranche 1 (US740816AS29):** An aggregate principal amount of USD 450 million, carrying a coupon rate of 4.887% and maturing in 2030.<sup>1</sup>

-   **Tranche 2 (US740816AT02):** An aggregate principal amount of USD 300 million, carrying a coupon rate of 5.259% and maturing in 2036.<sup>1</sup>

Both tranches were sold at an issue price of 100% of their par value.<sup>1</sup> Interest on these bonds is payable semiannually on March 15 and September 15, with the first payment scheduled for September 15, 2025.<sup>2</sup>

The bonds are unsecured general obligations of Harvard University, meaning that bondholders do not have a claim on any specific university assets in the event of default but rather rely on the overall financial strength and creditworthiness of the institution.<sup>2</sup> Reflecting this strength, Moody's Investors Service assigned its highest Aaa rating to the Series 2025B bonds with a stable outlook, and S&P Global Ratings similarly assigned its 'AAA' long-term rating, also with a stable outlook.<sup>1</sup> While an earlier document posting on MuniOS had indicated the bonds were not yet rated, these subsequent agency actions provide the definitive credit assessment.<sup>2</sup>

A substantial syndicate of financial institutions managed the placement of these bonds. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley served as lead underwriters for the USD 750 million taxable offering, with Goldman Sachs acting as the sole bookrunner and Morgan Stanley as co-senior manager.<sup>2</sup> The broader list of bookrunners for the tranches included Barclays, BofA Securities, Drexel Hamilton, Huntington Bancshares, JP Morgan, Loop Capital Markets, RBC Capital Markets, TD Securities, and Wells Fargo, in addition to Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley.<sup>1</sup> The engagement of such a large and diverse group of underwriters is noteworthy. For a debt issuance of this magnitude, particularly amidst heightened public scrutiny of the university's financial situation due to the federal dispute, a broad syndicate helps ensure wider market access and a more effective distribution to a diverse investor base. This approach can maximize the potential for a successful placement and distribute underwriting risk, suggesting a strategic move by Harvard to secure critical funding in what could be perceived as challenging market conditions for the issuer.

The offering was made without registration under the Securities Act of 1933, in reliance upon the exemption provided under Section 3(a)(4) of the Act, which applies to securities issued by non-profit educational institutions.<sup>7</sup> The offering documents were made available via MuniOS.<sup>2</sup>

### 2.2. Context: Series 2025A Tax-Exempt Bonds

To provide a fuller picture of Harvard's recent financing activities, it is important to note the issuance of the Series 2025A tax-exempt bonds in March 2025, shortly before the taxable Series 2025B offering. The Massachusetts Development Finance Agency issued approximately USD 434 million to USD 450 million in revenue bonds on behalf of Harvard University.<sup>3</sup>

These Series 2025A bonds were designated for financing and refinancing specific capital projects, including renovations to undergraduate student housing, various teaching and administrative buildings, research facilities, and the Newell Boathouse. A portion of the proceeds was also used to repay commercial paper that had financed these projects.<sup>14</sup> The bonds were issued in two sub-series (2025A-1 and 2025A-2) and structured as fully-registered, book-entry-only securities, available in denominations of USD 5,000 and integral multiples thereof.<sup>14</sup> Interest on these tax-exempt bonds is payable semiannually on May 15 and November 15 and is exempt from federal income taxes.<sup>14</sup> S&P Global Ratings assigned its 'AAA' rating to this series as well.<sup>15</sup>

The nearly concurrent issuance of tax-exempt bonds for traditional capital projects and a large taxable bond series for general purposes and contingency planning illustrates a sophisticated, multi-pronged financial strategy. Harvard appears to be optimizing its capital structure by using lower-cost tax-exempt financing where IRS regulations permit (i.e., for qualifying capital projects) while simultaneously building a substantial reserve of unrestricted, flexible capital through the taxable issuance to address the more amorphous and urgent challenges posed by the federal dispute. This dual approach underscores the extraordinary circumstances confronting the university.

## 3. Strategic Rationale: Why is Harvard Issuing These Bonds?

Understanding the impetus behind Harvard University's substantial Series 2025B taxable bond issuance requires looking beyond standard financial boilerplate to the extraordinary external pressures confronting the institution.

### 3.1. Stated Purpose vs. Underlying Drivers

The preliminary offering statement for the Series 2025B bonds, as is common for such issuances, indicates that the proceeds are intended "for general corporate purposes" and to cover the costs associated with the bond issuance itself.<sup>2</sup> While this language provides broad latitude, the clear and widely acknowledged underlying driver for this specific USD 750 million taxable debt is the university's strategy of "ongoing contingency planning for a range of financial circumstances".<sup>3</sup> University spokesperson Jason A. Newton confirmed the bond sale is part of evaluating necessary resources to advance Harvard's academic and research priorities amidst these challenging circumstances.<sup>4</sup> Harvard Business School professor Luis M. Viceira described the USD 750 million figure as "sobering," suggesting the university is raising liquidity in anticipation of a "severe funding shortfall".<sup>4</sup> This defensive posture, prioritizing liquidity and operational flexibility, marks a significant strategic pivot from typical university financing, which is more commonly directed towards specific capital expansion projects.

### 3.2. The Federal Funding Crisis as a Primary Catalyst

The primary catalyst for this contingency planning and the consequent bond issuance is an acute and escalating dispute with the U.S. federal government. This multifaceted pressure campaign includes:

-   **Threats to Indirect Research Cost Recovery:** The administration has signaled intent to reduce federal funding for the indirect costs associated with research, a move that could cost Harvard an estimated USD 100 million annually.<sup>3</sup>

-   **Review and Freezing of Existing and Future Funding:** A sweeping review of USD 256 million in Harvard's current research contracts and USD 8.7 billion in long-term federal funding commitments to the university and its affiliated hospitals was announced on March 31, 2025.<sup>3</sup> This escalated to a freeze of over USD 2.2 billion in multi-year grants and USD 60 million in contracts.<sup>5</sup> Furthermore, Education Secretary Linda McMahon announced in May 2025 that Harvard would receive no *new* federal research grants.<sup>6</sup> Federal funding is a significant revenue source, accounting for 10.5% of total revenue in 2023 and USD 686 million in fiscal year 2024.<sup>5</sup> For specific schools like the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, federal research support constituted approximately 46% of its FY25 budget.<sup>19</sup>

-   **Demands for Structural Reforms:** The federal government has issued a series of demands for "lasting, structural reforms" encompassing university governance, disciplinary procedures, admissions and hiring policies (including the dismantling of Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) programs), and the screening of international students.<sup>3</sup> Harvard has unequivocally rejected these demands, asserting they infringe upon its institutional independence and constitutional rights.<sup>9</sup>

These actions collectively create profound financial uncertainty and the specter of substantial operating shortfalls. The bond issuance is a direct measure to ensure Harvard has the liquid resources to maintain operational continuity, cover potential legal expenses related to the dispute, and bridge funding gaps for critical research and academic programs should federal support be withheld or significantly reduced long-term.<sup>3</sup> The timing of the bond issuance, announced on April 7 and priced on April 9, 2025, immediately followed the administration's March 31 funding review announcement and April 3 demands letter, strongly indicating a direct causal relationship and an urgent need to secure these funds.<sup>1</sup> The preliminary offering statement for the bonds explicitly acknowledged these federal communications.<sup>4</sup>

### 3.3. Broader Context: Why Universities Issue Taxable Bonds

While the federal dispute is the dominant factor for Harvard's Series 2025B issuance, universities, in general, may opt for taxable bonds for several strategic reasons:

-   **Flexibility in Use of Proceeds:** Unlike tax-exempt bonds, which are subject to strict IRS regulations tying their use to specific qualifying capital projects that serve a public purpose, taxable bond proceeds can be used with greater flexibility.<sup>4</sup> This is crucial for Harvard given the undefined and potentially broad nature of the financial contingencies it faces.

-   **Attracting a Broader Investor Base:** Taxable bonds can appeal to a different set of investors, including those who do not derive a significant benefit from tax-exempt income (such as pension funds or foreign investors) or those investing through tax-advantaged accounts like 401(k)s or IRAs.<sup>22</sup>

-   **Speed to Market:** In some circumstances, taxable bond offerings can be brought to market more quickly than tax-exempt issues, which may involve approvals from state or local issuing authorities.<sup>25</sup>

-   **Anticipating Tax Policy Changes:** Issuers might use taxable bonds to preempt potential adverse changes in tax law, such as increases in taxes on endowment income or the elimination of the tax exemption for municipal bonds.<sup>13</sup>

Harvard's decision aligns with these general principles, particularly the paramount need for financial flexibility. It is also part of a broader trend observed in 2025, where other elite U.S. universities, including MIT (USD 750 million taxable) <sup>26</sup>, Princeton (USD 320 million taxable) <sup>13</sup>, Stanford (USD 327 million taxable Series 2025A) <sup>30</sup>, and Yale (planning USD 350 million in taxable debt as part of an USD 850 million offering) <sup>25</sup>, have also turned to the taxable bond market. This collective movement suggests a sector-wide response to heightened financial uncertainties, including but not limited to threats to federal funding.

## 4. Investment Analysis: Potential Advantages for Individual Investors

Despite the complex environment surrounding Harvard's recent bond issuance, several factors may present advantages for individual investors considering these securities.

### 4.1. Credit Quality: Harvard's Strong Financial Standing and Premier Ratings

Harvard University stands as one of the most financially robust educational institutions globally. This is reflected in the exceptionally high credit ratings assigned to its debt, including the Series 2025B taxable bonds. Both Moody's Investors Service and S&P Global Ratings have assigned their highest possible long-term ratings, Aaa and AAA respectively, with stable outlooks.<sup>1</sup> These ratings signify the lowest level of credit risk and an extremely strong capacity for Harvard to meet its financial commitments.

This premier creditworthiness is underpinned by several key financial strengths:

-   **Massive Endowment:** As of June 30, 2024, Harvard's endowment was valued at USD 53.2 billion, providing a substantial financial cushion and a significant source of operating revenue.<sup>10</sup>

-   **Significant Liquidity:** The university maintains considerable liquid assets. Barclays' analysts estimated Harvard's liquid assets at USD 14.5 billion.<sup>10</sup> The university's FY24 financial report detailed USD 2.0 billion in liquid short-term investments held outside the endowment for operating purposes and USD 6.1 billion in its General Operating Account investments that could be liquidated if necessary, subject to Corporation approval.<sup>31</sup>

-   **Diversified Revenue Streams:** Harvard's income is derived from multiple sources, including distributions from its endowment (37% of FY24 operating revenue), net tuition and fees (21% of FY24 operating revenue), sponsored research support, and philanthropic gifts.<sup>2</sup>

-   **Consistent Operating Surpluses:** The university generated an operating surplus of USD 45 million in fiscal year 2024 on a revenue base of USD 6.5 billion.<sup>2</sup>

S&P Global Ratings, in its April 2025 report on the Series 2025B bonds, noted that the stable outlook reflects the expectation that Harvard will maintain its exceptional student demand and balance-sheet strength relative to operating expenses and debt over the next two years. While acknowledging that operating performance could be strained due to changes in federal funding, the agency's overall assessment remains highly positive.<sup>11</sup> This robust financial profile, often described as possessing "unparalleled financial depth" <sup>10</sup>, suggests that rating agencies believe Harvard has the capacity to withstand the current political and financial pressures, even if they prove to be significant in the short to medium term. For bond investors, this underlying financial fortitude is a primary attraction, indicating a very high probability of timely principal and interest payments.

### 4.2. Yield and Income Potential

The Harvard Series 2025B taxable bonds offer fixed coupon rates that provide a predictable income stream to investors: 4.887% for the tranche maturing in 2030 and 5.259% for the tranche maturing in 2036.<sup>1</sup> As taxable bonds, their yields are generally structured to be higher than those of comparable tax-exempt municipal bonds to compensate investors for the income taxes they will owe on the interest received.<sup>22</sup>

The attractiveness of these yields must be assessed in the context of the prevailing interest rate environment at the time of issuance (April 2025) for similarly rated corporate or university taxable bonds. For comparison, AAA-rated tax-exempt municipal bonds were yielding roughly 2.7% to 3.9% in March 2025 <sup>35</sup>, and the 10-year U.S. Treasury note yielded approximately 4.33% in early May 2025.<sup>36</sup> The spread of Harvard's taxable bond yields over comparable Treasury yields reflects the market's pricing of Harvard's credit risk plus any specific issuer risks, including the ongoing federal dispute. Barclays analysts noted that Harvard's recent tax-exempt offerings yielded between 2.7% and 3.9%, which compared favorably to taxable alternatives after considering tax implications.<sup>10</sup> AInvest also highlighted a 3.2% yield on Harvard's 2025 tax-exempt bonds as attractive.<sup>10</sup>

While Harvard's unique status as a premier global institution might typically allow it to borrow at slightly more favorable rates (a "prestige premium") than a comparable corporate issuer with the same rating, the current significant political risks could partially offset this effect. This dynamic may result in the yields on these taxable bonds being relatively attractive for a AAA/Aaa credit, as the university likely needed to offer competitive terms to ensure a successful placement amidst the surrounding controversies. The ultimate appeal of these yields will depend on an individual investor's specific tax circumstances and their alternative investment opportunities.

### 4.3. Investing in a Premier Global Institution

An investment in Harvard University bonds is an investment in one of the world's most enduring and prestigious educational and research institutions. The university's global reputation and the exceptional demand for its academic programs are significant intangible assets.<sup>10</sup> Despite the current acute challenges, the long-term viability and societal importance of Harvard are widely considered to be very high. Adam Stern, co-head of research at Breckinridge Capital Advisors, expressed confidence in the university's ability to manage the unusual circumstances, stating, "if anyone can handle it, it's this university".<sup>37</sup> For investors with a long-term horizon, the opportunity to align with such an institution can be a compelling factor beyond pure financial metrics.

## 5. Investment Analysis: Key Risks and Disadvantages

While Harvard University's bonds carry the highest credit ratings, the current environment presents a unique and significant array of risks that potential investors must carefully consider. These risks extend beyond typical market fluctuations and issuer-specific financial performance, delving into unprecedented political and legal challenges.

### 5.1. The Federal Government Dispute: An Unprecedented Challenge

The most prominent risk is the ongoing and escalating dispute with the U.S. federal government. This conflict has manifested in several critical ways:

-   **Frozen and Threatened Research Funding:** The administration has frozen over USD 2.2 billion in multi-year research grants and USD 60 million in contracts previously awarded to Harvard.<sup>5</sup> There are further threats to pull an additional USD 1 billion in health research funding.<sup>38</sup> Compounding this, Education Secretary Linda McMahon announced in May 2025 that Harvard will receive *no new* federal research grants.<sup>6</sup> This is highly significant as federal funding constituted 10.5% of Harvard's revenue in 2023 and amounted to USD 686 million in FY24.<sup>5</sup> Certain schools, like the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, are heavily reliant on this income, with federal research support making up about 46% of its FY25 budget.<sup>19</sup>

-   **Operational Impact:** The funding freeze has already led to the interruption of vital research projects, including studies on tuberculosis, chemotherapy side effects, the effects of space travel on human cells, coronavirus pandemic preparedness, neuroscience related to aging and neurodegenerative diseases, and the SMART IRB system for patient safety oversight in multi-site medical studies.<sup>40</sup> This disruption jeopardizes scientific progress, risks the loss of years of work, and puts researchers' salaries and positions at risk.<sup>40</sup> In response, Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences (FAS) Dean, Hopi E. Hoekstra, has cautioned faculty to prepare for long-term financial adjustments, including potential staff reorganizations and reductions.<sup>3</sup>

-   **Harvard's Legal and Political Response:** Harvard has firmly rejected the federal government's demands for extensive structural reforms related to university governance, admissions policies (including DEI initiatives), campus protest policies, and the screening of international students.<sup>8</sup> On April 21, 2025, Harvard filed a lawsuit against the Trump administration, asserting that the funding freeze and associated demands are unconstitutional (violating the First Amendment right to academic freedom), contravene the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), and fail to follow the procedural requirements of Title VI of the Civil Rights Act.<sup>16</sup> A court hearing for this lawsuit has been scheduled for July 21, 2025, under an expedited timeline.<sup>44</sup> The American Association of University Professors (AAUP) has also initiated separate legal actions challenging the administration's policies.<sup>45</sup>

The confluence of these factors creates an environment of extreme uncertainty. The actions taken by the federal government are described as "unprecedented" and represent a direct challenge to Harvard's operational stability and academic independence.<sup>6</sup> While Harvard possesses immense financial resources, the scale of the threatened funding cuts and the potential for protracted legal battles introduce a level of risk not typically associated with an issuer of its caliber. An investment in these bonds is therefore, in part, a judgment on Harvard's ability to navigate this complex political and legal minefield.

### 5.2. Threats to Tax-Exempt Status and Securities Law Implications

A further layer of risk stems from the Trump administration's explicit threats to revoke Harvard University's tax-exempt status under Section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code.<sup>7</sup>

-   **Legal Basis and Process:** The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) has the authority to revoke tax-exempt status if an organization's activities are found to be illegal or to violate "fundamental public policy," a principle established in the Supreme Court case *Bob Jones University v. United States* and IRS Rev. Rul. 80-278.<sup>48</sup> While the President cannot unilaterally direct the IRS to revoke an organization's status due to prohibitions under IRC Section 7217 <sup>38</sup>, the administration has reportedly asked the IRS to review Harvard's status.<sup>48</sup> Harvard maintains that there is no legal basis for such a revocation.<sup>49</sup> As of early May 2025, the IRS had not made a public decision, though reports indicated the matter was under consideration.<sup>48</sup>

-   **Implications for Bonds:** The loss of Harvard's tax-exempt status would have severe consequences. It could call into question the availability of the Section 3(a)(4) exemption under the Securities Act of 1933 for *future* bond offerings, as this exemption is predicated on the issuer being organized and operated exclusively for educational purposes and not for pecuniary profit.<sup>7</sup> The current Series 2025B taxable bonds were offered in reliance on this exemption. More directly, if Harvard's overall 501(c)(3) status were revoked, it would fundamentally alter its financial structure and could impact the tax treatment of interest on its *existing tax-exempt* bonds, potentially making them taxable to bondholders.<sup>37</sup>

-   **SEC Scrutiny and Disclosure Adequacy:** Even with the Section 3(a)(4) exemption from registration for the Series 2025B bonds, Harvard remains subject to anti-fraud provisions, specifically Section 12(a)(2) and Section 17 of the Securities Act.<sup>7</sup> Given the intense federal scrutiny and the materiality of the ongoing disputes, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) could take an interest in the adequacy and completeness of the disclosures made in Harvard's Offering Memorandum. Furthermore, state-level securities ("blue sky") laws regarding qualification, registration, and anti-fraud provisions may also apply to the offering and subsequent resales of the bonds.<sup>7</sup>

### 5.3. Market and Issuer-Specific Risks

Beyond the overarching federal dispute, investors should also consider more conventional market and issuer-related risks:

-   **Interest Rate Risk:** As with all fixed-income securities, the market value of Harvard's bonds can be affected by changes in general interest rates. If market rates rise, the price of existing bonds with relatively lower coupon rates may decline.<sup>23</sup>

-   **Secondary Market Liquidity:** While bonds from a high-quality issuer like Harvard are generally sought after, the market for university bonds, particularly specific tranches, can be less liquid than that for U.S. Treasury securities or bonds from very large corporations.<sup>24</sup> Lower liquidity can lead to wider bid-ask spreads, increasing transaction costs for individual investors. The current controversy surrounding Harvard could also influence trading patterns and price volatility. For instance, a portion of Harvard's Series 2025 bonds (maturing in 2055 with a 5% coupon), initially priced at 113.9 on March 18, 2025, was reported to be trading at 106.7 on April 22, 2025, indicating a price decline amidst the escalating federal pressures.<sup>37</sup>

-   **Reputational Damage and Philanthropic Impact:** The prolonged and public nature of the dispute with the federal government, coupled with investigations into issues like antisemitism on campus, could potentially damage Harvard's global reputation and deter philanthropic contributions.<sup>10</sup> The university experienced a 14% drop in total philanthropic contributions during fiscal year 2024.<sup>35</sup> While one report noted a surge of 3,800 alumni donations immediately following the initial funding freeze <sup>52</sup>, Harvard's FY24 financial report indicated that while current use giving was strong, overall fundraising levels might not be sustainable, and the balance of pledges receivable for current-use gifts declined.<sup>31</sup> Education Secretary McMahon has even suggested Harvard should rely more on its endowment and alumni fundraising to cover any federal shortfalls.<sup>53</sup>

-   **Covenants and Default Provisions:** The Series 2025B bonds are unsecured general obligations of the university.<sup>2</sup> Specific details regarding covenants (promises made by the issuer), call provisions (terms under which the bonds can be redeemed before maturity), and events of default would be contained within the full offering memorandum or bond indenture. While some Harvard bonds are callable <sup>55</sup>, comprehensive details for the Series 2025B bonds were not fully available in the reviewed materials.<sup>11</sup>

The federal government's actions against Harvard may also serve as a bellwether for the broader U.S. higher education sector. If such tactics of financial pressure and demands for ideological conformity are successfully applied to an institution of Harvard's stature and resources, it could embolden similar actions against other universities, fundamentally altering the risk landscape for the entire sector. This introduces a systemic political risk that investors in university bonds may need to consider more broadly.

## 6. Acquiring Harvard University Bonds: A Guide for Individual Investors

For individual investors interested in Harvard University's bonds, particularly the recently issued Series 2025B taxable bonds, understanding the acquisition process, market dynamics, and typical investment parameters is crucial.

### 6.1. Availability: Primary vs. Secondary Market

Newly issued bonds, such as Harvard's Series 2025B, are initially sold in the **primary market**. This initial sale is typically managed by an underwriting syndicate composed of investment banks and large brokerage firms, which distribute the bonds primarily to institutional investors (like pension funds, mutual funds, and insurance companies) and high-net-worth individuals.<sup>1</sup>

Once the initial offering is complete, these bonds become "outstanding" and begin trading in the **secondary market**.<sup>1</sup> It is in the secondary market that most individual investors can purchase bonds. Transactions in the secondary market occur between investors, facilitated by brokers, rather than directly with the issuer (Harvard). The Series 2025B bonds, having been issued in April 2025, would now be available for purchase in the secondary market.

### 6.2. Purchase Channels for Individual Investors

Individual investors typically acquire corporate bonds, including taxable university bonds like Harvard's Series 2025B, through the following channels:

-   **Brokerage Accounts:** Most major full-service and discount brokerage firms (e.g., Fidelity Investments, Charles Schwab, E\*TRADE) provide platforms that allow clients to search for, get quotes on, and purchase individual bonds. Fidelity, for example, offers a step-by-step guide for navigating the bond purchasing process on its website.<sup>58</sup>

-   **Online Investment Platforms:** A growing number of online platforms, such as Public.com, also offer access to corporate and government bonds.<sup>55</sup> Some of these platforms may offer innovative features like fractional bond investing.

-   **Financial Advisors:** Investors working with a financial advisor can typically request their advisor to research and purchase specific bonds on their behalf.

### 6.3. Denominations and Minimum Investment

The minimum investment required to purchase a bond can vary:

-   **Tax-Exempt Municipal Bonds:** Harvard's Series 2025A tax-exempt bonds were issued in authorized denominations of USD 5,000 and integral multiples thereof.<sup>14</sup> This is a common denomination size in the municipal bond market.

-   **Taxable Corporate Bonds:** For taxable corporate bonds like Harvard's Series 2025B, the standard face value (par value) per bond is often USD 1,000, and they are typically sold in minimum quantities of one bond (USD 1,000) or sometimes five bonds (USD 5,000), with purchases made in increments of USD 1,000 thereafter.<sup>60</sup> Specific information from Cbonds indicates a face value of USD 1,000 for the Harvard bond US740816AS29 <sup>57</sup> and a minimum settlement amount of USD 1,000 for another outstanding Harvard taxable bond (US7410EAC94).<sup>61</sup> While the official offering documents for the Series 2025B bonds would provide the definitive denominations, this USD 1,000 par value is highly probable.

-   **Fractional Bond Investing:** Some newer platforms, like Public.com, allow investors to purchase fractional interests in corporate and Treasury bonds for as little as USD 100.<sup>55</sup> This significantly lowers the entry barrier for individuals who may not wish to invest the traditional minimum amounts. However, investors should be aware that these fractional shares may have limitations; for instance, Public.com notes its fractional shares are illiquid outside its platform and not transferable.<sup>55</sup> This means that while accessibility is enhanced, flexibility in managing or moving the investment might be curtailed if held fractionally on certain platforms.

Prospective investors should consult their chosen brokerage platform or financial advisor for the precise minimum investment amounts and any associated transaction fees for Harvard bonds.

### 6.4. Holding Structure: Book-Entry Form

Modern bond issuances, including university bonds like Harvard's Series 2025A, are almost universally made in **book-entry-only form**.<sup>14</sup> This means that investors do not receive physical paper bond certificates. Instead, ownership is recorded electronically. The bonds are typically registered in the name of "Cede & Co.," which is the nominee for The Depository Trust Company (DTC). DTC is a central securities depository that holds the master certificates and maintains records of the brokerage firms and banks whose accounts are credited with owning the bonds. Individual investors' ownership is then recorded in the books of their respective brokerage firms. This streamlined, dematerialized system is standard practice for most publicly traded securities in the U.S., including corporate and municipal bonds, and the Harvard Series 2025B taxable bonds would adhere to this structure.

While Harvard bonds are considered high-quality, individual investors seeking to purchase them in the secondary market should be cognizant of potential liquidity. The market for specific university bonds, even from a prominent issuer, may not be as deep or active as the market for U.S. Treasuries or bonds from very large, frequently traded corporations.<sup>24</sup> This can sometimes result in wider bid-ask spreads, which is the difference between the highest price a buyer is willing to pay and the lowest price a seller is willing to accept. A wider spread represents a higher implicit transaction cost. The ongoing controversies surrounding Harvard might also temporarily affect trading activity and price discovery for its bonds.<sup>37</sup> Therefore, it is advisable for individual investors to obtain current price quotes and assess trading volume through their broker before executing a purchase.

## 7. Concluding Assessment for the Individual Investor

Investing in Harvard University's Series 2025B taxable bonds presents a nuanced proposition, characterized by the juxtaposition of exceptional institutional credit quality against a backdrop of unprecedented political and financial challenges.

The bonds are issued by one of the world's most prestigious and financially robust universities, carrying the highest credit ratings (AAA/Aaa) from major agencies.<sup>1</sup> This implies a very low risk of default on principal and interest payments based on Harvard's intrinsic financial strength, supported by a colossal endowment, diversified revenue streams, and a history of sound financial management.<sup>10</sup> The coupon rates offered on these taxable bonds may provide an attractive fixed-income stream, particularly when compared against other high-grade taxable fixed-income alternatives available at the time of investment.

However, these traditional strengths are currently overshadowed by a unique and severe set of risks stemming directly from the ongoing dispute with the U.S. federal government. The freezing of substantial research funding, the threat to halt all new federal grants, demands for sweeping institutional reforms, and the potential challenge to Harvard's long-standing tax-exempt status collectively create a highly uncertain operating environment.<sup>3</sup> These are not typical market or credit cycle risks; they are politically driven and their resolution is difficult to predict, depending heavily on the outcomes of legal battles and shifts in the political landscape.<sup>16</sup>

Therefore, an investment in Harvard's current bond offerings is not merely a traditional credit decision. It also involves an implicit assessment of the university's capacity to navigate and withstand a prolonged period of intense external pressure without suffering irreparable financial or operational damage. While Harvard's vast resources provide a significant buffer, the potential for material adverse effects from these external actions cannot be dismissed.

The situation is dynamic and requires continuous monitoring. Developments related to Harvard's lawsuits against the federal government, any IRS actions concerning its tax-exempt status, further decisions on federal funding, and ongoing commentary from credit rating agencies will be critical indicators for bondholders.

Ultimately, the decision to invest in Harvard's Series 2025B bonds should be carefully weighed against an individual investor's risk tolerance, investment objectives, and time horizon. These bonds may appeal to investors seeking high credit quality who are willing to accept a higher degree of uncertainty tied to non-financial, political factors. The current circumstances may also be viewed as a stress test for the broader Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) investment framework, particularly concerning institutional governance ('G') and the management of complex social issues ('S') under intense political scrutiny. The way Harvard addresses these challenges, and how the market responds, could offer valuable insights for the wider higher education sector and its stakeholders. This report does not offer investment advice but aims to provide a comprehensive analytical foundation upon which an investor can base their own informed decision.
