# Howard Marks: The Investor as Modern Philosopher

## Introduction

Howard Marks, co-founder and co-chairman of Oaktree Capital Management, stands as a towering figure in the investment world.<sup>1</sup> Oaktree, under his guidance, became the largest investor in distressed securities globally, managing vast sums and navigating complex market environments.<sup>1</sup> Yet, Marks' influence extends far beyond the confines of Oaktree's Los Angeles headquarters.<sup>4</sup> Through his widely read client memos, initiated in 1990, and his seminal book, "The Most Important Thing," Marks has emerged as a profound thinker whose insights resonate deeply with investors and decision-makers across disciplines.<sup>3</sup> These writings, brimming with commentary on market dynamics, risk, and investor psychology, offer more than just investment advice; they present a coherent framework for rational thinking and decision-making under uncertainty. This report delves into the core tenets of Howard Marks' thinking, arguing that his contributions constitute a form of practical, modern philosophy - a guide to navigating complexity applicable far beyond the realm of finance.<sup>10</sup>

## Core Philosophy I: The Imperative of Second-Level Thinking

Central to Howard Marks' philosophy is the concept of "second-level thinking".<sup>6</sup> He posits that to achieve superior investment results - results better than the market average - one's thinking must also be superior.<sup>6</sup> Since the market represents the collective actions and wisdom (or folly) of its participants, merely thinking like everyone else will, at best, yield average returns.<sup>17</sup>

Marks contrasts this deeper approach with "first-level thinking," which he describes as simplistic, superficial, and conventional.<sup>18</sup> A first-level thinker might see a company with strong recent earnings and conclude, "It's a good company; let's buy the stock".<sup>18</sup> This approach seeks easy answers and simple formulas.<sup>25</sup>

Second-level thinking, however, is "deep, complex, and convoluted".<sup>19</sup> It goes beyond the obvious. The second-level thinker considers a multitude of factors: the range of possible future outcomes, the probability of each, the prevailing consensus view, how their own expectation differs, and how the current price reflects these differing expectations and the underlying psychology.<sup>7</sup> Returning to the example, a second-level thinker might reason: "It's a good company, but everyone thinks it's a great company, and it's not. So the stock's overrated and overpriced; let's sell".<sup>18</sup> This approach is inherently contrarian, questioning assumptions and looking beyond surface appearances.<sup>18</sup>

Achieving superior results requires not just thinking differently, but thinking *better* - being more right than the consensus.<sup>13</sup> This is difficult because the consensus is often correct.<sup>24</sup> Extraordinary performance, therefore, comes only from holding correct, non-consensus views.<sup>6</sup> This is intrinsically linked to the concept of market inefficiency.<sup>6</sup> If markets were perfectly efficient, reflecting all available information and rational analysis, there would be no opportunity to outperform through superior insight. Second-level thinking is predicated on the belief that markets, being composed of fallible humans driven by emotion, frequently make mistakes, creating opportunities for those who can think more deeply and objectively.<sup>6</sup>

This requirement to think differently and correctly highlights a significant psychological challenge. It necessitates resisting the comfort of the crowd and the powerful urge to conform, especially when one's unconventional view appears wrong in the short term.<sup>15</sup> It demands intellectual rigor and emotional discipline to develop and act upon insights that deviate from the norm. Thus, second-level thinking is not merely an analytical technique but a demanding cognitive and behavioral process aimed at uncovering realities hidden beneath the surface of conventional wisdom.

## Core Philosophy II: Mastering the Market Cycle

A fundamental principle in Howard Marks' worldview is the cyclical nature of markets and economies. He frequently states, "Most things will prove to be cyclical".<sup>13</sup> While acknowledging that cycles are not perfectly predictable in their timing, duration, or amplitude, Marks insists that their existence is dependable.<sup>6</sup> He often invokes the Mark Twain aphorism: "History doesn't repeat itself, but it does rhyme" <sup>39</sup>, suggesting that while specific events change, underlying patterns driven by human nature recur.

Marks prefers to frame cycles not merely as ups and downs, but as oscillations between *excesses* and *corrections*.<sup>11</sup> This perspective emphasizes the causal relationship within the cycle: "The events in the life of a cycle shouldn't be viewed merely as each being followed by the next, but - much more importantly - as each causing the next".<sup>37</sup> Periods of success and optimism breed excesses - overconfidence, increased risk-taking, and readily available capital. These excesses inevitably sow the seeds of their own demise, leading to disappointments, corrections, pessimism, risk aversion, and capital scarcity. Conversely, periods of failure and pessimism create the conditions for future recovery and success.<sup>11</sup>

Crucially, Marks argues that these cycles are driven more by the persistent and predictable swings in human psychology than by fundamental economic or business developments alone.<sup>3</sup> He often uses the metaphor of a pendulum swinging between emotional extremes: euphoria and depression, greed and fear, optimism and pessimism, risk tolerance and risk aversion.<sup>6</sup> This emotional oscillation causes the majority of investors to behave pro-cyclically - becoming enthusiastic and buying near market tops when optimism is high, and becoming fearful and selling near market bottoms when pessimism prevails.<sup>6</sup> Such behavior is inherently detrimental to long-term returns. The market cycle, viewed through Marks' lens, is thus a large-scale manifestation of collective psychological biases like herd behavior, overconfidence, fear, greed, and recency bias playing out in a recurring pattern.

Given the unpredictability of cycle timing, Marks stresses the importance of knowing where we stand in the current cycle rather than attempting to predict its turning points.<sup>6</sup> As he puts it, "You can't predict. You can prepare".<sup>9</sup> Preparation involves assessing the "temperature" of the market by observing investor sentiment, levels of risk aversion, the ease of capital availability, and valuation levels.<sup>11</sup> This awareness allows investors to adjust their posture - becoming more defensive when excesses prevail and more aggressive when corrections have run their course.<sup>20</sup>

The greatest opportunities, Marks contends, often arise at the extremes of the cycle, particularly when others forget the fundamental rule that cycles exist and trends don't last forever.<sup>13</sup> He describes the "three stages of a bull market" (from cautious optimism among a few, to broad recognition, to universal euphoria) and the corresponding "three stages of a bear market" (from early recognition of unsustainability, to widespread realization of deterioration, to pervasive hopelessness).<sup>11</sup> Recognizing these stages helps inform the necessary contrarian actions: being cautious or selling during the euphoric third stage of a bull market, and being aggressive and buying during the hopeless third stage of a bear market.<sup>20</sup> This highlights a paradox: while cycles are inevitable and understanding them is vital, their psychological drivers and variable specifics make precise forecasting impossible. The true value lies not in predicting the *when*, but in recognizing the *where* - the current state of the cycle - and adjusting one's odds and risk exposure accordingly.

## Core Philosophy III: The Primacy of Risk - Control Over Prediction

Perhaps the most foundational element of Howard Marks' investment philosophy is his relentless focus on risk. However, he starts by challenging the conventional academic definition. Risk, Marks insists, is *not* the same as volatility.<sup>25</sup> While volatility (the degree of price fluctuation) is easily quantifiable and often used as a proxy for risk in financial models, it doesn't capture the essence of what investors truly fear.<sup>48</sup> For Marks, risk is fundamentally the probability of permanent loss - the chance that an investment will suffer a decline from which it doesn't recover, or that an investor is forced to realize a loss at an inopportune time.<sup>6</sup>

A crucial aspect of Marks' view is that this true risk - the probability of loss - is inherently unquantifiable, both before an investment is made and often even in retrospect.<sup>6</sup> Future events are uncertain, randomness plays a significant role, and a positive outcome doesn't necessarily mean a decision was low-risk (it could have been luck), just as a negative outcome doesn't automatically mean a decision was flawed (it could have been bad luck).<sup>12</sup> Therefore, assessing risk relies on qualitative judgment and experience, not precise formulas.<sup>20</sup>

This leads to a distinction between risk control and risk avoidance.<sup>35</sup> Risk avoidance means refusing to engage in any activity with an uncertain negative outcome, which is incompatible with investing.<sup>54</sup> Risk control, conversely, involves the "intelligent bearing of risk for profit".<sup>54</sup> It means understanding the risks involved, deciding which risks are acceptable and adequately compensated, and actively avoiding those that are not.<sup>54</sup> Oaktree's primary goal is explicitly stated as achieving superior performance with *less-than-commensurate* risk.<sup>33</sup>

This focus on risk control manifests in a defensive investment posture.<sup>6</sup> Marks advocates "investing scared," constantly worrying about the potential for loss and what might be unknown.<sup>13</sup> The objective is to structure investments and portfolios to ensure survival under negative outcomes, prioritizing loss avoidance.<sup>6</sup> This leads to the pursuit of asymmetry: participating meaningfully in market gains while limiting losses during downturns.<sup>6</sup> The Oaktree mantra encapsulates this: "If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves".<sup>33</sup> This approach directly embodies the inversion principle - rather than focusing solely on how to achieve gains, Marks prioritizes understanding and avoiding the factors that lead to permanent loss.

Counterintuitively, Marks argues that risk is often highest when it is least perceived, and lowest when it is most perceived.<sup>6</sup> When optimism is rampant and investors believe there is no risk, they bid asset prices up to dangerous levels, thereby *creating* risk.<sup>29</sup> Conversely, when pessimism is widespread and fear is high, prices are often depressed to levels where the actual risk of further loss is low.<sup>35</sup> "The riskiest thing in the world is the belief that there's no risk".<sup>11</sup> This highlights that risk control is deeply intertwined with behavioral discipline. Managing risk effectively requires resisting the psychological pull of the crowd - the greed and euphoria when risk perception is low, and the fear and panic when risk perception is high.

Finally, Marks acknowledges that risk takes multiple forms. Beyond the primary risk of capital loss, investors face the risk of missing opportunities (often driven by FOMO - fear of missing out) and the risk of taking too little risk, leading to inadequate returns.<sup>48</sup> The challenge for the investor is to strike a thoughtful balance between the fear of losing money and the fear of missing out.<sup>53</sup>

## Core Philosophy IV: Navigating Uncertainty - The Limits of Knowing

Flowing directly from his views on risk and cycles is Marks' profound skepticism about the ability to predict the future, particularly on a macroeconomic scale.<sup>6</sup> He frequently quotes John Kenneth Galbraith's assertion that there are "two kinds of forecasters: those who don't know, and those who don't know they don't know".<sup>24</sup> Marks argues that the future is inherently unknowable due to the vast number of variables, the complexity of their interactions, the instability of relationships, and the significant role of randomness.<sup>34</sup> Most forecasts, he contends, are merely extrapolations of recent trends, information already reflected in market prices, and thus offer no edge for achieving superior returns.<sup>61</sup>

Since precise prediction is futile, the focus must shift. Marks emphasizes understanding the future not as a single point estimate, but as a *distribution* of possibilities.<sup>12</sup> The intelligent investor strives to understand the range of potential outcomes and assign probabilities to them, however imperfectly.<sup>13</sup> This probabilistic thinking is the necessary mental framework for operating under uncertainty; it replaces the impossible quest for certainty with a more realistic assessment of likelihoods. The mantra becomes: "You can't predict. You can prepare".<sup>9</sup> Preparation involves constructing portfolios robust enough to withstand a variety of potential future scenarios, particularly unfavorable ones.<sup>11</sup>

This approach necessitates profound intellectual humility - a core theme in Marks' writing, exemplified by his memo titles like "Nobody Knows".<sup>8</sup> Acknowledging the limits of one's knowledge is essential for avoiding the dangerous overconfidence that often leads to investment errors.<sup>11</sup> It also encourages investors to be aware of their own cognitive biases, such as confirmation bias, which leads us to favor information confirming our existing beliefs.<sup>61</sup>

Furthermore, Marks stresses the critical distinction between the quality of a decision and its outcome.<sup>12</sup> A good decision is one based on sound logic, thorough analysis, and the information available *at the time it was made*, before the future unfolds.<sup>12</sup> Because randomness and unforeseen events heavily influence outcomes, good decisions can lead to poor results, and poor decisions can occasionally lead to good results (luck).<sup>12</sup> Superior investors understand this difference and focus on maintaining a sound decision-making process, recognizing that over the long run, good decisions should lead to favorable outcomes, even if short-term results vary.<sup>12</sup> They differentiate between luck and skill, both in themselves and others.<sup>13</sup>

This embrace of uncertainty and intellectual humility actively shapes Oaktree's investment strategy. It leads directly to their stated disavowal of macro-forecasting and market timing as primary drivers of investment decisions.<sup>32</sup> Instead, effort is focused on what *is* knowable (or at least more knowable) and controllable: deep, bottom-up analysis of specific companies, securities, and industries; understanding the current market environment and its position in the cycle; and rigorously controlling risk within the portfolio.<sup>6</sup> This aligns with Warren Buffett's criteria that useful information must be both important and knowable.<sup>38</sup> Humility about predicting the future becomes an active strategy, channeling resources towards more reliable potential sources of investment edge.

## Core Philosophy V: Contrarianism and Value - Price, Psychology, and Patience

Howard Marks is a strong advocate for contrarianism, defined as thinking and acting in opposition to the prevailing consensus or herd behavior.<sup>3</sup> Since achieving superior results requires diverging from the average, contrarianism is a necessary, though not sufficient, condition.<sup>19</sup> Marks emphasizes that being different is not enough; one must also be *right*.<sup>13</sup> Simple, knee-jerk opposition to the consensus is unlikely to succeed.<sup>31</sup> Successful contrarianism must be grounded in superior insight - the second-level thinking that allows one to understand why the consensus is wrong.<sup>18</sup>

This contrarian stance is deeply connected to understanding and exploiting the cyclical swings of investor psychology.<sup>11</sup> The market pendulum swings between greed and fear, euphoria and depression.<sup>15</sup> The contrarian aims to lean against these emotional excesses.<sup>43</sup> This means buying when fear is rampant, prices are depressed, and others are panic selling; conversely, it means being cautious or selling when greed and euphoria dominate, and prices are high.<sup>11</sup> This requires significant emotional discipline to resist the powerful pull of the herd and act against one's own potentially mirroring emotions.<sup>11</sup>

The practical application of this contrarian, psychology-aware approach lies in value investing, specifically focusing on the relationship between an asset's price and its intrinsic value.<sup>3</sup> Marks repeatedly emphasizes, "It's not what you buy, it's what you pay".<sup>3</sup> Investment success stems from "buying things well," not merely "buying good things".<sup>15</sup> Even a high-quality asset can be a poor investment if overpaid for, while a lower-quality asset bought cheaply can yield excellent returns.<sup>15</sup> Value investing, in Marks' framework, is essentially applied contrarianism: buying below intrinsic value typically means purchasing assets when the market's perception (reflected in a low price) is overly pessimistic relative to the underlying reality (intrinsic value).

A key tool in this process is the "margin of safety".<sup>6</sup> This means purchasing an asset at a price significantly below its estimated intrinsic value.<sup>13</sup> This discount provides a crucial buffer against estimation errors, unforeseen negative developments, or simple bad luck.<sup>10</sup> It is a fundamental risk control mechanism <sup>13</sup>, and the larger the discount, the greater the margin of safety.<sup>24</sup> Identifying and acting upon this margin of safety is how the contrarian value investor exploits the market's psychological extremes.

Executing this strategy requires immense patience.<sup>6</sup> Opportunities where price significantly diverges from value are not always present.<sup>6</sup> Investors must wait for bargains to appear, often resisting the urge to act when nothing compelling is available - "Sometimes the best action is inaction".<sup>10</sup> Furthermore, even a correct contrarian value judgment may take a long time to be validated by the market; prices can remain irrational or diverge further before converging with value.<sup>22</sup> Patience is therefore not just a virtue but a strategic necessity, enabling the investor to wait for opportunities and hold positions until the underlying value thesis plays out.<sup>45</sup> Marks suggests looking for bargains among assets that are controversial, complex, frightening, unpopular, unloved, underperforming, or recently discarded by others - areas where the herd is unlikely to tread.<sup>3</sup> He also notes that buying from motivated or forced sellers can present excellent opportunities.<sup>6</sup>

## Marks' Philosophy Beyond Investing: Principles for Rational Decision-Making

While Howard Marks' writings primarily address the investment world, the core principles he espouses possess a universality that extends far beyond financial markets.<sup>3</sup> His emphasis on second-level thinking, understanding cycles and psychology, rigorous risk assessment, intellectual humility, contrarianism, and patience forms a robust framework for rational decision-making in any complex domain.

Consider the broader applications:

-   **Second-Level Thinking:** In business strategy, it means looking beyond a "good product" to analyze market saturation, competitive responses, and long-term sustainability.<sup>18</sup> In policymaking, it involves considering unintended consequences and systemic effects, not just immediate goals. In personal life, it encourages deeper understanding of relationship dynamics or career trajectories beyond surface appearances. It is the discipline of questioning the obvious and digging deeper.<sup>19</sup>

-   **Understanding Cycles and Psychology:** Recognizing cyclical patterns in social trends, political moods, technological adoption, or organizational morale can inform strategic timing and resource allocation.<sup>11</sup> Understanding how collective optimism or pessimism influences group decisions is vital in leadership and team management.

-   **Risk Assessment:** Applying Marks' definition of risk - the probability of significant negative, potentially irreversible outcomes - rather than just volatility, is crucial in career planning, health decisions, or major project management.<sup>48</sup> The focus shifts to avoiding catastrophic failures ("permanent loss") rather than managing minor fluctuations.

-   **Intellectual Humility and Uncertainty:** Acknowledging the limits of knowledge is fundamental in science, medicine, law, and even personal judgments.<sup>11</sup> This fosters open-mindedness, adaptability, and a focus on preparation rather than rigid prediction.<sup>11</sup> Valuing the quality of the decision-making process over the immediate outcome promotes learning and resilience in the face of inevitable setbacks.<sup>12</sup>

-   **Contrarianism and Value:** Questioning conventional wisdom is essential for innovation and progress in any field.<sup>14</sup> Identifying undervalued ideas, overlooked approaches, or neglected problems can lead to breakthroughs. Assessing the "price" (effort, cost, risk) versus the "value" (potential benefit) is a fundamental calculation in resource allocation across all endeavors.

-   **Patience:** Resisting impulsive action, allowing situations to mature, waiting for the opportune moment - these applications of patience are critical in negotiation, long-term planning, skill development, and relationship building.<sup>14</sup>

Through his memos and books, Marks acts as a modern teacher of practical wisdom, offering tools for clearer thinking and more rational behavior in a complex and uncertain world.<sup>5</sup> His emphasis on continuous learning, rationality, discipline, and understanding human behavior provides enduring lessons.<sup>6</sup> Ultimately, Marks provides less a set of specific rules for investing and more a transferable "operating system" for thought - a way to process information, assess complex situations, understand human tendencies, and make reasoned decisions when faced with incomplete information and an unknowable future.

## Conclusion: The Enduring Wisdom of Howard Marks

Howard Marks has carved a unique niche not just as a highly successful investor, but as a profound and practical thinker whose insights offer enduring value. His core philosophical contributions - the discipline of second-level thinking, the crucial understanding of market cycles as driven by human psychology, the redefinition of risk with an emphasis on control over prediction, the humble embrace of uncertainty coupled with probabilistic reasoning, and the patient, contrarian application of value principles - form a cohesive and powerful framework for navigating complex systems.

While rooted in the demanding world of investment management, these principles transcend finance. They offer a guide to rationality, urging decision-makers in any field to look beyond the superficial, question consensus, understand the interplay of logic and emotion, manage downside potential rigorously, acknowledge the limits of foresight, and act with disciplined patience. In this sense, Marks can indeed be regarded as a modern philosopher of practical affairs.<sup>10</sup> He has distilled decades of experience confronting uncertainty and irrationality into a set of generalizable principles for clearer thinking and more effective action. His writings serve as a valuable resource for anyone seeking to improve their judgment and make more rational decisions in an inherently unpredictable world.
