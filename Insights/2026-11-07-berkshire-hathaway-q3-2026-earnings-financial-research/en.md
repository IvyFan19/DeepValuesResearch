## 1. Owner Earnings and Cash Flow Dynamics over Adjusted EPS

In evaluating Berkshire Hathaway’s economic performance for Q3 2026 (period ended September 30, 2026) and fiscal year 2025, GAAP reported net income and conventional adjusted EPS fail to reflect true underlying operating performance. Under GAAP standards, net income must include unrealized investment gains and losses from Berkshire’s large public equity portfolio—including core positions such as Apple, American Express, Bank of America, Coca-Cola, Chevron, Occidental Petroleum, and The Kraft Heinz Company. These accounting adjustments create wide swings in reported net income that carry zero correlation to the operational cash generation of Berkshire's underlying operating subsidiaries.

To establish a clear view of sustainable cash generation power, analysts under the Warren Buffett framework utilize Owner Earnings rather than GAAP Net Income. The precise mathematical derivation of Owner Earnings proceeds from Operating Cash Flow:

$$\text{Owner Earnings} = \text{Net Operating Cash Flow} + \text{Non-Cash Adjustments (Deferred Taxes, Amortization)} - \text{Maintenance Capital Expenditures} - \text{Stock-Based Compensation}$$

In GAAP cash flow reporting, non-cash charges such as stock-based compensation (SBC) are added back to Net Income to compute Cash Flow from Operations. Because stock-based compensation represents a genuine economic dilutive expense already deducted in GAAP net income, it must be subtracted when calculating Owner Earnings starting from Operating Cash Flow to prevent treating employee equity issuance as "free" cash flow. Conversely, when analysts evaluate net income directly, stock-based compensation is already embedded as an operating expense and must never be subtracted a second time.

Maintenance capital expenditures—defined as capitalized property, plant, and equipment additions required to maintain existing economic position and competitive rank, such as BNSF track structure relaying and BHE utility grid upkeep—absorb a predictable share of operational cash. DeepValues Research analyst inferences regarding cash flow durability project that Berkshire’s underlying operating cash flows, after accounting for maintenance capital expenditures and stock compensation, provide an annual normalized owner earnings baseline of substantial magnitude, offering unmatched strategic liquidity in tight credit environments.

## 3. Return on Capital vs. Cost of Capital: Margins vs. Leverage

Berkshire Hathaway’s Return on Equity (ROE) and Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) are uniquely shaped by its capital structure, which pairs conservative debt ratios with non-funded operational leverage derived from insurance "float."

Float represents upfront insurance premiums collected from policyholders before claims are settled—often spanning decades in long-tail retroactive reinsurance and structured annuities. When insurance underwriting operates at a cumulative profit (earned premiums exceed loss payouts and operating expenses), the effective cost of float is negative. This converts insurance float into low-cost financial leverage, amplifying returns on equity across non-insurance operations without subjecting the enterprise to commercial paper rollover risks or restrictive debt covenants.

Berkshire’s capital allocation matrix displays a sharp structural dichotomy between operating divisions:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        BERKSHIRE HATHAWAY CAPITAL MATRIX                          |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| CAPITAL-INTENSIVE SEGMENTS                       HIGH-MARGIN, LOW-CAPITAL SEGMENTS|
| (BNSF Freight Rail / BHE Energy)                 (Insurance Group / Manufacturing)|
|                                                                                   |
| • Heavy annual CapEx (track maintenance, grid)  • Minimal physical CapEx requirements|
| • Returns governed by regulated utility tariffs  • High returns on incremental capital|
| • High capital reinvestment burdens              • Generates massive net cash inflow  |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

1.  **Capital-Intensive Infrastructure:** BNSF Freight Rail and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (BHE) demand ongoing capital expenditures to maintain physical track infrastructure, replace locomotives, build out renewable generation, and strengthen transmission grids. Returns in these divisions are constrained by regulatory rate bases and capital requirements, yielding moderate ROIC on large invested asset bases.
2.  **High-Margin, Low-Capital Operating Units:** Core insurance underwriting, consumer manufacturing, and specialty industrial entities generate high returns on incremental invested capital. These entities require minimal reinvestment to sustain operations, releasing significant free cash flow to the central corporate parent for reallocation.

*   **Wildfire Litigation & Contingencies (PacifiCorp / BHE):** As detailed in *2025 Form 10-K, Item 3: Legal Proceedings* and *Note 22: Commitments and Contingencies*, BHE subsidiary PacifiCorp faces substantial claims stemming from 2020 and 2022 wildfires (including the Beachie Creek, Echo Mountain Complex, 242, and South Obenchain fires). In the lead *James Case et al.* litigation in Multnomah County Circuit Court, Oregon, jury verdicts have awarded economic and non-economic damages to named plaintiffs. Subsequent mass complaints, opt-out trials, and state court proceedings involve aggregate damages claims that remain subject to ongoing legal appeals, statutory challenge, and settlement negotiations.
