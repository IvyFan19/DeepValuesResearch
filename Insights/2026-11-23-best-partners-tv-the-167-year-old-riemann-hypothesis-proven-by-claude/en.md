### The Problem & History
In 1859, Bernhard Riemann published a 6-page paper connecting the distribution of prime numbers—the fundamental building blocks of integer arithmetic—to the non-trivial zeros of a complex function known as the Riemann zeta function ($\zeta$). While trivial zeros are easily located at negative even integers, the non-trivial zeros reside within a critical vertical strip where the real part lies between 0 and 1. Riemann hypothesized that all non-trivial zeros lie precisely on the central line with a real part of $1/2$.

The spatial distribution of these zeros dictates the error bound in prime counting. If every zero resides strictly on the $1/2$ critical line, the statistical fluctuations in prime distribution relative to the logarithmic integral are suppressed to a minimal scale proportional to $\sqrt{X}$. Although computer algorithms had previously verified the first 10 trillion zeros on the critical line, empirical computation cannot validate an infinite set. Consequently, the Clay Mathematics Institute designated the Riemann Hypothesis one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems in 2000, establishing a $1,000,000 reward for a rigorous proof.
