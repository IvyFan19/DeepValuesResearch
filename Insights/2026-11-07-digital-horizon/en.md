# Modeling Expected Stock Market Returns: Applying Jack Bogle’s Two-Factor Framework to Today’s Valuations

Analyzing long-term expected market returns through the lens of Jack Bogle’s two-factor valuation framework, alongside Benjamin Graham’s classic asset allocation rules, indicates that U.S. stock market returns are projected to hover around 4% annually over the coming decade due to elevated price-to-earnings (P/E) multiples. While historical market cycles demonstrate the immense power of compounding capital over extended horizons, current valuation levels impose a heavy headwind on forward equity premiums. For long-term investors, this subdued forecast underscores why navigating future market environments successfully requires disciplined, rule-based asset allocation across market cycles rather than aggressive, unscientific attempts at short-term market timing.

## The Anatomy of Market Returns: Investment Return vs. Speculative Return

Jack Bogle’s two-factor methodology provides a structured, economically grounded framework for predicting stock market performance over decade-long horizons. Rather than relying on macro forecasts or short-term market noise, Bogle’s model decomposes total stock market return into two fundamental drivers:

1. **Investment Return:** This represents the underlying economic productivity of corporate enterprise. It is calculated as the sum of today’s initial dividend yield (currently sitting at approximately 2%) and future corporate earnings growth. Historically, long-term corporate earnings growth has averaged about 5% annually, remaining bounded between 2% and 7% (absent an economic depression). When modeling forward returns, Bogle pairs today's ~2% dividend yield with a conservative 4% earnings growth assumption—sitting comfortably within the historical range—to establish a baseline investment return of 6%.
2. **Speculative Return:** This captures psychological shifts in valuation multiples, specifically changes in the market P/E ratio. Speculative return measures how much investors are willing to pay per dollar of underlying accounting earnings. Multiple expansion provides a powerful tailwind; for instance, if the market P/E ratio expands from 10x to 20x over a decade, that re-rating contributes an additional 7% in annualized return over that 10-year period (as occurred in both the 1980s and 1990s). Conversely, multiple contraction penalizes total returns.

This structural divide between fundamental economic productivity and speculative multiple expansion also explains the historical wealth gap between simple index tracking and active management. During the greatest 35-to-40-year bull market in recorded history, the S&P 500 index generated an annualized return of approximately 12%, turning every $1 invested into $60. Over the same period, the average active mutual fund delivered roughly 10% annually, turning $1 into $40. Satisfied with a 40-fold return on their capital, millions of investors failed to realize that active management fees and trading friction surrendered 20 dollars of wealth generation per dollar invested relative to the broad market index.

## Analyzing Current Valuations and the 4% Return Reality

Applying Bogle’s framework to today's market environment begins with an objective assessment of current valuation multiples. The market currently trades at a P/E ratio of approximately 25x based on Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) earnings. Experienced value investors must insist on GAAP accounting earnings rather than management-promoted "operating earnings." Operating earnings routinely strip out restructuring costs, asset write-downs, and stock-based compensation—effectively leaving out "all the bad stuff" to present a sanitized, promotional view of corporate profitability.

The mathematical baseline for expected stock market returns over the coming decade builds upon conservative accounting fundamentals:
* **Current Dividend Yield:** ~2%
* **Conservative Earnings Growth:** ~4% (bounded historically between 2% and 7%)
* **Baseline Investment Return:** 2% + 4% = 6%

To calculate total expected return, we must incorporate speculative return by accounting for mean reversion in valuation multiples. If the market P/E multiple contracts from its current elevated level of 25x GAAP earnings down to a historical norm of 18x over the next 10 years, that multiple contraction subtracts roughly 2 percentage points per year from investor performance. Subtracting 2 percentage points of annual speculative drag from the 6% baseline investment return results in an expected nominal stock market return of approximately 4% per year over the next decade.

Crucially, an overvalued market does not resolve in a single, predictable manner. According to Bogle’s framework, an overvalued market resolves in one of two ways:
1. **A prolonged decade of low returns:** A slow, drawn-out environment where market performance averages roughly 4% per year over a 10-year horizon.
2. **A sharp, severe market crash:** A rapid drawdown of 30%, 40%, or even 50%, which swiftly resets valuation multiples back to historical averages before returning to traditional growth rates.

This conservative ~4% baseline projection is far from an isolated view. It aligns directly with 10-year return forecasts published by major institutional managers, including Vanguard and Larry Fink at BlackRock.

## Methodology Track Record and Reversion to the Mean

Bogle’s 10-year forecasting methodology is supported by a 25-year track record of real-world testing. Evaluated across 15 distinct, overlapping 10-year forecasting periods, the model’s predicted returns achieved an impressive statistical correlation of approximately 0.81 with actual market outcomes.

While short-term market timing is inherently unpredictable, extended evaluation windows demonstrate the persistent force of mean reversion. For example, when Bogle published his initial model forecast in 1990, he projected that 1990s stock market returns would average 11% annually. Driven by aggressive speculative multiple expansion (P/E ratios expanding from 10x to over 20x), actual market returns reached 18% by the end of 1999. However, following the subsequent dot-com crash and market correction, the 13-year annualized return from 1990 through 2003 reverted precisely to the forecasted 11%. Over extended horizons, short-term valuation noise is neutralized by fundamental economic realities.

This historical record refutes periodic claims that equity markets have achieved a "permanently high plateau"—a famous proclamation made by economist Irving Fisher immediately prior to the 1929 market crash. Valuation multiples cannot expand indefinitely; a P/E multiple can move from 10x to 20x or 25x, but it cannot compound continuously to 40x or 80x. Over multi-year cycles, elevated valuations inevitably bow to mean reversion.

## Risk Management and Asset Allocation: The Graham Framework

When confronted with low expected real returns, long-term investors should prioritize structural risk management over emotional reactions. Bogle strongly cautions against attempting to dodge potential drawdowns by liquidating stock holdings, reiterating the core rule of compounding: "never, never, never get out of the market."

Instead of engaging in speculative market timing, intelligent investors should apply the asset allocation framework outlined by Benjamin Graham in the 1949 edition of *The Intelligent Investor*. Graham advised establishing strict floor and ceiling bounds on portfolio exposure:
* **Minimum Exposure:** Maintain no less than 25% of total capital in either major asset class (stocks or bonds).
* **Maximum Exposure:** Maintain no more than 75% of total capital in either major asset class.

Within Graham’s framework, the 25/75 rebalancing rule functions as an institutional margin of safety. When forward expected returns on equities are low (~4%), shifting portfolio weightings toward a conservative posture (e.g., 25% to 50% equities, with the remainder in fixed income) systematically reduces portfolio drawdowns while preserving upside participation—eliminating the reliance on unscientific timing strategies.
