For long-term investors, Chancellor’s historical perspective reinforces the fundamental boundary separating investment from speculation. This distinction was captured by Fred Schwed in his classic 1930s Wall Street volume, *Where Are the Customers' Yachts?*: investment represents an earnest attempt to prevent a large sum of money from turning into a small sum, relying on capital preservation, a strict margin of safety, and gradual compounding; speculation represents the high-risk attempt to turn a small sum into a fortune quickly. In speculative manias, market participants transition from disciplined investors into short-term gamblers, causing risk to be systematically mispriced across asset classes.

### The Greater Fool Theory and Momentum Investing
Speculative frenzies rely heavily on the "Greater Fool Theory"—the willingness to purchase assets far above their intrinsic value under the assumption that another buyer will pay a higher price before a crash occurs. Modern "momentum investing" operates as a rebranded form of this theory. While quantitative backtests extending to the 1920s show that the momentum factor has historically generated excess returns, empirical research demonstrates that momentum strategies generated zero net returns between 1995 and 2015. Recent momentum dominance has been heavily amplified by the structural shift from active to passive indexing—where mechanical capital flows systematically push overvalued megacaps higher—and by high retail trading activity, where retail brokerages process trading volumes up to twice those of professional institutions.
