### 1. The Dynamics of Market Crashes and Hidden Leverage
Fu Peng deconstructed the mechanics of severe market crashes using the 2015 Chinese A-share deleveraging crisis as a primary case study. A critical driver of market crashes is the divergence between regulatory perception and actual market leverage. During the 2015 cycle, regulatory authorities assumed that market leverage was contained within standard mechanisms like two-times leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs) or standard margin financing. However, regulatory rules cannot suppress human greed. 

Fu Peng explicitly connected this pattern to modern retail investing behavior in South Korea, where young retail investors utilize 2x leveraged ETFs and subsequently amplify them into 8x to 10x off-balance-sheet leverage via secondary financial channels. Field research conducted by Fu Peng’s team in regional capital hubs such as Hangzhou and Ningbo revealed a vast network of grey-market margin financing, umbrella trusts, and zero-margin futures accounts. In reality, the actual leverage embedded in the system was off-balance-sheet margin financing multiplied by ten. Once regulators realized the magnitude of the exposure and began withdrawing liquidity, systemic collapse became mathematically inevitable because the market lacked sufficient underlying liquidity to absorb the forced selling.

This structural vulnerability was compounded by retail investor behavior, characterized by `"right-side thinking"` and crowd mentality. As speculative slogans projected market targets of 6,000 to 10,000 points, rational decision-making dissolved. Domestic institutional and retail investors refused to exit early due to peer risk and career risk—adopting a consensus mindset that everyone must stay invested together and exit together to avoid underperforming peers during momentum rallies. ### 3. The Three-Phase AI Revolution & Capital Expenditure (Capex) Cycles
Tracing a 150-year historical technology timeline, Fu Peng categorized modern technological history into two major revolutions: the Industrial Revolution and the Information Technology (IT) Revolution. The IT revolution consists of three distinct phases spanning roughly one century, targeted for foundational completion around the year 2050:
1. *Phase 1 (1940s–1950s):* Computation (hardware development to solve basic machine calculation).
2. *Phase 2 (1980s):* Interconnection (fiber optics, networking, and the commercial internet).
3. *Phase 3 (Current Phase):* Intelligence/Self-Computation (AI functioning as the final base layer of the IT infrastructure).

Within this current phase, commercial developments follow a predictable industrial S-curve lifecycle. During the *Growth Phase*, high certainty, low volatility, and massive capital expenditures funded by the direct free cash flow (FCF) of mega-cap internet platforms drive dominant hardware suppliers. This was evidenced by NVIDIA’s valuation expanding sixfold from $500 billion to $3 trillion between late 2022 and July 2024. The *Transition to Maturity* occurs when capital expenditures saturate, component suppliers experience price erosion and margin compression, and industry leaders transition into "legacy stock" status. Fu Peng noted that further extensions, such as embodied intelligence and humanoid robotics, represent a subsequent technological era combining industrial and IT base layers, requiring another 3 to 5 years before reaching commercial maturity.

Fu Peng presented a critical analytical framework regarding hardware Capex cycles based on the hardware "teardown" indicator. Drawing a direct parallel to historical Apple supply chain investing—where research analysts disassembled iPhones down to batteries, speakers, and screws—he stated that when market speculation shifts from core GPUs to secondary components (CPU, HBM) and eventually down to liquid cooling ("夜冷") and server rack frames, the hardware speculation S-curve has reached its terminal peak.

### 4. Commercialization Bottlenecks and Production Relations
A structural bottleneck currently exists in downstream AI monetization. Using China’s AI short drama (AMG) sector as an empirical example, Fu Peng noted that AMG projects account for 50% of total domestic computing token consumption. Despite this massive technical utilization, the sector remains broadly unprofitable.

An accounting analysis of AMG production costs reveals the underlying economic paradox:
*   **Computing Token Costs:** 3% of total project costs.
*   **Hardware Depreciation (e.g., RTX 5090 GPUs):** 5% of total project costs.
*   **Platform Traffic Acquisition ("Stream Buying" / 投流):** 80% to 90% of total revenue paid directly to central internet distribution platforms.

This structural imbalance illustrates a critical economic principle: while advanced technology improves unit productivity and Total Factor Productivity (TFP), it fails to resolve flawed economic production relations. Because distribution monopolies control user traffic, efficiency savings generated by AI tools are immediately absorbed by traffic acquisition costs, leaving content creators unprofitable while increasing labor intensity.

Fu Peng cited specific domestic platform entry models—such as Tongyi Qianwen (通义千问 / Qwen) and Doubao (豆包)—which extract middleman commissions when users book hotel itineraries or travel services generated by AI. This demonstrates how novel AI tools inevitably revert to legacy internet distribution tollbooths, preventing application creators from capturing economic value.

### 5. Wealth Generation: Labor vs. Leverage
Fu Peng categorized global wealth generation into three sequential tiers: Labor Value, Debt Value, and Leverage Value. Direct labor is inherently bounded by human lifespan and time, making pure labor productivity an inefficient mechanism for building significant wealth. 

Over the past 40+ years, structural global wealth accumulation has been distributed as follows:
*   **Debt and Leverage Dynamics:** Account for over 60% of global wealth accumulation.
*   **Direct Labor and Productivity:** Account for under 40% of global wealth accumulation.

Debt serves as an intermediate store of value, allowing individuals to lock in asset purchases against future projected labor earnings—exemplified by average functional home mortgage durations of 33 years across global markets. Leverage represents the extreme extension of debt, utilizing financial derivatives such as contract-for-difference (CFD) instruments or cryptocurrency perpetual contracts offering 25x, 50x, 100x, or 200x leverage multiples. While leverage can rapidly alter capital accumulation, unmanaged leverage exposes market participants to catastrophic downside risk during cycle turns.

*   **Business Quality & IP Moats:** Fundamental value investing prioritizes enduring economic moats over transient consumer viral trends. Fast-fading consumer behaviors (such as speculative toy unboxing) lack long-term customer stickiness. Without decade-long product retention, initial high gross margins inevitably erode as consumer preferences shift.

Furthermore, central platform tollbooths absorb downstream efficiency gains, directly eroding the ROIC of AI software developers.
