## 1. The investor: philosophy, fund and lifetime work

Peter Andreas Thiel (born October 11, 1967 in Frankfurt, West Germany) is an entrepreneur, venture capitalist, and global macro investor whose career spans four decades of foundational contributions to technology, finance, and political economy. Raised in an itinerant family before settling in Foster City, California, Thiel attended a German-language elementary school in Swakopmund, South West Africa (modern-day Namibia), an environment marked by strict uniform requirements and corporal punishment. This early exposure to institutional rigidity contributed to a lifelong skepticism of bureaucratic regimentation, laying the foundation for his intellectual adherence to individualism and libertarian philosophy `[Inference]`. Thiel excelled in mathematics, was valedictorian of San Mateo High School in 1985, and earned a B.A. in Philosophy from Stanford University in 1989. At Stanford, he co-founded *The Stanford Review* in 1987, establishing an intellectual network that produced numerous technology and policy leaders. He completed his Juris Doctor at Stanford Law School in 1992.

After serving as a law clerk to Judge James Larry Edmondson of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit (1992–1993), working as a securities lawyer at Sullivan & Cromwell in New York, writing speeches for former U.S. Secretary of Education William Bennett, and trading currency option derivatives at Credit Suisse, Thiel returned to California in 1996. Capitalizing on the emerging internet economy, he established Thiel Capital Management with $1 million raised from friends and family. In 1998, Thiel backed Max Levchin’s cryptography concept, launching Fieldlink (later Confinity), which developed PayPal in 1999. Confinity conceived PayPal as a digital wallet designed to facilitate secure online transactions and liberate users from currency inflation. PayPal merged with Elon Musk’s X.com in 2000, went public in February 2002, and was acquired by eBay in October 2002 for $1.5 billion. As CEO until the sale, Thiel held a 3.7% stake worth $55 million at acquisition, establishing his reputation as the leader of the "PayPal Mafia."

In August 2004, Thiel made the first outside angel investment in Facebook, providing a $500,000 convertible note for a 10.2% stake at a $4.9 million valuation. When Facebook reached its May 2012 initial public offering, Thiel liquidated 16.8 million shares for $638 million, selling the vast majority of his remaining equity shortly after the lock-up period for over $1 billion in total proceeds. Parallel to his consumer technology investments, Thiel co-founded Palantir Technologies in May 2003, serving as Chairman. Built on PayPal’s anti-fraud data engine following the September 11 attacks, Palantir focused on big-data analytics for intelligence agencies, defense modernization, and commercial enterprise operations.

Thiel’s intellectual framework synthesizes René Girard’s mimetic theory and Leo Strauss’s critique of modernity. Girard’s thesis—that human desire is imitative, leading inevitably to rivalry and destructive social conflict—informs Thiel’s preference for non-imitative business strategy. In his book *Zero to One*, Thiel contends that true economic value is created by progressing from "0 to 1" through technological innovation that captures monopoly profits in niche markets, avoiding the destructive competition of going from "1 to n." Furthermore, Thiel emphasizes a structural divergence between "atoms and bits," critiquing a regulatory regime that heavily restricts physical innovations (energy, aerospace, medicine) while leaving digital software less regulated. This stance drives his venture capital allocation toward defense hardware, nuclear power, space logistics, and national security infrastructure.
