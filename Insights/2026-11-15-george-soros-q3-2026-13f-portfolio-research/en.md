## 1. The Investor: Philosophy, Fund, and Lifetime Work

George Soros was born Schwartz György in Budapest, Hungary, in 1930. Having survived the Nazi occupation of Hungary as a teenager, he moved to the United Kingdom in 1947 to attend the London School of Economics (LSE). At LSE, he earned a Bachelor of Science in philosophy in 1951 and a Master of Science in philosophy in 1954 under the direct mentorship of philosopher Karl Popper. Soros initiated his financial career as a clerk and arbitrage trader at merchant banks in London and New York—including Singer & Friedlander, F. M. Mayer, Wertheim & Co., and Arnhold and S. Bleichroeder—before establishing the Double Eagle hedge fund in 1969 with $4 million in capital. Double Eagle laid the operational foundation for founding Soros Fund Management LLC in 1970 and launching the Quantum Fund, which went on to become one of the most compounding-efficient investment vehicles in financial history.

***Reflexivity in Capital Markets***
Soros’s underlying theoretical framework centers on his concept of *reflexivity*, derived from Popperian philosophy and applied directly to global security markets. Reflexivity rejects the neoclassical economic assumption that markets naturally tend toward a stable market equilibrium. Instead, it posits a two-way feedback loop between cognitive perceptions and reality: participant fallibility creates subjective market biases that alter economic fundamentals (such as corporate liquidity, collateral values, and credit availability), which in turn further reinforce participant biases. Soros categorizes financial markets into *near-equilibrium* states—where self-correcting mechanisms keep security prices tied to fundamental cash flows—and *far-from-equilibrium* states, where boom-and-bust feedback loops drive prices into extreme disequilibrium before violent reversals occur.

## 8. 13F Regulatory Limitations & Closing Disclaimer

Form 13F reports provide a snapshot of U.S. long equity holdings and convertible securities managed by institutional investment managers as of the final calendar day of each quarter. Because filings are submitted up to 45 days following quarter-end, they do not present a real-time account of current fund allocations. Furthermore, Form 13F disclosures omit cash reserves, international equity holdings, fixed-income portfolios, short positions, private investments, and non-reported derivative positions.
