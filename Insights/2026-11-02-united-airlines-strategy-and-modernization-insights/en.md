# United Airlines: Capital Allocation, Technological Moats, and Capitalizing on Industry Distortions

This analysis evaluates United Airlines' strategic divergence under CEO Scott Kirby from traditional airline operating models. United is establishing a durable operational and commercial advantage by aggressively deploying capital during industry downturns into proprietary IT architecture, Starlink connectivity, and wide-body fleet expansion, while legacy competitors face cost inflation and structural loss-making hubs. In a capital-intensive industry historically prone to value destruction, United's technology-led differentiation and disciplined route network economics offer a potential widening of owner earnings and margin of safety relative to market expectations.

## 1. Business Model & Monetization: Hub-and-Spoke Network Discipline

United Airlines generates revenue and protects its margin structure by strictly adhering to hub-and-spoke network dynamics rather than point-to-point flying. Operating a hub model relies on catchment-area flying, where smaller regional aircraft bring connecting passengers from surrounding secondary markets into central hubs. For example, United operates eight daily feeder flights from Milwaukee to its Chicago hub. These regional flights carry passengers who connect onward to high-margin long-haul international destinations like Paris or Tokyo. In contrast, point-to-point routes lack this connection density, leaving aircraft dependent entirely on local origin-and-destination demand, which fails to support broad network profitability.

Low-cost carrier (LCC) and point-to-point models face severe structural pressures in high-cost infrastructure environments. Spirit Airlines demonstrated the non-viability of this unit economic model when New York airport fees rose to $52 per passenger, while Spirit's average fare sat at $58. Across the United States, major airport authority expenses have surged approximately 70% from pre-COVID levels to the present. Low-cost carriers historically maintained a price advantage by utilizing secondary, low-cost airports, such as Southwest Airlines flying into Oakland rather than San Francisco, or domestic LCCs using secondary LA-area airports rather than Los Angeles International (LAX). By abandoning secondary airports to compete directly in primary hubs like Chicago, Newark, and San Francisco, low-cost carriers were priced out by inflated airport fees without possessing the high-margin connecting international traffic required to absorb cost inflation. United protected its gross margin structure and pricing power by introducing Basic Economy and up-gauging aircraft size, allowing it to compete profitably on ticket prices while managing unit costs without resorting to short-term CASM-X compromises.

A core operational discipline enforced under Scott Kirby is the immediate elimination of loss-making routes, rejecting the industry myth of "strategic flying." Airlines frequently justify loss-making routes under the excuse of corporate relationship building or regional credit card market share. Management's mandate dictates that any route structurally losing money must be canceled immediately. For instance, when United trialed a direct route to Bergen, Norway, and it generated a slight net loss, the route was promptly discontinued. Conversely, disciplined route selection has unlocked highly profitable niche markets where customer demand is high but unserved by competitors, such as direct flights to Nuuk, Greenland. To deploy open-jaw pilgrimage routes in Spain and Portugal, United's network planning team under Patrick Quayle analyzed customer booking data to identify "broken itineraries"—patterns where travelers flew into one city on United and departed out of a secondary city a few hundred miles away on a rival carrier. By launching dual-city pilgrimage routes, United captured unserved round-trip traffic through analytical data synthesis rather than speculative "strategic" expansions.

Market participants frequently misinterpret the net margin structure of airline credit card loyalty programs. While consensus financial analysis views credit card partnership revenue as near-100% net profit, this perspective ignores the substantial operational cost of fulfilling redemptions. United transports approximately 50,000 passengers per day who fly for free using credit card miles. Revenue figures from loyalty programs do not represent pure profit because the airline must absorb real physical expenses—including jet fuel, aircraft wear, and labor costs—required to transport those 50,000 redemption passengers daily across its route network.

## 2. Competitive Moat & Strategic Differentiation: Tech Stack Ownership and Starlink

United's competitive moat is anchored in structural technological ownership rather than outsourced infrastructure. Unlike nearly all global competitors who outsource reservation systems to third-party Global Distribution System (GDS) vendors such as Sabre or Apollo, United owns and maintains its legacy mainframe reservation system in-house, sharing access only with its partner, Copa Airlines. Owning this core infrastructure allows United to build, test, and deploy proprietary software features internally. Building on this legacy base, United committed several hundred million dollars starting in 2020 to migrate off mainframes into cloud-native database architecture. Executed without an upfront ROI payback metric, this modernization unlocked operational speed that competitors using third-party vendors cannot replicate. During the cutover off legacy mainframe systems, management demonstrated strict risk control by intentionally unscheduling and canceling several hundred flights on the day of the migration to ensure passenger safety and system stability in the event of an outage, allowing the cutover to complete successfully without operational disruption.

The deployment of SpaceX’s low-Earth-orbit (LEO) Starlink Wi-Fi serves as a central customer acquisition vector. Starlink is currently active on 48% of United's flights, expanding to 50% within a week at an installation pace of approximately three aircraft per day. United targets its Los Angeles (LA) hub for 100% fleet coverage in the first half of next year, whereas its San Francisco (SFO) hub lags in deployment speed due to SFO's high concentration of Boeing 787 wide-body aircraft, which face slower certification timelines from Boeing. High-speed LEO satellite connectivity offers high bandwidth and low latency, standing in sharp contrast to legacy satellite Wi-Fi systems. Legacy systems lack the physical bandwidth to support free internet access, resulting in slow, unusable connectivity similar to dial-up internet. United views fast LEO Wi-Fi, rather than slow free Wi-Fi, as the primary decision driver for brand-loyal business travelers.

In-house technology ownership enables rapid deployment of proprietary customer-facing software features. Key software innovations include ConnectionSaver, which manages tight passenger flight connections, and customized seat-back entertainment software. United is taking over seat-back map software directly from vendor Panasonic to integrate personalized customer map preferences and loyalty milestones, such as automated flight cabin celebrations for million-mile flyers. Additionally, United developed an automated system that texts visual weather radar maps directly to passengers during flight delays. Providing passengers with visual proof of closed flight corridors through lines of thunderstorms shifted United's Net Promoter Score (NPS) on weather delays from -60 to +60, generating a 120-point net shift. Expanding on this predictive intelligence, United is deploying real-time disruption probabilities directly into its app, informing passengers that a scheduled flight carries a 41% chance of weather disruption versus a 12% or 3% probability on alternative flights.

This technology advantage creates a temporal competitive window of two to three years before major domestic competitors can certify and deploy LEO satellite hardware across their fleets. United's strategy focuses on utilizing high-speed Starlink Wi-Fi as an initial trial hook to capture market share from competing legacy carriers. Once trial passengers board, United aims to retain their permanent brand loyalty through superior app integration, personalized seat-back software, and operational reliability, converting a temporary Wi-Fi window into a permanent software-driven competitive moat.

## 3. Management & Capital Allocation: Contrarian Down-Cycle Bets

Management's capital allocation track record demonstrates a willingness to deploy capital during tail-risk events outside two standard deviations. At the height of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, global industry consensus predicted a permanent 50% reduction in international travel demand. Global long-haul carriers permanently retired wide-body fleets and canceled capital expenditures based on short-term customer fear surveys. Evaluating this consensus as fundamentally flawed and rejecting risk models like Value at Risk (VaR) and Black-Scholes that assume normally distributed outcomes, Scott Kirby executed the largest aircraft order in corporate history: contracting for 200 wide-body aircraft (carrying a list price of ~$400 million each, prior to negotiated discounts) alongside several hundred narrow-body aircraft.

From a Benjamin Graham valuation perspective, this entry price created a substantial margin of safety. By acquiring 30-year long-life assets at deep down-cycle discounts while global peers permanently retired wide-bodies, United locked in a structural capital cost advantage over competitors who will eventually be forced to re-fleet at peak-cycle prices. This contrarian strategy parallels Samsung's historical dominance in memory chip manufacturing, where market leadership was built by systematically deploying capital into cyclical memory market troughs while competitors halted investment.

This counter-cyclical framework extended to enterprise technology. In the middle of the 2020 downturn, while industry peers slashed capital budgets to preserve cash, United initiated a several-hundred-million-dollar modern IT stack migration. Management bypassed traditional short-term return-on-investment payback hurdles, recognizing that cloud-native database architecture and modern data access layers were necessary preconditions for future enterprise agility and software deployment.

Management's decision-making philosophy explicitly rejects short-term Wall Street pressure regarding cost per available seat mile excluding fuel (CASM-X). Legacy airline management teams often cut technology investments and passenger product features to satisfy quarterly analyst CASM-X targets. United's corporate culture prioritizes long-term asset value creation over short-term unit cost smoothing. Furthermore, Kirby highlights how the global Airbus and Boeing duopoly has quietly ceased investing in clean-sheet airframes (such as a 757 replacement), forcing United to create capital efficiency through alternative investments in engine technology, blended wing-body airframe concepts via its investment in JetZero, and software-driven route optimization.

## 4. Industry Bottlenecks, Cost Dynamics, and Macro Structural Limits

The primary structural bottleneck capping U.S. airline industry capacity is the Federal Air Traffic Control (ATC) system. FAA operations suffer from political facility distribution, maintaining 450 regional control facilities across political districts rather than consolidating controllers into modern, centralized hubs. Furthermore, ATC capital projects are hampered by annual government budget appropriation cycles, which interrupt long-term technology development. System capacity is further strained by private jet traffic; commercial airlines pay 100% of FAA user fees, allowing private jets to utilize constrained airspace infrastructure while paying virtually zero user fee costs.

To mitigate macro ATC bottlenecks through internal operational agility, United developed AI-driven simulation technology to manage flight corridors around pop-up thunderstorms in Denver. By simulating alternative arrival and departure routes during localized weather events, United estimates it can reduce weather-impacted flight delays in Denver by 50% to 75%.

Severe structural cost inflation presents a major headwind for the airline sector. Annual fuel expenditures for United increased by $8 billion this year, a cost shock that exceeds United's historical record annual net profit of $5 billion. Concurrently, hub airport authority fees have inflated by approximately 70% post-COVID, creating an elevated baseline cost structure across major metropolitan terminals.

These macro dynamics have driven a stark divergence in industry profitability. Currently, six out of ten U.S. commercial airlines are operating at a net loss, leaving profitable operations concentrated among four carriers. Competitor hub mismanagement highlights this dispersion: American Airlines is projected to lose approximately $1.5 billion in Chicago alone this year. Following the pandemic, American shifted its strategic focus toward Sun Belt leisure markets (Dallas, Phoenix, Miami) and failed to restore its pre-COVID flight schedules in Chicago. United maintained its schedule density, prompting city airport authorities to reallocate six underutilized gates from American to United, allowing United to double its revenue share over American in that market.

Structural imbalances also persist across long-haul international routes. State-subsidized foreign air carriers operate two-thirds of all international seats entering the United States. However, originating passenger data shows that only 40% of international travel volume originates overseas, meaning U.S. carriers face asymmetric competition against government-backed fleets despite commanding the majority of passenger demand.

## 5. Margin of Safety & Intrinsic Value Assessment

From a Benjamin Graham valuation perspective, United's long-term owner earnings power is supported by fleet scale, expanding international route economics, and significant domestic market share gains. Over the past decade, United expanded its transatlantic network from 23 destinations to 53 planned destinations for next summer, while sustaining only two failed route trials. United has systematically captured market share from Southwest and Alaska across primary hubs, including Denver, San Francisco, and Chicago, driving higher passenger yields and network revenue density.

A fundamental margin of safety is provided by United's tangible asset base. The carrier acquired 30-year wide-body and narrow-body aircraft assets at deep contract discounts during the 2020 market dislocation. Operating long-life capital assets purchased at trough valuations shields the firm's balance sheet against replacement cost inflation and provides structural cost advantages over competitors sourcing fleet capacity at current market rates.

Key operational and intrinsic value risks include:
*   Persistent FAA air traffic control capacity caps in critical hubs (New York, Chicago, San Francisco) that restrict flight growth and limit available seat mile expansion.
*   Certification delays from aircraft manufacturer Boeing, specifically regarding 787 wide-body Starlink integration and delivery schedules, which could stall long-haul international expansion.
*   Macroeconomic shocks impacting corporate travel spending or global leisure demand, alongside unhedged jet fuel price spikes that compress operating margins.
